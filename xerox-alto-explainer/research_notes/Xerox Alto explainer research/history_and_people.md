## 1. Why Xerox founded PARC, where it was, who ran CSL, and how Bob Taylor's ARPA background shaped the lab

### Takeaway
Xerox chief scientist Jacob "Jack" Goldman pushed Xerox to open PARC (opened July 1970, Palo Alto) as a hedge against the copier business slowing down. CEO Peter McColough's phrase for its mission was "the architecture of information". Physicist George Pake ran PARC. The Computer Science Laboratory (CSL) was formally co-run by Jerry Elkind and Bob Taylor, but Taylor, the former head of ARPA's IPTO, set its culture: he hired the pick of the ARPA research community (above all the bankrupt Berkeley Computer Corporation), ran it with a flat structure, and held weekly "Dealer" debates in a beanbag-chair room.

### Cited Findings
**Founding motive and "architecture of information"**
- Lampson, writing as an insider, says Xerox started PARC in 1970 to develop the "architecture of information" and lay the technical base for electronic office products for the 1980s. By then copiers seemed unlikely to be a high-growth business, and electronics looked set to reshape office systems, Xerox's main market. — [Lampson, "Personal Distributed Computing: The Alto and Ethernet Software" (HOPW, 1986/88)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Goldman proposed an open-ended research lab to explore what CEO C. Peter McColough called "the architecture of information"; offices had no computers yet and little was known about the "office of the future" (search summary). — [Boston Globe obituary of Jacob Goldman, Dec 2011](https://bostonglobe.com/metro/obituaries/2011/12/23/jacob-goldman-founded-xerox-lab-that-invented/GGH22ZYFVf64bnMZFNcQnJ/amp.html); [PARC (company), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PARC_(company))
- PARC officially opened at 3180 Porter Drive, Palo Alto, on July 1, 1970 (search summary). — [PARC (company), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PARC_(company)); [Xeroxnostalgia, "PARC celebrates 50 years"](https://xeroxnostalgia.com/2020/05/24/parc-celebrates-50-years/)
- Kay's dating: in July 1970 Xerox, "at the urging of its chief scientist Jack Goldman", decided to set up a long-range research center in Palo Alto. In September 1970 George Pake hired Bob Taylor, then on sabbatical at Utah after leaving ARPA, to start a "Computer Science Laboratory". — [Kay, "The Early History of Smalltalk" (1993)](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/) ([GitHub mirror](https://github.com/worrydream/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk))
- Quotable moment: a PARC engineer later said "the architecture of information" was a great phrase "because nobody knew exactly what it meant", so it could justify almost anything. — [Hiltzik, *Dealers of Lightning*, excerpted in M. R. Cook's book notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Hiltzik lists PARC's enabling conditions. The first was Xerox's copier near-monopoly, "a seemingly limitless cascade of cash". The second was a buyer's market for research talent, because Vietnam-era politics and a recession were squeezing government and corporate research budgets. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Pake worried about corporate "feast-or-famine" research funding. His line (paraphrased): research is steady-state and can't be switched on and off. In a memo dated January 4, 1971 he set a research agenda for a staff of 23. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Scene detail: before the lab was furnished, early staff bought brooms and mops at the nearest K Mart and cleaned the first rented building themselves. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Lampson credits George Pake, first as PARC's manager and later as head of all Xerox research, with leaving researchers "free to pursue their ideas without interference" from the rest of Xerox. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)

**Lab structure and leadership**
- Two groups built the Alto system. CSL was "run by Robert Taylor and Jerome Elkind" and built the hardware and most of the software. The Learning Research Group (LRG), run by Alan Kay inside the Systems Science Laboratory, built Smalltalk. The commercial Star came later from the Systems Development Division (SDD) under David Liddle. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Pake hired Elkind (from BBN) to co-manage CSL. Taylor assumed he would run day-to-day operations, while Elkind assumed Taylor would be his associate, a built-in ambiguity (search summary). — [Jerome I. Elkind, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_I._Elkind)
- CHM describes Taylor as founder and manager of CSL from 1970 through 1983 (search summary). — [CHM Revolution: The PARC Computer Science Laboratory](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/input-output/14/348/1868)
- Hiltzik portrays Elkind as the lab's skeptic. His "Let me play devil's advocate" was dreaded as a kiss of death, and CSL engineers joked that Elkind "knows enough to be dangerous". — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)

**Taylor's ARPA/IPTO background**
- Taylor joined ARPA in 1965 and took over IPTO the following year. He directed the ARPANET effort from 1966 to 1969 (search summary). — [Robert Taylor profile, CHM](https://computerhistory.org/profile/robert-w-taylor/); [IPTO, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_Processing_Techniques_Office)
- Lampson traces the Alto to Licklider's "man-computer symbiosis" as funded by IPTO under Licklider, Sutherland, Taylor and Roberts. Taylor went on to manage the lab that built most of the Alto system, and people from those ARPA projects "formed its core". — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- According to Hiltzik, Taylor at ARPA funded the first full graduate computer science programs at Stanford, Carnegie-Mellon and MIT, and seeded Utah's graphics program under Dave Evans. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Taylor on ARPA's research philosophy (paraphrased): IPTO chased order-of-magnitude impact rather than incremental work, and he saw the Mansfield-era turn to mission-oriented funding as a threat. Kay also names the Mansfield Amendment as a reason PARC looked attractive. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md); [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)

**The Berkeley Computer Corporation "heist"**
- Kay: "Right after New Years 1971", Taylor brought most of the struggling Berkeley Computer Corp. to PARC: Butler Lampson, Chuck Thacker, Peter Deutsch, Jim Mitchell, Dick Shoup, Willie Sue Haugeland and Ed Fiala. Mitchell urged hiring Ed McCreight from CMU. Gary Starkweather was already at PARC, having left Xerox's Rochester (Webster) labs because building a laser printer there was "against the local religion" (Kay's phrase). Many of Engelbart's SRI people followed. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- Hiltzik's version: BCC's last drunken party, on Friday November 13, 1970, used up its petty cash. As it went bankrupt, PARC hired six of its best: Lampson, Thacker, Deutsch, Fiala, Shoup and Mitchell. Hiltzik also tells how Taylor raided Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center at SRI. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- In Hiltzik's account, Thacker was the rare engineer who aimed "for less, not more". He watched BCC's system fall to the "second-system effect", which later shaped the Alto's simplicity. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)

**Dealer meetings, beanbags and culture**
- Taylor held weekly "Dealer" meetings in CSL's informal "beanbag" conference room. Staff presented ideas and got frank, sometimes brutal, feedback (search summary of CHM caption). — [CHM Revolution: The PARC Computer Science Laboratory](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/input-output/14/348/1868); [CHM Revolution: Xerox PARC](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/input-output/14/348)
- The name comes from Edward O. Thorp's blackjack book *Beat the Dealer*. One researcher (the "dealer") defended an idea against everyone at the table, the same format as the adversarial ARPA contractor meetings. The dealer set the rules. Taylor wanted someone to be able to "set rules as czar": no interruptions, or only right-handers may argue. Topics ranged widely, and once included how to take apart and lubricate a bicycle. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Unsound ideas at Dealer were likely to be met with an explosive "Bullshit!" from Thacker (Hiltzik). Taylor distinguished "Class One" disagreements, where neither side can state the other's view, from productive "Class Two" disagreements, where both can. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Informal recruitment of colleagues onto a project was called "Tom Sawyering". Taylor hosted weekend touch football, with himself at quarterback, and games of *Diplomacy*. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Lampson's organizational lessons: build systems 5–10 years ahead of commercial feasibility, designers should use their own systems, and "there should not be a grand plan". He opens that section with Conway's line, "Systems resemble the organizations that produce them." — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Kay: Stewart Brand's 1972 *Rolling Stone* article on PARC and the hacker scene caused "a major furor" at Xerox HQ in Stamford, Connecticut. Xerox then made staff wear badges and restricted publications, which hurt LRG, whom the other computer scientists called the "lunatic fringe". — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)

### Inferences
- Two forces shaped PARC. Xerox's stated goal was the "office of the future". Taylor's personal agenda, which Pake accepted "without devoting much thought to what those terms implied" (Hiltzik), was interactive, networked computing. That gap explains both how much freedom CSL had and why its results later sat poorly with Xerox's product divisions.
- Taylor brought ARPA's model into a corporate lab: hire the best people, leave them alone, and let peer debate (Dealer) do the managing. This fits a video framing of "an ARPA lab with a corporate checkbook."

### Gaps
- I could not read the CHM Revolution PARC/CSL pages or Taylor's CHM oral history directly ([PDF link](http://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/text/Oral_History/Taylor_Robert/102702015.05.01.acc.pdf); the host was blocked). The beanbag and Dealer details rest on search summaries plus Hiltzik.
- I found no primary text of McColough's 1969/1970 "architecture of information" speech (date, venue). It is often attributed to a speech to the New York Society of Security Analysts in 1969; I could not confirm this.
- I found no primary source for the "paperless office fear" framing, i.e. that Xerox feared the paperless office would kill copiers. Lampson's text supports only the softer version: copiers would stop growing, and electronics would transform the office.
- The claim that Taylor was formally Elkind's "associate manager" because he had no PhD appears only in secondary summaries. I could not verify it from a primary source.

## 2. Precursors and influences: Engelbart/NLS and the mouse, Kay's Dynabook and "Interim Dynabook", Lampson's "Why Alto" memo, and MAXC

### Takeaway
The Alto came out of three threads. The first was Engelbart's NLS (publicly demoed December 9, 1968), which set the vision of an interactive, mouse-driven, document-centric system. The second was Alan Kay's FLEX/Dynabook idea of a personal, dynamic medium, which gave the machine its "Interim Dynabook" identity. The third was CSL's time-sharing experience. Xerox would not buy CSL a PDP-10, so CSL built its own clone, MAXC, and that project was the team's hardware boot camp. Lampson's memo of December 19, 1972 ("Why Alto") made the case to management for building 10–30 Altos.

### Cited Findings
**Engelbart, NLS and the mouse**
- On December 9, 1968, at the Fall Joint Computer Conference in San Francisco, Engelbart gave a 90-minute demonstration that debuted the mouse and showed interactive computing, hypertext links, cut/copy/paste editing, shared documents and teleconferencing (search summary). — [Smithsonian Lemelson Center, "The Mother of All Demos"](https://invention.si.edu/invention-stories/mother-all-demos); [SRI, "The computer mouse and interactive computing"](https://www.sri.com/hoi/computer-mouse-and-interactive-computing/)
- Lampson: NLS was "a prototype of the electronic office" demonstrated at the 1968 FJCC. It was too expensive to be practical then, but "made a profound impression" on many who later built the Alto. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- The mouse was conceived by Engelbart and built by Bill English at SRI in 1964 as a wooden box with two perpendicular wheels. The patent ("X-Y Position Indicator for a Display System", US 3,541,541) was filed June 21, 1967 and issued in 1970 (search summary). — [SRI](https://www.sri.com/hoi/computer-mouse-and-interactive-computing/); [CHM, SRI Mouse](https://computerhistory.org/sri-mouse-mn052024/)
- Kay first saw Engelbart when Engelbart visited Utah in early 1967, carrying his own 16 mm projector. Kay calls him "a prophet of Biblical dimensions" and says he "immediately adopted many of the ideas" for the FLEX machine. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- Bill English moved to PARC and led POLOS (PARC On-Line Office System), an attempt to rebuild Engelbart's NLS on a network of commercial minicomputers. POLOS competed with Kay's ideas for resources. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)

**Kay: FLEX, Dynabook, KiddiKomp/miniCOM, "Interim Dynabook"**
- Lampson names Kay's ARPA-funded Flex machine ("Reactive Engine") as another major influence. Kay wanted a machine that could model any system, was "small, cheap, and easy for nonprofessionals to use", and had a fast, high-quality display. He coined "Dynabook" for this. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Lampson describes the two threads that led to the Alto as "the electronic office and the Dynabook". Both were "ideals to draw us on, not milestones to be achieved". — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Kay's PARC-era designs went through several names: KiddiKomp, then miniCOM, a portable suitcase-sized machine. In spring 1972 his miniCOM proposal was knocked down by Elkind. Kay recalled that PARC had "used too many Green Stamps getting Xerox to fund the time-shared MAXC", so personal machines would confuse Xerox. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md); [Kay 1993 (table of contents: "KiddiKomp, miniCOM, Smalltalk-71")](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- Bill English then taught Kay to write budgets. Kay says he really did ask English, "What's a budget?" Kay's plan shifted to experiments on Data General Novas driving character-generator displays. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- The phrase "interim Dynabook" was Kay's name for a fast, compact machine with a high-resolution display. This was the point where Lampson's, Thacker's and Kay's goals met. Lampson (per Hiltzik): the whole point "was to have one for everybody". — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Framing line: Kay opens his 1993 history by calling the 5-pound notebook on his lap "1992's 'Interim Dynabook'", which sold for under $700. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)

**MAXC: the PDP-10 clone**
- Xerox had bought Max Palevsky's Scientific Data Systems (SDS) in 1969 and wanted PARC to use an SDS/Xerox Sigma machine instead of the DEC PDP-10 the ARPA community used for TENEX. CSL was not allowed to buy a PDP-10. Its answer was to build MAXC, a microcoded PDP-10-compatible time-sharing machine running TENEX, named with a nod to Max Palevsky (search summary). — [Maxc, Computer History Wiki (gunkies.org)](https://gunkies.org/wiki/Maxc); [Xerox PARC source code archive, CHM](https://xeroxparcarchive.computerhistory.org/Xerox_PARC_source_code.html)
- Reasoning quoted in the summary: a good OS for a Sigma 7 would take three years, while "we could build an entire PDP-10 in just one year" (search summary; original speaker not confirmed). — [Maxc, Computer History Wiki](https://gunkies.org/wiki/Maxc)
- Hiltzik's scene: some staff talked of quitting. Kay urged, "Let's not chuck our badges in just yet". The insight was that they needed software (TENEX), not a particular vendor's box, so they could build a compatible machine. Estimate: about one year and under $1 million. Lampson: fighting Xerox for a PDP-10 "would cost us so many brownie points". — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Work on the clone began in February (1971 in Hiltzik's chronology). Thacker was project manager and designed the internal logic. Lampson, an OS designer, was assigned the CPU design ("It was fun to see how easy it was"). Ed McCreight's disk controller borrowed idle CPU cycles instead of carrying its own processor; in his words, he could "kidnap the processor". — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Even after the Alto existed, MAXC outperformed it on every measure and stayed PARC's machine for heavy computation for a long time. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)

**Lampson's "Why Alto" memo**
- Xerox inter-office memo, dated December 19, 1972, addressed to CSL. It argues for building "a substantial number (10-30)" of Altos, "designed by Chuck Thacker and others". The original motivation was Alan Kay's need for about 15–20 "interim Dynabooks" for education research. Estimated cost was about $10.5K each, or $9.7K with a 2.5 MB disk (search summaries of the memo). — [Lampson, "Why Alto" (web version)](https://bwlampson.site/38a-WhyAlto/WebPage.html); [DigiBarn: Why Alto?](https://www.digibarn.com/friends/butler-lampson/); [Microsoft Research listing](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/why-alto/)
- Kay's context for the memo: "Executive 'X'" had been away on a corporate "task force". He came back around Christmas 1972, found out about the interim Dynabook, "got even more angry and tried to kill it". Butler then wrote "a masterful defense of the machine" to hold him off. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)

### Inferences
- The "masterful defense" Kay describes lines up with the December 19, 1972 date of "Why Alto" (just before Christmas, about four weeks into the build). The memo is almost certainly that defense. Kay never names the document or "Executive X". Hiltzik's portrait of Elkind as the lab's skeptic makes Elkind the likely "X", but I did not find a source in this session that names him explicitly.
- MAXC is the origin story behind the origin story. Being refused a PDP-10 pushed CSL to design hardware, and it trained Thacker, Lampson and McCreight as a hardware team. McCreight's cycle-stealing disk controller foreshadows the Alto's central idea, in which one microcoded processor also does the I/O work.
- Scene idea: Kay's miniCOM was rejected in spring 1972 because MAXC had used up political capital. Six months later Thacker and Lampson turned up in his office offering to build a version of it anyway.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full "Why Alto" memo (bwlampson.site and digibarn were blocked). The $10.5K/$9.7K figures and the 15–20 interim Dynabooks come from search summaries. Worth verifying against the PDF: https://bwlampson.site/38a-WhyAlto/Acrobat.pdf
- MAXC's first operational date is not established here. Hiltzik gives February (1971) as the start of work but I did not capture a completion date.
- Mouse patent issue date: one summary said "January 17, 1970"; many references give November 1970 for US 3,541,541. Unresolved here, so the writer should check the patent record.
- I did not find how the Alto's three-button mouse related to SRI's design (who redesigned it at PARC, and when the ball mouse arrived).

## 3. The build: Thacker's bet, the three-month sprint, McCreight's role, the Cookie Monster image, first boot, and who wrote what

### Takeaway
According to Kay, Lampson and Thacker approached him in September 1972 asking "Do you have any money?", and Thacker had a bet with a Xerox executive that he could build a whole machine in three months. Thacker started November 22, 1972. With two technicians he built everything except the disk interface, which Ed McCreight designed. In early April 1973, "just a little over three months" in, the first Alto (called "Bilbo") ran, and its first bitmap image was Cookie Monster, drawn by Kay. Dan Ingalls soon ported Smalltalk to it. Later "first released March 1, 1973" claims come from weak sources and conflict with the participants' accounts.

### Cited Findings
**The pitch and the bet**
- Kay: in September 1972, "two bets happened". Butler and Chuck came over and asked, "Do you have any money?" Kay had "about $230K for NOVAs and CGs [character generators]". They asked, "How would you like us to build your little machine for you?" — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- Lampson summed up the three agendas: he wanted "a '$500 PDP-10'", Chuck wanted "a '10 times faster NOVA'", and Kay wanted "a 'kiddicomp'". — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/); consistent with [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md) (Lampson: a suitcase-sized MAXC with about $500 in parts; Thacker: a Nova 800 at ten times the speed)
- Kay: Butler explained that they wanted to do it anyway. Executive "X" was away for a few months on a task force, so they could "Sneak it in". And Chuck "had a bet with Bill Vitic that he could do a whole machine in just 3 months." — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- IEEE Spectrum's version: a Xerox executive angered Thacker by insisting a major hardware system would take 18 months. Thacker said three, a bet followed, and it "took a little longer than three months, but not much" (search summary). — [IEEE Spectrum, "Xerox PARC's Engineers on How They Invented the Future"](https://spectrum.ieee.org/xerox-parc)
- The second of Kay's two bets was Smalltalk. Ted Kaehler and Dan Ingalls challenged Kay's claim that the "most powerful language in the world" could fit on "a page of code", with "Put up or shut up." Kay then came to PARC at 4 a.m. for two weeks and wrote it. This produced Smalltalk-72, and Ingalls implemented it. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)

**The build**
- "Chuck had started his 'bet' on November 22, 1972." He and two technicians did the whole machine except the disk interface, done by Ed McCreight. Specs per Kay: about 500,000-pixel (606×808) bitmap display, microcode instruction rate about 6 MIPS, 128K of memory, and the whole machine apart from memory in 160 MSI chips on two cards. Kay: "It was beautiful." — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- Kay: the Alto's architecture ran several microcode "tasks" as coroutines. Thacker said he got the idea from a Kay lecture on coroutines, while Kay credits Wes Clark's TX-2 (the Sketchpad machine) as the first to use it. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- Hiltzik: the display matched an 8½×11-inch sheet of paper in size and orientation. The processor ran at under 6 MHz, and driving the display slowed it by about a factor of three. Thacker's tight deadline stripped out anything superfluous, giving the design its sleekness. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Lampson: the Alto could present a single 8.5"×11" black-on-white page and let the user point into it. The team "judged most important" the machine's ability to show images and the user's ability to point at them. Its "address space and memory size limitations were its most serious deficiency" (128 KBytes). — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)

**First light: Cookie Monster**
- "In early April, just a little over three months from the start, the first Interim Dynabook, known as 'Bilbo,' greeted the world." Within minutes they had the first bitmap picture on screen: "the Muppets' Cookie Monster that I had sketched on our painting system." — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- "Soon Dan had bootstrapped Smalltalk across". For many months Smalltalk was the only software on the machine. Lampson's verdict on Smalltalk-72's speed was "majestic" (Kay). — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- A popular article describes the Cookie Monster image as clutching the letter "C" and a cookie (secondary, stylized account). — [CLOT Magazine, "Why Cookie Monster is the mascot of Big Tech"](https://clotmag.com/oped/why-cookie-monster-is-the-mascot-of-big-tech-and-has-been-since-the-1960s-by-lucy-hunter)
- Coincidence worth noting: Dick Shoup's SuperPaint frame buffer, a separate PARC project and not the Alto, also produced its first stable image in April 1973 (search summary). — [IEEE Spectrum, "Superpainting"](https://spectrum.ieee.org/xerox-parc/computer-paint-history); [Shoup, "SuperPaint: An Early Frame Buffer Graphics System" (IEEE Annals)](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/45/2017/09/Annals_final.pdf)

**Aftermath of first light**
- Kay's $230K paid for 15 of the originally projected 30 machines. "Executive 'X'" then decided the Interim Dynabook was a good idea and wanted all but two for his own lab. Kay had to go "to considerable lengths" to get them back. Kay adds that over the years "some 2000 Interim Dynabooks were actually built." — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- Lampson: Alto system software was begun in the middle of 1973. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)

**Who wrote what (Lampson's attributions, 1986/88)**
- Bravo WYSIWYG editor: designed by Lampson and Charles Simonyi, implemented by Simonyi, Tom Malloy and others. "Probably the most widely used Alto application". Lampson also calls its user interface "clumsy". — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Bravo was operational on September 14, 1974 (search summary). — [Bravo (editor), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bravo_(editor))
- Gypsy: Larry Tesler and Tim Mott reused much of Bravo but refined the UI into modeless editing, with bold and italics shown on screen and an integrated file directory edited with the same commands. Gypsy was finished in early 1975 for Ginn & Co., Xerox's textbook-publishing subsidiary. It had early cut/copy/paste, and double-click to select a word is credited to Mott (search summary). — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf); [Gypsy (software), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gypsy_(software)); [Tesler, "A Personal History of Modeless Text Editing and Cut/Copy-Paste"](http://worrydream.com/refs/Tesler%20-%20A%20Personal%20History%20of%20Modeless%20Text%20Editing%20and%20Cut-Copy-Paste.pdf); [CHM catalog: Tesler demonstration of Gypsy](https://www.computerhistory.org/collections/catalog/102738551)
- BitBlt: Lampson says it was "designed by Dan Ingalls". Kay says Diana Merry "built an early version of a bit field block transfer (bitblt)" for variable-pitch fonts, and that overlapping windows were the first Smalltalk project, tackled with Merry. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf); [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- Illustrators: Markup (bitmap painting, William Newman, the first Alto illustrator, which Lampson says MacPaint is "quite similar to"); Draw (lines and splines, Patrick Baudelaire); SIL (logic diagrams, Chuck Thacker); Fred (spline font editor, Baudelaire). — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Networking: Pup (PARC Universal Packet) was designed by Bob Metcalfe and implemented with David Boggs and Ed Taft. FTP was by Boggs and Chat by Bob Sproull. IFS (Interim File System) was assembled by Boggs and Taft using McCreight's B-tree package. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Mail: Laurel was by Doug Brotz, Roy Levin, Mike Schroeder and Ben Wegbreit. Its UI took "about a man-year of design", and most users never read the manual. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Printing: the first imager, for the XGP, was by Peter Deutsch. The Press format was by William Newman and Bob Sproull. Interpress (1980) was by Sproull and Lampson with John Warnock. The Dover printer engine (384 dpi) was managed by John Ellenby, and the Orbit imaging hardware was by Sproull and Severo Ornstein from a Lampson design. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Languages: the Alto OS was written in BCPL ("C's immediate ancestor"). The Swat debugger was by Jim Morris. Mesa was begun in 1971 by Jim Mitchell, Chuck Geschke and Ed Satterthwaite and ported to the Alto in 1975. Alto Lisp was by Peter Deutsch and Willie-Sue Haugeland but proved "too slow to be useful". Smalltalk-72 and -76 ran on the Alto. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Laser printing: Gary Starkweather moved to PARC in 1971 and built SLOT from a modified Xerox 7000 copier. With Lampson and Ron Rider he added the Research Character Generator, giving EARS (Ethernet, Alto, Research character generator, Scanned laser output terminal). EARS became the basis of the Xerox 9700 (search summary). — [Laser printing, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_printing); [Hiltzik via Cook notes (RCG by Lampson and Rider)](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Ethernet: Metcalfe's memo of May 22, 1973 named "The Ether Network" (described as 12 pages). The first experimental Ethernet ran November 11, 1973 at 2.94 Mbit/s, half the Alto's clock rate, and was built with David Boggs (search summary). — [IEEE Milestone: Ethernet LAN 1973–1985, ETHW](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Ethernet_Local_Area_Network_(LAN),_1973-1985); [UT Austin news on Metcalfe's Turing Award](https://news.utexas.edu/2023/03/22/bob-metcalfe-receives-computer-industrys-highest-honor-for-ethernet-creation/)

### Inferences
- The best-supported build window is November 22, 1972 to early April 1973, about four and a half months. Kay's "just a little over three months" probably counts working time or rounds down; IEEE says it took "a little longer than three months, but not much". A fair video line is that the bet was three months and it took about four.
- The "March 1, 1973" first-release date found on LinkedIn and a blog ([LinkedIn/Grogan](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/xerox-alto-first-released-march-1-1973-mark-f-grogan); [wemakers.dev](https://wemakers.dev/posts/the-first-pc-was-designed-and-built-in-three-months/)) has no primary backing and conflicts with Kay's first-hand "early April". Treat it as an error.
- Division of credit: Thacker built the hardware and Lampson did the conceptual and software architecture, with Lampson's memo selling it to management. McCreight did the disk interface. Kay supplied the vision, the budget and the first user community. Ingalls brought up the first software (Smalltalk).

### Gaps
- Bet counterparty's name: Kay's HOPL text (worrydream transcription) says "Bill Vitic". The name "Bill Vitaliano" is widely repeated from Hiltzik, but I could not see Hiltzik's exact sentence (it is absent from the excerpt notes), nor Vitaliano's title. The stake ("a case of wine") appears only on a low-quality blog ([wemakers.dev](https://wemakers.dev/posts/the-first-pc-was-designed-and-built-in-three-months/)), so it is unverified.
- Thacker's own hardware paper ("Personal Distributed Computing: The Alto and Ethernet Hardware", HOPW 1986; [PDF on UW course site](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/csep590a/06au/readings/Thacker.Alto.Hardware.pdf)) was blocked. I could not confirm his dates, his account of the bet, the names of the "two technicians" (sometimes given as Larry Clark and Mike Overton; not verified), or the exact first-boot day.
- "Our painting system", on which Kay sketched Cookie Monster, is not identified in the text. It was probably LRG's Nova-based painting program, not Shoup's SuperPaint, but this is unconfirmed.
- The ACM/HOPL version of Kay's text may differ in wording from the worrydream HTML; I read only the HTML.

## 4. Numbers: how many Altos were built, cost (then and now), the Alto II, university donations, and White House/Congress deployments

### Takeaway
The best-supported total is "about 2,000" Altos. Kay (a participant) says "some 2000", and Hiltzik says "nearly two thousand" against the 30 originally planned. Commonly cited splits are about 120 Alto Is and about 2,000 Alto IIs, or about 1,000 in use at Xerox plus about 500 at universities. Cost: the "Why Alto" memo estimated about $10.5K per machine (1972 dollars), and Thacker said the first cost Xerox about $12K. Lampson says a commercial version in 1974 would have cost about $40K. The frequently seen "$32,000 introductory price" has no primary source I could find. Xerox donated about 50 Altos, plus IFS file servers and Dover printers, to Stanford, CMU, MIT and Rochester (1978 or 1979; sources conflict). By early 1978 Altos were in field tests at the White House, the U.S. House of Representatives, Atlantic Richfield and a Xerox sales office.

### Cited Findings
**How many**
- Kay: $230K bought 15 of the original 30 planned. "Over the years some 2000 Interim Dynabooks were actually built." — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- Hiltzik: the display impressed people so much that the modest plan expanded. "In the end Xerox would build not thirty Altos, but nearly two thousand." — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Commonly cited split: Alto I about 120 units, Alto II about 2,000 units. Roughly 1,000 were in use at Xerox and about 500 at universities (search summaries of Wikipedia and IEEE). — [Xerox Alto, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/xerox-parc)
- Ken Shirriff: Xerox built about 2,000 Altos for use at Xerox, universities and research labs, and the Alto was never sold as a product. The Y Combinator machine he restored is an Alto II XM (extended memory, 512 KB on 4 boards using 16-kbit chips) (search summary). — [Shirriff, "Y Combinator's Xerox Alto: restoring the legendary 1970s GUI computer" (righto.com, 2016)](http://www.righto.com/2016/06/y-combinators-xerox-alto-restoring.html)
- Lampson counts "150 Altos at PARC" (and 50 Dorados), a figure for PARC itself, not the total built. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)

**Cost**
- Why Alto memo (Dec 1972): about $10.5K per Alto, reducible to $9.7K with a 2.5 MB disk (search summary). — [DigiBarn: Why Alto?](https://www.digibarn.com/friends/butler-lampson/); [Lampson, Why Alto](https://bwlampson.site/38a-WhyAlto/WebPage.html)
- CHM: "lead engineer Charles Thacker noted that the first one cost Xerox $12,000", and as a product the price might have been $40,000 (search summary of CHM Revolution). — [CHM Revolution: Xerox Alto](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/input-output/14/347)
- Lampson (1986): "If the Alto had been an article of commerce in 1974, it would have cost $40,000 and would have had few buyers." In 1984 a PC with four times the memory and disk and a display half the size cost about $3,500. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Wikipedia's infobox gives an "introductory price US$32,000 (equivalent to $140,000 in 2025)" (search summary). No primary source was found, and the Alto was never sold, so treat this as unreliable. — [Xerox Alto, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto); contradicted in spirit by [Shirriff](http://www.righto.com/2016/06/y-combinators-xerox-alto-restoring.html) ("never sold as a product")
- Hiltzik's "time machine" framing: memory that cost about $10,000 in 1973 would cost about $30 by 1983. PARC's premise was to buy a ten-year head start, and staff called the Alto "a time machine" (Hiltzik's figures, not independently verified). — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Xerox 8010 Star, the commercial descendant: $16,595 at launch in 1981 (search summary). — [Xerox Star, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Star)

**University donations**
- Xerox donated 50 Altos to Stanford, Carnegie Mellon, MIT and the University of Rochester, with IFS file servers and Dover laser printers (search summary). Year conflict: most summaries say 1978, while one says "late 1979" for the donation of Altos, a laser printer and a file server on Ethernet (probably the Xerox University Grant program). — [History-Computer, Xerox Alto guide](https://history-computer.com/products/xerox-alto-guide/); [Stanford University Network, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University_Network); [SUN workstation, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SUN_workstation)

**White House and Congress**
- "By the beginning of 1978, Altos were being used in four test sites: the White House, the United States House of Representatives, the Atlantic Richfield Company, and the operations of Xerox's copier sales division in Santa Clara, California" (search summary). — [History-Computer, Xerox Alto guide](https://history-computer.com/xerox-alto-guide/)
- CHM has a video clip titled "Chuck Thacker: Xerox Altos in the White House", a first-person source the writer can quote or license (content not viewable here). — [CHM Revolution: Chuck Thacker, Xerox Altos in the White House](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/input-output/14/347/2296)
- A weaker claim says the Carter-era Alto was in or near the Oval Office and that Reagan had it removed (search summary of a low-quality page). Treat as unverified. — [History-Computer](https://history-computer.com/xerox-alto-guide/)

### Inferences
- Rough inflation conversions (my own, using approximate US CPI-U annual averages: 1972 ≈ 41.8, 1973 ≈ 44.4, 1974 ≈ 49.3, 1979 ≈ 72.6, 1981 ≈ 90.9, 2025 ≈ 322; confirm with the BLS CPI calculator before airing):
  - Why Alto estimate, $10.5K (1972) ≈ $80K today.
  - Thacker's first Alto, $12K (1973) ≈ $85–90K.
  - Lampson's hypothetical commercial price, $40K (1974) ≈ $260K.
  - The dubious $32K, if 1979 dollars ≈ $140K (matching Wikipedia's stated conversion).
  - Kay's $230K budget (1972) ≈ $1.75M.
  - MAXC's "under $1 million" (1971) ≈ under $8M.
  - Star's $16,595 (1981) ≈ $59K.
- The "1,500" figure in the brief most likely comes from adding "about 1,000 at Xerox" and "about 500 at universities" (machines in use at one time). The "about 2,000" figure counts total production. Both can be true. For narration, "about 2,000 built" is best supported, since two independent sources (a participant and a historian) agree.
- For narration, the cleanest cost line is: "about $12,000 to build in 1973, well over $80,000 today, and PARC's own estimate was that a product version would have sold for about $40,000."

### Gaps
- No primary source found for the Alto II's introduction date or exact production numbers. Lampson's chronology table shows "Alto 2" in the mid-1970s, but the PDF text extraction scrambled the table columns.
- The White House/House of Representatives field-test details come from a secondary summary. The CHM Thacker clip should be the authoritative source but could not be viewed. There is no confirmation of the Oval Office or Reagan claim.
- Exact year and full recipient list of the university grant (1978 vs. 1979) remain unresolved.

## 5. Key people: what each contributed to the Alto and their later careers

### Takeaway
The Alto was a team artifact. Thacker (hardware, Turing 2009), Lampson (architecture, software and the case for building it, Turing 1992), Kay (vision, budget and Smalltalk, Turing 2003), Metcalfe and Boggs (Ethernet; Metcalfe Turing 2022), and Taylor (the lab and its culture) are the core. Lampson, Taylor and Thacker shared the 1984 ACM Software System Award for the Alto.

### Cited Findings
- **Chuck Thacker**: led MAXC's logic design and project management, and designed and built the Alto (Nov 1972 to Apr 1973) with two technicians. He also wrote SIL, the logic-drawing program. Turing Award 2009, cited for designing Alto workstations that anticipated the most powerful PCs of the late 1980s by about 15 years (search summary). He was later at Microsoft Research. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/); [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf); [A.M. Turing Award by year](https://amturing.acm.org/byyear.cfm); [Microsoft Research, "Chuck Thacker Attains Computing's Peak"](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/chuck-thacker-attains-computings-peak/)
- **Butler Lampson**: co-founded the Berkeley 940 project and BCC. At PARC from 1971 to 1983 he designed or co-designed MAXC, the Alto, Dorado and Dandelion; the Alto OS; the Bravo editor (with Simonyi); and the EARS, Dover and Interpress printing work. He wrote "Why Alto". He later moved to DEC's Systems Research Center (then Microsoft). Ed McCreight's introduction says it would be hard to name any PARC computing development Butler hadn't at least "kibitzed on". Turing Award 1992, for distributed personal computing environments (search summary). — [Lampson HOPW (bio and intro)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf); [A.M. Turing Award by year](https://amturing.acm.org/byyear.cfm)
- **Alan Kay**: conceived the Dynabook and led LRG. He supplied the $230K that bought the first 15 Altos, drew the Cookie Monster, and designed Smalltalk-72. He credits Smalltalk's realization chiefly to Dan Ingalls ("the central figure") and Adele Goldberg. Turing Award 2003. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/); [A.M. Turing Award by year](https://amturing.acm.org/byyear.cfm)
- **Bob Taylor**: founded and managed CSL (1970–83) and assembled the team. Shared the 1984 ACM Software System Award with Lampson and Thacker "for conceiving and guiding the development of the Xerox Alto System" (search summary). He died in 2017 aged 85. — [CHM profile](https://computerhistory.org/profile/robert-w-taylor/); [CHM search summary on the 1984 award](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/input-output/14/347); [Naughton, "Bob Taylor … is dead at 85"](https://medium.com/@jjn1/bob-taylor-the-man-who-funded-the-arpanet-is-dead-at-85-d93fe234c189)
- **Ed McCreight**: came from CMU at Jim Mitchell's urging. Designed MAXC's cycle-stealing disk controller and the Alto disk interface, and wrote the B-tree package used in IFS. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/); [Hiltzik via Cook](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md); [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- **Bob Metcalfe**: joined CSL in 1972 (Harvard/MIT), wrote the Ethernet memo (May 22, 1973), and designed Pup. Hiltzik calls him acerbic, a man who "never met an ego he couldn't pierce". Turing Award 2022 for the "invention, standardization, and commercialization of Ethernet" (search summary). — [Hiltzik via Cook](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md); [ACM Turing: Metcalfe](https://amturing.acm.org/award_winners/metcalfe_3968158.cfm); [ETHW Ethernet milestone](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Ethernet_Local_Area_Network_(LAN),_1973-1985)
- **David Boggs**: a Stanford doctoral student who co-built the first Ethernet with Metcalfe. He implemented Pup and wrote FTP, WFS (a file server) and, with Ed Taft, IFS. — [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf); [ETHW](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Ethernet_Local_Area_Network_(LAN),_1973-1985)
- **Gary Starkweather**: moved from Xerox Webster to PARC in 1971. He built the SLOT laser printer and then EARS with Lampson and Ron Rider; EARS printed Alto documents sent over Ethernet from 1973 and led to the Xerox 9700 (search summary). — [Laser printing, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_printing); [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- **Charles Simonyi**: co-designed and implemented Bravo with Lampson, and later led Microsoft Word (secondary). Hiltzik quotes him praising Thacker's rare instinct for aiming low. — [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf); [Microsoft Word, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Word); [Hiltzik via Cook](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- **Larry Tesler**: co-built Gypsy (with Tim Mott), the modeless editor with cut/copy/paste. He gave the main Smalltalk demo to Steve Jobs's team in 1979 (Kay). — [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf); [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/); [Tesler memoir PDF](http://worrydream.com/refs/Tesler%20-%20A%20Personal%20History%20of%20Modeless%20Text%20Editing%20and%20Cut-Copy-Paste.pdf)
- **Dan Ingalls**: implemented Smalltalk-72 and ported ("bootstrapped") it to the first Alto. He designed BitBlt (per Lampson) and made "at least 80 major releases" of Smalltalk over ten years. In the 1979 Apple demo he changed scrolling from jumpy to smooth in under a minute at Jobs's request. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/); [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- **Adele Goldberg**: named by Kay, with Ingalls, as the person who "got it out the door" (Smalltalk-80). She supplied figures for Lampson's paper. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/); [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- **Diana Merry**: worked with Kay on the first overlapping windows in Smalltalk and built an early bitblt for variable-pitch fonts. With Ingalls she made most of the improvements to Smalltalk text editing, and she built multimedia documents. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- **Bob Sproull**: co-designed the Press print format (with Newman) and Interpress (with Lampson and Warnock). Wrote Chat and co-built the Orbit imaging hardware. — [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- **Patrick Baudelaire**: wrote Draw (the spline illustrator) and Fred (the font spline editor), and co-built a Press imager and an image-processing package with Sproull. — [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- **Dick Shoup**: came from BCC. He built SuperPaint, a separate frame-buffer paint system (conceived late 1972, first stable image April 1973), and won an Emmy (1983) and a shared Academy Award (1998) (search summary). SuperPaint was not part of the Alto. — [IEEE Spectrum, "Superpainting"](https://spectrum.ieee.org/xerox-parc/computer-paint-history); [Shoup, IEEE Annals paper](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/45/2017/09/Annals_final.pdf)
- **Children as users** (good scene material): 12-year-old Marian Goldeen wrote a full painting system in Smalltalk. 12-year-old Susan Hamet wrote an OOP illustration system Kay likens to the later MacDraw. 15-year-old Bruce Horn wrote a music score system; as a teenager he later wrote much of the Smalltalk microcode used in the Apple demo. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)

### Inferences
- For a software-literate audience, the strongest "who wrote what" hooks are these. BCPL (C's ancestor) ran the OS. Bravo, via Simonyi, led to Word. Gypsy gave us cut/copy/paste and double-click. Ingalls' BitBlt is the graphics primitive behind windows and fonts. Pup and Ethernet led on to modern networking.

### Gaps
- I could not verify from primary sources in this session: Metcalfe founding 3Com (1979), Goldberg at ParcPlace, Tesler's later roles at Apple, Starkweather's later roles, Sproull at Sun Labs, Taylor's move to DEC SRC (1984), Thacker's death date (2017). They are well known but unsourced here.
- Diana Merry's full name history and later career, and whether she is credited as co-inventor of BitBlt, are not verified beyond Kay's text. Lampson's attribution to Ingalls and Kay's attribution of an early version to Merry should both be mentioned.

## 6. Myths to verify or debunk

### Takeaway
- "First personal computer": depends on definitions. The Kenbak-1 (1971), a $750 commercial machine, won the 1986 Computer Museum "first PC" contest, but the Alto was arguably the first workstation built for personal use with a bitmapped GUI, mouse and network.
- "Never sold": true as a commercial product, though about 2,000 were built and placed at universities and government test sites.
- "PARC invented the mouse/GUI": false for the mouse (SRI, 1960s) and only partly true for the GUI. PARC built on NLS and Sketchpad but pioneered overlapping windows, modeless editing and WYSIWYG.
- "Jobs stole the GUI": left to the Apple researcher. Kay's first-hand account is that Jobs wanted to buy or get the technology and Xerox would neither sell it nor fund it.
- "Fumbling the future": the 1988 book's thesis. It is partly fair, but it ignores the laser printer, which Xerox did commercialize.

### Cited Findings
- **First PC**: the Computer Museum (Boston) held a 1986 contest on "What was the first PC?" and chose John Blankenbaker's Kenbak-1 (1971, 256 bytes, $750, about 40 sold). The 1973 Micral is cited as the first microprocessor-based PC (search summary). — [CHM Revolution, "What Was The First PC?"](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/personal-computers/17/297)
- The subtitle of Smith and Alexander's book, "How Xerox Invented, Then Ignored, the First Personal Computer" (1988), is the main source of the "Alto = first PC" and "fumbling" framing. — [Goodreads: *Fumbling the Future*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/638812.Fumbling_the_Future); [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Fumbling_the_Future.html?id=ITFPAAAAMAAJ)
- CHM's own framing is narrower: the Alto "embodied for the first time" many defining features of personal computing, including individual use, bitmapped graphical displays, networking, overlapping windows and icons, WYSIWYG, and email (search summary). — [CHM Revolution: Xerox Alto](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/input-output/14/347)
- Lampson's own term is "personal distributed computing". He stresses the networked-system character (Alto + Ethernet + servers + laser printers), not the standalone box. In 1975, he notes, "it was hard for people to believe that an entire computer is required to meet the needs of one person." — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- **Never sold**: Shirriff says it was "never sold as a product" (search summary). Lampson says it was deliberately built as tomorrow's machine and would have cost $40K as a 1974 product "with few buyers". The Xerox 8010 Star ($16,595, April 1981) was the commercial derivative, developed from 1977 by SDD. — [Shirriff](http://www.righto.com/2016/06/y-combinators-xerox-alto-restoring.html); [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf); [NMAH, Xerox 8010 Star](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1064205); [Xerox Star, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Star)
- **Mouse and GUI origins**: SRI's Engelbart and English built the mouse in 1964 and patented it in 1970. NLS showed windows and panes, hypertext and mouse editing in 1968. Lampson says the "views" idea goes back to Sketchpad. — [SRI](https://www.sri.com/hoi/computer-mouse-and-interactive-computing/); [Lemelson Center](https://invention.si.edu/invention-stories/mother-all-demos); [Lampson HOPW](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- Lampson's table of derived commercial systems lists the Apple Lisa and Macintosh, Microsoft Windows, MacWrite, Microsoft Word, MacPaint and MacDraw, the Xerox 8010, Sun, Apollo and Perq as descended from Alto-system ideas. — [Lampson HOPW paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/1988/01/Personal-distributed-computing-The-Alto-and-Ethernet-software-HOPW-book-version.pdf)
- **Jobs/Apple (note only)**: Kay says that in 1979 Jobs, Jef Raskin and other Apple staff got a demo "more than six years after the ALTO started running". Tesler drove the demo. Afterwards Jobs "tried to get and/or buy the technology from Xerox", which was one of Apple's minority venture investors, "but Xerox would neither part with it" nor fund it internally. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)
- **Fumbling the future**: the book argues Xerox failed to commercialize PARC's inventions, while noting the laser printer was the major exception (search summary). — [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/638812.Fumbling_the_Future); [Max Olson, "Fumbling the Future at Xerox PARC"](https://futureblind.com/p/fumbling-the-future-at-xerox-parc)
- Hiltzik's counter-texture: pressure to reduce PARC's vision to boardroom paper started early (e.g. Xerox's Pendery pressing Kay). Hiltzik also describes the corporate "instinct for self-preservation" meeting PARC. — [Hiltzik via Cook notes](https://github.com/maryrosecook/book-notes/blob/master/dealers-of-lightning-michael-hiltzik.md)
- Kay's own list of Xerox failures: after 1978 the NoteTaker portable was forced onto the wrong Intel CPU and the wrong HP display "because there was not enough corporate will". Xerox then declined to fund a better NoteTaker. — [Kay 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/)

### Inferences
- Defensible narration: "Not the first personal computer, but the first computer built from the start to be used the way we use computers now: one person, a bitmapped screen, a mouse, windows, a network, a laser printer."
- The "fumbling" story is half-true. Xerox did not turn the Alto into a mass product in the 1970s. But PARC was designed to work 5–10 years ahead (Lampson), the Alto as it stood would have been a $40K product with few buyers, and Xerox did ship the Star (1981) and the laser printer (9700, from EARS). A fairer framing is that Xerox commercialized the parts closest to its copier business and was too early and too expensive with the rest.
- "Jobs stole the GUI" is weak on its own terms: Kay says Jobs tried to buy it and Xerox had invested in Apple. Leave the detailed rebuttal to the Apple researcher.

### Gaps
- I did not find a source quantifying laser-printer revenue against PARC's total cost. The oft-repeated claim that the laser printer "paid for PARC many times over" is not verified here.
- I did not verify the terms of Xerox's pre-IPO Apple investment (commonly cited as 100,000 shares for $1M in 1979). Left to the Apple researcher.
- Other candidates for "first PC" (LINC 1962, HP 9100A 1968, Olivetti Programma 101) were not researched in detail.
