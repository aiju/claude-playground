## 1. Steve Jobs's PARC visits (Dec 1979), the pre-IPO stock deal, what Apple already had, and Atkinson's regions

### Takeaway
Apple made **two** visits to PARC in December 1979. They were arranged under a deal that let Xerox buy 100,000 pre-IPO Apple shares for about $1M. The first demo was a cut-down one and the second was a full demo that Adele Goldberg objected to and was ordered to give. Larry Tesler was among the demonstrators. Lisa and Macintosh were already heading toward bitmapped graphical machines before the visits. The visits sped up and redirected that work. They did not start it.

### Cited Findings
**Number of visits and who went**
- There were two visits by Apple groups to PARC in 1979, and the Stanford "Making the Macintosh" project says Jobs was on the **second**. Jef Raskin helped arrange both. He wanted Jobs to see that others valued the kind of work already going on in Raskin's Macintosh group. — [Stanford, "The Xerox PARC Visit" (Making the Macintosh)](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/SUL/sites/mac/parc.html) (search extract)
- Michael Hiltzik's account has two demos as well. The first was a "bowdlerized" version showing mostly things already public, such as the Alto, its mouse and the Bravo editor. The second was a "full-dress" demo normally given only to potential corporate customers. — [Hiltzik column via ArcaMax](https://www.arcamax.com/knowledge/scienceandtech/technews/s-4050526); [Hiltzik via AOL](https://www.aol.com/finance/hiltzik-inside-1979-silicon-valley-100000560.html); [MakeUseOf summary of Hiltzik](https://www.makeuseof.com/xerox-invented-the-future-in-1979-then-handed-it-to-a-24-year-old/)
- Raskin had visited PARC several times before, and he got Bill Atkinson to suggest the visit to Jobs. — [Low End Mac, History of Apple's Lisa](https://lowendmac.com/2005/history-of-apples-lisa/)

**Who demoed, and Goldberg's objection**
- Larry Tesler was one of the PARC demonstrators, and he "had his hand on the mouse" when Jobs saw the demo. — [HandWiki, Larry Tesler](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Biography:Larry_Tesler); [IEEE Spectrum "Of Modes and Men" (search listing)](https://spectrum.ieee.org/computing/software/of-modes-and-men)
- Tesler saw no reason not to show Apple everything. Goldberg still hoped Xerox would bring Smalltalk to market, and she thought disclosing it to a team able to commercialise it would be a serious error. — [Stanford Making the Macintosh](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/SUL/sites/mac/parc.html) (search extract)
- Goldberg at first refused to give Jobs's team a Smalltalk demo, and Xerox management overruled her. In her own account she said Xerox was about to "give away the kitchen sink". She said she would do the demo only if ordered to, so that the responsibility would be management's. — [Wikipedia, Adele Goldberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adele_Goldberg_(computer_scientist)); [CHM Revolution, Adele Goldberg at PARC](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/artifact/348/1863)
- Tesler later said the Apple visitors' questions were "better than any I had heard" in his seven years at Xerox, and that they showed the team understood the implications. — [MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/xerox-invented-the-future-in-1979-then-handed-it-to-a-24-year-old/); [Industry Analysts, "PARC scientist Larry Tesler recalls Jobs' famous Xerox visits"](https://www.industryanalysts.com/x_xerox/)
- Tesler gave an eyewitness account at a Churchill Club panel in November 2011, which Fortune published as raw footage. In it he says Jobs was "hopping around" but kept asking questions. — [Fortune, raw footage of Tesler](https://fortune.com/2014/08/24/raw-footage-larry-tesler-on-steve-jobs-visit-to-xerox-parc)
- In one account Jobs asked Atkinson how long it would take to duplicate what they had seen, and Atkinson answered "About six months." — [MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/xerox-invented-the-future-in-1979-then-handed-it-to-a-24-year-old/) (second-hand; the original source is not identified in the extract)
- Tesler joined Apple in July 1980, partly because of the demo to Jobs. — [HandWiki, Larry Tesler](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Biography:Larry_Tesler)

**What was shown**
- The visitors saw the Alto and the Smalltalk environment: a graphical interface, bitmapped display and WYSIWYG editing. — [Stanford/Making the Macintosh (search extract)](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/SUL/sites/mac/parc.html)
- Smalltalk had pioneered overlapping windows, pop-up menus and paned browsers. Dan Ingalls's BitBLT graphics primitive arrived with Smalltalk-74. — [CHM, "Introducing the Smalltalk Zoo"](https://computerhistory.org/blog/introducing-the-smalltalk-zoo-48-years-of-smalltalk-history-at-chm/)
- **What Jobs later said he missed:** in 1995 interviews Jobs said PARC showed him three things. The first was the GUI, and he was so taken with it that he barely registered the other two. Those were object-oriented programming and a networked system of more than a hundred Altos using email. He also said that "within ten minutes" it was obvious to him that all computers would one day work that way. — [Steve Jobs: The Lost Interview (1995) transcript](https://sameerbajaj.com/jobs/); [PBS, Triumph of the Nerds transcript Part III](https://www.pbs.org/nerds/part3.html)

**The stock deal (the figures conflict)**
- Hiltzik: in April 1979 Jobs offered to sell Xerox 100,000 private shares at **$10.50** each (about $1.05M) in exchange for an invitation to PARC. — [Hiltzik via ArcaMax](https://www.arcamax.com/knowledge/scienceandtech/technews/s-4050526); [MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/xerox-invented-the-future-in-1979-then-handed-it-to-a-24-year-old/)
- Other summaries say an option on 100,000 shares at **$10** a share, a $1M investment made by the venture arm **Xerox Development Corporation**. — [Wikipedia, Apple Lisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Lisa); [raskincenter.org (search extract)](https://raskincenter.org/mac/xerox-parc/)
- Apple's IPO was on 12 December 1980. It priced at $22 and closed its first day at $29, which made Xerox's stake worth several times what it had paid. — [Hiltzik via AOL](https://www.aol.com/finance/hiltzik-inside-1979-silicon-valley-100000560.html)
- Walter Isaacson writes that by the IPO, Xerox's $1M of shares were worth **$17.6M**. — [Quotefancy quoting Isaacson's *Steve Jobs*](https://quotefancy.com/quote/1956940/Walter-Isaacson-By-the-time-Apple-went-public-a-year-later-Xerox-s-1-million-worth-of) (aggregator; check against the book)

**What Apple already had before the visit**
- Macintosh project documents in *The Book of Macintosh* from **fall 1979**, months before the visit, already planned a friendly interface, multiple fonts (so a bitmapped screen), graphics and a graphical input device. The Lisa was also moving toward a bitmapped screen, pushed by Raskin and Atkinson. — [Mac-History.net, "Did Steve Jobs steal everything from Xerox PARC?"](https://mac-history.net/apple-and-xerox-parc/) (search extract)
- In late 1979 the Lisa team had not yet chosen a processor. — [Low End Mac](https://lowendmac.com/2005/history-of-apples-lisa/)
- After the visit, Atkinson wrote QuickDraw for the Lisa and worked with Tesler on the Lisa user interface. — [Low End Mac](https://lowendmac.com/2005/history-of-apples-lisa/)

**Atkinson, regions and the overlapping-windows anecdote**
- The well-known story is that Atkinson "remembered" seeing overlapping windows at PARC and then set out to build them. The Alto did have overlapping windows and he probably did see them. But when a window was moved, the uncovered parts of the windows behind it were not redrawn, because only the front window updated. — [The Digital Antiquarian, "Doing Windows, Part 6"](https://www.filfre.net/2018/07/doing-windows-part-6-look-and-feel/) (search extract)
- Atkinson's own framing on folklore.org is that seeing it done built his confidence; knowing it was possible "empowered me to invent a way". — [Folklore.org, "I Still Remember Regions"](https://www.folklore.org/I_Still_Remember_Regions.html) (search extract)
- Atkinson invented regions and Apple patented them separately. Regions let QuickDraw clip to arbitrary shapes, which made fast overlapping-window redraw possible on Lisa and Mac. — [Wikipedia, QuickDraw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QuickDraw)

### Inferences
- The story that works best on screen: Apple arrived with a bitmapped-GUI direction already under way, saw a working and much more advanced example, and left with confidence, specific ideas and, within a year, key people such as Tesler.
- The regions story is the strongest example of Apple going past what it saw. A partial misunderstanding of what PARC's system did seems to have pushed Atkinson to solve a harder problem.
- The $10 vs $10.50 difference is probably rounding. Hiltzik's $10.50 ($1.05M) is the more specific figure, and "about $1 million" is safe for narration.

### Gaps
- The exact dates of the two visits, and whether Jobs attended both, are unsettled. Stanford (via Raskin) puts Jobs on the second only. Other retellings, such as Isaacson's (not verified here), describe Jobs visiting and then returning. I could not fetch Hiltzik's column to confirm the dates.
- Which version of Smalltalk was demoed (Smalltalk-76 or Smalltalk-78 on the Alto/NoteTaker) is not confirmed by any source I could read.
- I could not confirm the full list of Apple attendees (commonly given as Jobs, Atkinson, Raskin, John Couch, Richard Page and others) from a readable source.
- When Xerox sold its Apple shares and for how much is not established here.
- The "about six months" line is second-hand. Its original source (probably Isaacson or Hiltzik) was not seen.

## 2. Xerox Star 8010 (1981), the Alto → Dorado/Dolphin/Dandelion lineage, and ViewPoint

### Takeaway
The Star, launched in April 1981, commercialised PARC's ideas as a **networked office system**, not a personal computer. It cost $16,595 per workstation and roughly $50k–$100k+ for a working installation. It sold only about 25,000 units. The reasons were price, slowness, a closed design and a poor fit with the early-1980s PC market. It survived as the 6085/ViewPoint (1985) and later GlobalView.

### Cited Findings
- The 8010 "Star" Information System was introduced in April 1981 at US$16,595 per workstation (about $59,000 in 2025 dollars). — [Wikipedia, Xerox Star (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Star); [Bard Graduate Center, "The Interface Experience"](https://interface-experience.org/objects/xerox-star-8010-information-system/)
- A basic Star system was about $75,000 at launch, plus about $16,000 for each extra workstation. A full office with several workstations, storage and a laser printer could reach $100,000. A base system meant an 8010 workstation, a dedicated server, a floor-standing laser printer and file, print and distributed-services software. — [The Free Dictionary, "8010 Star"](https://encyclopedia2.thefreedictionary.com/8010+Star); [Wikipedia, Xerox Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Star)
- Sales were about **25,000 units**, usually described as a commercial failure. One common comparison: $50k–$100k for an installation when a secretary earned about $12,000 a year. — [Wikipedia, Xerox Star (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Star); [HandWiki, Xerox Star](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Engineering:Xerox_Star)
- The Star was proprietary and closed: no clones and no third-party software. Xerox later saw this as a problem, and ViewPoint was a more open replacement. — [Wikipedia, Xerox Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Star); [Toastytech GUI gallery, Xerox Star](http://toastytech.com/guis/star.html)
- It was slow on its hardware. The file system was poorly implemented, so saving a large file could take minutes, and a crash could be followed by an hours-long file "scavenging" run. — [Wikipedia, Xerox Star (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Star)
- **UI design team and principles:** "Designing the Star User Interface" by D.C. Smith, E.F. Harslem, C.H. Irby, R.B. Kimball and W.L. Verplank appeared in *Byte* 7(4), April 1982, pp. 242–282. It describes a desktop metaphor whose icons stand for office objects (documents, folders, file drawers) and a small set of design principles applied consistently. — [Smith et al. 1982 PDF (worrydream mirror)](https://worrydream.com/refs/Smith_DC_1982_-_Designing_the_Star_User_Interface.pdf); [ACM DL listing, "The Star user interface: an overview" (NCC 1982)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1500774.1500840)
- The main retrospective by the Star's own designers is Jeff Johnson et al., "The Xerox Star: A Retrospective" (IEEE *Computer*, 1989). It covers where Star came from, what was distinctive about it and how the design changed. — [Johnson et al. 1989 PDF](https://worrydream.com/refs/Johnson_1989_-_The_Xerox_Star,_a_Retrospective.pdf); [Digibarn HTML copy](https://digibarn.com/friends/curbow/star/retrospect/index.html)
- **Hardware lineage:** about 2,000 Altos were built over roughly ten years. The Xerox workstation family that followed included the Dorado, the Dolphin and the Dandelion; the Star 8010 ran on the Dandelion. — [Wikipedia, Xerox Alto (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto); [Digibarn, Alan Freier's Dolphin & Dandelions](https://www.digibarn.com/friends/alanfreier/index.html); [bitsavers, "Alto–Dolphin–Dorado Briefing Blurb" (1981)](https://www.bitsavers.org/pdf/xerox/parc/Exploring_the_Ethernet_with_Mouse_and_Keyboard_May81.pdf)
- **Successor:** the Xerox 6085 "Daybreak", running ViewPoint, came out in 1985 at an introductory US$4,995. It was the direct successor to the 8010, and its software later became GlobalView. Xerox used it widely internally until Sun workstations and PCs replaced it. — [Wikipedia, Xerox Daybreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Daybreak); [Digibarn, Xerox 6085/ViewPoint](https://www.digibarn.com/collections/systems/xerox6085/)
- The Stanford Mac project holds a primary document comparing the Star and the Macintosh. I could not read it. — [Stanford, "Comparing the Xerox Star and Macintosh"](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/SUL/sites/mac/primary/docs/starmac.html)

### Inferences
- The Star shipped nearly two years before the Lisa and about three before the Mac, so Xerox *was* first to market with a commercial desktop-metaphor GUI. Its failure came from pricing and product fit, not from being late.
- The Star was sold as an office **system** (workstations plus servers plus printer) through a copier-style sales force. It was not a personal computer that one department could buy on its own budget, and that probably mattered as much as the sticker price.

### Gaps
- Nothing I could read confirms the ~25,000 figure beyond Wikipedia-derived text. Its original source (possibly Johnson's retrospective or a later interview) was not identified.
- The Star's CPU and memory specifications and the Dandelion's production numbers were not checked.
- Whether "property sheets" and "generic commands" (Move, Copy, Delete, Show Properties) are named as design principles in the Byte article is common knowledge but was not seen in an extract here. Check the PDF.
- No readable source here breaks down the Star team roles (e.g. David Canfield Smith's icons from his Pygmalion thesis, Charles Irby, Bill Verplank's human-factors testing).

## 3. Did Xerox ever sell the Alto? Futures Day (1977), the 860 and the 820

### Takeaway
Xerox **never marketed the Alto as a product**. It built about 2,000 of them, mostly for internal use, universities and a few field trials, including the Carter White House. Executives saw it at "Futures Day" in Boca Raton in November 1977. Instead of an "Alto III", Xerox backed the Dallas division's dedicated word processors (the 850 and then the 860, launched 1979). When it finally entered the PC market in 1981, it chose a conventional CP/M machine, the 820.

### Cited Findings
- About 2,000 Altos were produced. Alto IIs were built in quantity by Xerox's SDS group and shipped mainly inside Xerox. — [Wikipedia, Xerox Alto (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto); [History of Computer Communications, "Personal Distributed Computing – Xerox PARC"](https://historyofcomputercommunications.info/section/2.26/Personal-Distributed-Computing-Xerox-PARC-1980/)
- By early 1978 Altos were in use at four **test sites**: the White House, the U.S. House of Representatives, Atlantic Richfield (ARCO), and Xerox's copier sales operation in Santa Clara. — [Wikipedia, Xerox Alto (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto); [CHM 1978 timeline (search listing)](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/1978/)
- In 1979 Xerox donated Altos to Stanford's Computer Science Department and other universities, networked with Ethernet. — [Wikipedia, Stanford University Network / SUN workstation (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SUN_workstation)
- **The Alto III decision:** in 1976 John Ellenby, who led the Alto II project, proposed an Alto III. An alternative from the Office Systems Division (OSD) in Dallas won instead. — [Wikipedia, John Ellenby (search listing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ellenby); [David Gross, "Xerox PARC and yesterday's office of tomorrow"](https://dgross.ca/blog/xerox-parc)
- OSD head Robert Potter wanted his own machine (the Xerox 850). One account says OSD resisted the Alto III because its bonuses depended on sales targets it could not hit if it had to add a new product line. — [David Gross blog (search extract)](https://dgross.ca/blog/xerox-parc)
- **Xerox 860** (codename "Rodeo"): built by the Office Products Division in Dallas and released 11 December 1979 at US$15,300. It was marketed as a word processor but was really a general-purpose computer, and it is called the first commercially available computer with Ethernet connectivity. — [Wikipedia, Xerox 860 (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_860); [CHM 1981 Xerox 860 brochure](http://s3data.computerhistory.org/brochures/xerox.860.1981.102646130.pdf)
- **Xerox 820** (June 1981): an 8-bit CP/M machine with a Z80A at 2.5 MHz, 64 KB of RAM and an 80×24 text screen. It cost $2,995 with two 5¼-inch drives or $3,795 with two 8-inch drives. — [Wikipedia, Xerox 820 (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_820); [Rhode Island Computer Museum, Xerox 820](https://www.ricomputermuseum.org/collections-gallery/small-systems-at-ricm/xerox-820)
- When Xerox entered the PC market with the 820, it pointedly set aside the Alto design for a conventional CP/M model. — [History of Computer Communications (search extract)](https://historyofcomputercommunications.info/section/2.26/Personal-Distributed-Computing-Xerox-PARC-1980/)
- **Futures Day:** held in Boca Raton, Florida, on **10 November 1977** for 300-plus Xerox executives and their wives at an annual meeting. More than 100 PARC staff worked on it and nearly 50 went. They built a Hollywood-made set with four mock cubicles and flew 20 Altos and six laser printers on space leased on two DC-10s. The demos covered networked Altos, laser printing, word processing, email and circuit-design tools. — [IEEE Spectrum, "Behind the scenes at Xerox PARC's Futures Day, 40 years ago"](https://spectrum.ieee.org/behind-the-scenes-at-xerox-parcs-futures-day40-years-ago); [IEEE Spectrum, "Xerox PARC's Engineers on How They Invented the Future"](https://spectrum.ieee.org/xerox-parc)

### Inferences
- "Did Xerox sell the Alto?" In the ordinary sense, no. It was given to universities and placed at test sites, but it was never a catalogued, priced product. The commercial descendant was the Star, four years after Futures Day.
- The 860 shows Xerox did not ignore office computing. It picked a narrower, lower-risk category (word processing), run by a division whose incentives favoured it.

### Gaps
- **The "wives at Futures Day" anecdote** (usually told as the wives trying the machines eagerly while the male executives, who did not type, held back) could not be confirmed from any readable source in this session. It comes from *Fumbling the Future* and later retellings. Treat it as colourful but unverified, and check the IEEE Spectrum article directly.
- A search extract said President Reagan had the Alto removed from the Oval Office. No identifiable reliable source was attached, so do not use it without confirmation.
- Whether any Altos were ever *sold* (rather than lent or donated) to outside customers is unclear. None of the sources I found say so.
- The extracts disagree on whether the division was the Office *Systems* Division or the Office *Products* Division in Dallas. The 860 extract says Office Products Division; the Alto III extract says Office Systems Division.

## 4. Why commercialisation failed: "Fumbling the Future" vs the counterarguments

### Takeaway
The standard account, from Smith & Alexander's *Fumbling the Future* (1988) and Jobs in 1995, is that a copier company run from the East Coast could not see the value of what PARC built. The counter-account, from Hiltzik and others, is that PARC more than paid for itself through the **laser printer** alone. On this view Xerox's failure was narrower: it did not turn *computing* inventions into products, while it did profit handsomely from printing.

### Cited Findings
- *Fumbling the Future: How Xerox Invented, Then Ignored, the First Personal Computer* (Douglas K. Smith & Robert C. Alexander, William Morrow, 1988) argues that Xerox built a large lead in personal computing, then miscalculated and mishandled it, and presents this as a case study in management failure. — [Internet Archive record](https://archive.org/details/fumblingfutureho0000smit); [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/638812.Fumbling_the_Future)
- Jobs (1995) said Xerox's leaders were "copier heads" (PARC staff reportedly called them "toner heads") who "grabbed defeat from the greatest victory" in the industry. He also said Xerox could have been "the IBM of the nineties". — [PBS, Triumph of the Nerds transcript](https://www.pbs.org/nerds/part3.html); [All About Steve Jobs, 1995 Cringely interview](https://allaboutstevejobs.com/videos/misc/triumph_of_the_nerds_interview_1995)
- **Incentives and structure:** Dallas's office division resisted the Alto III partly because of sales-target bonuses, and company politics set its goals against bringing an Alto-class PC to market. — [David Gross blog](https://dgross.ca/blog/xerox-parc)
- **Counterargument, the laser printer:** the Xerox 9700 laser printer (1977) became one of Xerox's best-selling products and "routinely generated more than $1 billion" in annual revenue. — [Xerox Newsroom, 40th anniversary of the 9700](https://www.news.xerox.com/news/40-year-anniversay-of-the-Xerox-9700-and-its-innovation)
- Gary Starkweather moved to PARC in 1971 and adapted a Xerox 7000 copier into the SLOT laser printer. The laser printer earned Xerox billions and is described as PARC's most profitable product. — [National Inventors Hall of Fame, Starkweather](https://www.invent.org/inductees/gary-k-starkweather); [Wikipedia, Gary Starkweather (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Starkweather)
- **Hiltzik's view:** *Dealers of Lightning* (1999) disputes the received wisdom that Xerox simply "muffed" the PC era. It sets what reached market (the laser printer) against what leaked out (WYSIWYG word processing, Ethernet and more). A review notes that Hiltzik praises Xerox executives for understanding the difference between basic research and product development and for sparing PARC the latter. — [MIT Technology Review, "Unbottled Lightning" (1999 review)](https://www.technologyreview.com/1999/07/01/102180/unbottled-lightning/); [Goodreads, Dealers of Lightning](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/1101290.Dealers_of_Lightning)
- The Stanford PARC-visit page records that Goldberg still hoped Xerox would take Smalltalk to market in 1979. So people *inside* PARC thought commercialisation was still possible. — [Stanford Making the Macintosh (search extract)](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/SUL/sites/mac/parc.html)

### Inferences
- A fair version for the video: Xerox did not fail to commercialise PARC. It commercialised the parts that fit its business model (printing, which sells pages and supplies) and fumbled the parts that did not (the personal computer as a product sold to individuals). Both "fumbled the future" and "PARC paid for itself" can be true together.
- The Star's system pricing and the 820's conventional design suggest the problem was less about blindness and more about Xerox trying to fit PARC's ideas into its existing sales and product-division structure.

### Gaps
- I could not read *Fumbling the Future*'s text on the Stamford/Rochester vs Palo Alto culture clash, "per-copy" metered billing, or specific executives (e.g. Peter McColough, David Kearns, Jack Goldman). These are commonly cited but not verified here.
- No readable source gave a figure for PARC's total cost against laser-printer profits (a true ROI number).
- Hiltzik's exact wording on whether Xerox "fumbled" was only available through review summaries.

## 5. The diaspora: who left and what they built

### Takeaway
Most of the commercial value of PARC's computing ideas reached the market through people who left Xerox between 1979 and 1983. The main ones were Metcalfe (3Com), Simonyi (Microsoft Word), Tesler (Apple Lisa), Warnock and Geschke (Adobe/PostScript), Kay (Atari, then Apple), and Taylor, Lampson and Thacker (DEC SRC). Stanford's Alto donation shaped the SUN workstation.

### Cited Findings
- **Metcalfe → 3Com:** Metcalfe left Xerox and co-founded 3Com in 1979. On 30 September 1980 DEC, Intel and Xerox published the Ethernet "Blue Book" (the DIX standard, 10 Mbit/s, 48-bit addresses), which went on to the IEEE. — [Computer Weekly, "How Ethernet became the world's networking standard"](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/252489944/How-Ethernet-became-the-worlds-networking-standard); [ACM, 2022 Turing Award to Metcalfe](https://awards.acm.org/about/2022-turing); [History of Computer Communications, Metcalfe and 3Com](https://historyofcomputercommunications.info/section/9.9/Robert-Metcalfe-and-the-Founding-of-3Com/)
- **Simonyi → Microsoft:** Simonyi and Butler Lampson built **Bravo**, described as the first WYSIWYG document program (working by 1974). Simonyi stayed at PARC until 1981, when, at Metcalfe's suggestion, he applied directly to Bill Gates. At Microsoft he began "Multi-Tool Word", with former Xerox intern Richard Brodie as lead engineer, and Microsoft announced it in 1983. He later led the Word and Excel teams. — [Wikipedia, Microsoft Word (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Word); [CHM, Charles Simonyi profile](https://computerhistory.org/profile/charles-simonyi/)
- **Tesler → Apple:** joined Apple in July 1980. — [HandWiki, Larry Tesler](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Biography:Larry_Tesler)
- **Warnock & Geschke → Adobe:** at PARC they created the page-description language **Interpress**, which became Xerox's internal standard. Unable to convince Xerox to commercialise it widely, they left and founded Adobe in December 1982. Interpress ideas grew into **PostScript**, which shipped in Apple's LaserWriter in 1985. — [CHM, "PostScript: A Digital Printing Press"](https://computerhistory.org/blog/postscript-a-digital-printing-press/); [EBSCO Research Starters, Charles Geschke](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/charles-geschke)
- **Alan Kay:** Atari chief scientist 1981–1984, then Apple (Advanced Technology Group) as an Apple Fellow from 1984. In 1997 he moved to Disney Imagineering, where he continued work on Squeak Smalltalk. — [Wikipedia, Alan Kay (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Kay); [ACM Turing Award profile, Alan Kay](https://amturing.acm.org/award_winners/kay_3972189.cfm)
- **Taylor, Lampson, Thacker → DEC SRC:** Bob Taylor left PARC in **1983** after an acrimonious resignation and founded DEC's Systems Research Center. Lampson and Thacker followed him. — [The Register, Bob Taylor obituary](https://theregister.com/2017/04/17/obituary_bob_taylor); [Wikipedia, Butler Lampson (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butler_Lampson); [Wikipedia, DEC Systems Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DEC_Systems_Research_Center)
- **Bechtolsheim → Sun:** as a Stanford graduate student Bechtolsheim designed the SUN workstation (1980). It was inspired by the Alto but used cheaper modular parts. He was a "no fee consultant" at Xerox with free access to PARC's research. Sun Microsystems was co-founded in 1982. Its early Ethernet interface was soon replaced by a commercial 3Com board. — [Wikipedia, SUN workstation (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SUN_workstation); [Wikipedia, Andy Bechtolsheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andy_Bechtolsheim)

### Inferences
- A useful framing for the video is that PARC's ideas spread mainly through *people*, not licences. Metcalfe's suggestion that Simonyi go to Microsoft is a clear example of the network passing talent around.
- For Ethernet, 3Com and the DIX standard show Xerox deliberately opening a PARC technology. That undercuts the idea that Xerox only hoarded or ignored its inventions.

### Gaps
- The brief said "Taylor → DEC SRC 1984". Sources here say Taylor left PARC in 1983. SRC may have formally opened in 1984, but that was not confirmed.
- Other departures (e.g. the Star/Smalltalk people who went to Apple, Microsoft or ParcPlace, and Bob Belleville to Apple as Mac engineering lead) were not researched here.
- No source here confirms that Windows 1.0 (1985) had specific PARC-alumni authors beyond the Simonyi link to Microsoft generally.

## 6. Xerox v. Apple (1989–1990) and Apple v. Microsoft (1988–1994)

### Takeaway
Xerox sued Apple in December 1989, **after** Apple had sued Microsoft. In March 1990 a federal judge threw out most of the case, mainly because Xerox had waited too long. Apple's own case against Microsoft also failed, largely because of a 1985 licence Apple had granted for Windows 1.0. In the end neither plaintiff won.

### Cited Findings
- On **14 December 1989** Xerox sued Apple for **$150 million**. It alleged that the Lisa and Macintosh copied protected elements of the Star (and, in some descriptions, Smalltalk) interface. — [Santa Clara High Tech Law Journal, "Xerox Finally Wakes Up, But Is It Too Late?"](https://digitalcommons.law.scu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1099&context=chtlj); [UPI, 23 Mar 1990](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1990/03/23/Judge-throws-out-bulk-of-Xerox-suit-against-Apple/3021638168400/)
- On **23 March 1990** U.S. District Judge **Vaughn Walker** (N.D. Cal.) dismissed most of the suit. He dismissed Xerox's unfair-competition and unjust-enrichment claims and rejected its attempt to invalidate Apple's copyrights. Only one claim survived: a request for a declaratory judgment confirming Xerox's ownership of the 1980 Star copyright. — [UPI Archives](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1990/03/23/Judge-throws-out-bulk-of-Xerox-suit-against-Apple/3021638168400/)
- Citation: *Xerox Corp. v. Apple Computer, Inc.*, No. C-89-4428-VRW, 734 F. Supp. 1542 (N.D. Cal. 1990). — [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/734/1542/1461830/); [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1461830/xerox-corp-v-apple-computer-inc/); [vLex](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/xerox-corp-v-apple-892422113)
- The main reason cited for dismissal was **delay and the statute of limitations**. Xerox had waited too long to sue for copyright infringement and fell back on weaker claims such as unfair competition. — [Santa Clara law journal (search extract)](https://digitalcommons.law.scu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1099&context=chtlj); [Wikipedia, Apple litigation (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_litigation)
- *Disputed/weaker:* a local-newspaper column says Walker also found that Apple copied ideas rather than expression. That reasoning belongs to the idea/expression line in Apple v. Microsoft, so this may mix up the two cases. — [Arlington MN News, "Xerox versus Apple: 'Star' Wars"](https://arlingtonmnnews.com/articles/bits-and-bytes/xerox-versus-apple-star-wars/)
- **Apple v. Microsoft:** after Windows 1.0, Apple and Microsoft signed a **1985 licence** letting Microsoft use and sublicense derivative works of Windows 1.0 in present and future products. Microsoft rejected Apple's draft language limiting it to the look and feel of Windows 1.0. The district court found that more than 90% of the disputed elements were covered by the licence, and ruled for Microsoft in 1992. The **Ninth Circuit affirmed 3–0 on 19 September 1994**, applying a "virtual identity" standard to what remained. — [Wikipedia, Apple Computer, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp. (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Computer,_Inc._v._Microsoft_Corp.); [Justia, 35 F.3d 1435 (9th Cir. 1994)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/35/1435/605245/); [Richmond JOLT, "Apple v. Microsoft: Virtual Identity in the GUI Wars"](http://jolt.richmond.edu/jolt-archive/v1i1/myers.html)
- Hiltzik sums up that the demo led to two federal lawsuits (Apple v. Microsoft and Xerox v. Apple) and that "both plaintiffs lost". — [Hiltzik via AOL](https://www.aol.com/finance/hiltzik-inside-1979-silicon-valley-100000560.html)
- **Context, "a rich neighbor named Xerox":** Microsoft showed Windows at Comdex in 1983 and Jobs confronted Gates. Andy Hertzfeld reports that Gates replied that they had both had a rich neighbour named Xerox, and that Gates had broken in to steal the TV only to find Jobs had already taken it. Hertzfeld also says Jobs had made Microsoft promise not to ship mouse-based software until a year after the Mac shipped. — [Folklore.org, "A Rich Neighbor Named Xerox"](https://www.folklore.org/A_Rich_Neighbor_Named_Xerox.html) (search extract); [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/quote-of-the-day-by-bill-gates-i-broke-into-his-house-to-steal-the-tv-set-and-found-out-that-you-had-already-stolen-it-a-witty-defense-against-a-stunning-accusation)

### Inferences
- The timing is key. Xerox sued only after Apple's 1988 suit against Microsoft made GUI "look and feel" look legally valuable. The court then held Xerox's delay against it, and the case was never tried on whether Apple "stole" anything.
- For narration, avoid saying "the court ruled Apple didn't copy Xerox". The main ruling was procedural (timing), with the substantive claims left mostly unresolved.

### Gaps
- NYT coverage from December 1989 and March 1990 could not be fetched, so no NYT quotes or reported reactions are included.
- What happened to the surviving declaratory-judgment claim after March 1990 (later dismissal, settlement, or dropped) was not found.
- The exact filing date of Apple v. Microsoft (commonly 17 March 1988) was not confirmed from a readable source.

## 7. Broader influence: OOP, WYSIWYG desktop publishing, Ethernet, Berners-Lee/NeXT, Engelbart

### Takeaway
PARC's ideas reached the mainstream through several chains. Printing: Interpress → PostScript → LaserWriter plus PageMaker (1985). Networking: Ethernet → DIX → 3Com/Sun. Programming: Smalltalk → Objective-C → NeXTSTEP. The only well-supported **link to Tim Berners-Lee is indirect**: he built the first web browser on a NeXT in Objective-C with Interface Builder. That toolchain descends from Smalltalk-style OOP, but Berners-Lee's work did not come from PARC.

### Cited Findings
- **Desktop publishing:** Apple announced the LaserWriter on 23 January 1985, the same day Aldus announced PageMaker, which shipped in July 1985. The Mac GUI, PageMaker and the LaserWriter with Adobe PostScript are credited with starting desktop publishing. Aldus founder Paul Brainerd coined the phrase "desktop publishing". PageMaker came to Windows in 1987. — [Wikipedia, LaserWriter (search extract)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaserWriter); [Wikipedia, Aldus PageMaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldus_PageMaker); [History of Information, PageMaker](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=991); [CHM Revolution, Desktop Publishing](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/personal-computers/17/306)
- **Ethernet:** DIX Blue Book on 30 September 1980; Metcalfe's 2022 Turing Award cites invention, standardisation and commercialisation. — [Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/252489944/How-Ethernet-became-the-worlds-networking-standard); [ACM Turing 2022](https://awards.acm.org/about/2022-turing)
- **OOP via Objective-C:** Brad Cox and Tom Love designed Objective-C, which adds Smalltalk-style messaging to C. Cox met Smalltalk through *Byte*'s 1981 special issue. NeXT became a key Objective-C customer for NeXTSTEP, and Objective-C later underpinned Mac OS X and iOS. — [Wikipedia, Objective-C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective-C); [CHM, "The Deep History of Your Apps"](https://computerhistory.org/blog/the-deep-history-of-your-apps-steve-jobs-nextstep-and-early-object-oriented-programming/); [Cox & Naroff et al., HOPL IV paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3386332)
- **Berners-Lee:** he wrote the first browser-editor, *WorldWideWeb*, on a NeXT computer in Objective-C using Interface Builder, and finished it around late 1990 after about two months. The NeXT tools let him do in months what would have taken about a year elsewhere. — [CERN, WorldWideWeb NeXT application history](https://worldwideweb.cern.ch/history/); [Wikipedia, Interface Builder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interface_Builder)
- **Engelbart → PARC:** Engelbart filed the mouse patent ("X-Y Position Indicator for a Display System") on 27 June 1967, granted 17 November 1970 as US 3,541,541. Bill English built the first prototype in 1964. NLS was shown on 9 December 1968 with shared-document editing in windows and live video. English joined PARC in 1971, bringing mouse technology with him. — [Scientific American, "The Origin of the Computer Mouse"](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/origins-computer-mouse/); [History of Information, Engelbart mouse](https://historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=855)

### Inferences
- Berners-Lee and PARC: the defensible statement is that the web's first browser was built on Jobs's NeXT platform, whose object-oriented toolkit came from Smalltalk-style ideas. There is **no evidence here of a direct PARC → Berners-Lee influence**, and the video should not suggest one.
- NeXT can be seen as Jobs going after the two things he said he missed in 1979 (objects and networking). That is an interpretation, not a documented statement of intent.

### Gaps
- **Engelbart's view vs PARC's** (Engelbart's goal of augmenting skilled teams vs PARC's easy-to-learn personal computer, and his reported dismay that the "novice" approach won) was not verified in any readable source this session. It is commonly discussed, for example in Bardini's *Bootstrapping* and Engelbart's oral histories, but was not checked here.
- The claim that Smalltalk "made OOP mainstream" is contested. C++ (from Simula, not Smalltalk) was arguably more important commercially. I did not source this here.
- Adobe's move from Interpress to PostScript and Apple's role (Jobs's investment and licence for the LaserWriter) are only lightly covered.

## 8. Myths to address explicitly

### Takeaway
Each popular myth has a real core that has been exaggerated. For each one, the video should say what is true and what goes too far.

### Cited Findings
- **Myth: "Jobs stole the GUI from Xerox."**
  - *Against:* Apple *paid* for the visits through a stock deal Xerox agreed to, and Xerox management ordered the full demo over Goldberg's objection. Apple's bitmapped-GUI plans predate the visit. — [Hiltzik via ArcaMax](https://www.arcamax.com/knowledge/scienceandtech/technews/s-4050526); [Wikipedia, Adele Goldberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adele_Goldberg_(computer_scientist)); [Mac-History.net](https://mac-history.net/apple-and-xerox-parc/)
  - *For (partly):* Jobs himself said he saw the GUI and knew all computers would work that way. Xerox's 1989 suit claimed Lisa and Mac copied Star elements. The suit failed on timing, not because the court found no copying. — [Lost Interview transcript](https://sameerbajaj.com/jobs/); [UPI](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1990/03/23/Judge-throws-out-bulk-of-Xerox-suit-against-Apple/3021638168400/)
- **Myth: "Xerox got nothing out of it."**
  - Xerox's roughly $1M Apple stake was worth about $17.6M at the December 1980 IPO, according to Isaacson. More importantly, PARC's laser printer (the 9700 line) routinely earned more than $1B a year. Ethernet became an open DIX standard co-sponsored by Xerox. — [Quotefancy/Isaacson](https://quotefancy.com/quote/1956940/Walter-Isaacson-By-the-time-Apple-went-public-a-year-later-Xerox-s-1-million-worth-of); [Xerox Newsroom](https://www.news.xerox.com/news/40-year-anniversay-of-the-Xerox-9700-and-its-innovation); [Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/252489944/How-Ethernet-became-the-worlds-networking-standard)
- **Myth: "Xerox invented the mouse."**
  - False. Engelbart and English built it at SRI (prototype 1964, patent filed 1967, 1968 demo). English brought it to PARC in 1971. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/origins-computer-mouse/)
  - Gladwell's "Creation Myth" (New Yorker, 16 May 2011) uses the mouse to argue for *implementation over invention*. His story: after the visit, Jobs told designer Dean Hovey (Hovey-Kelley) "You've got to do a mouse". Xerox's mouse cost about $300 and Apple wanted one around $15. — [Cult of Mac on Gladwell](https://cultofmac.com/94194/malcolm-gladwell-takes-on-steve-jobs-and-the-mouse); [Fortune, "How Apple made the mouse"](https://fortune.com/2011/05/09/how-apple-made-the-mouse); [PDF copy of Gladwell article](https://dayonecomptwo.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/11/gladwell-creation-myth.pdf)
  - Gladwell's framing has been criticised for simplifying. — [Forbes, Steve Denning, "Gladwell on Innovation: Truths & Confusions"](https://www.forbes.com/sites/stevedenning/2011/05/23/gladwell-on-innovation-truths-confusions-part-1/) (only the title and existence were seen; the substance of the critique was not read)
- **Myth: "The Alto was the first GUI."**
  - Engelbart's NLS showed windowed, mouse-driven editing in December 1968, before the Alto (1973). The Alto is better described as the first *personal* workstation built around a bitmapped GUI, networking and laser printing. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/origins-computer-mouse/); [History of Information](https://historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=855)
- **Myth: "Apple copied the Alto exactly."**
  - Apple's versions differed in important ways. Atkinson's regions allowed full redraw of uncovered overlapping windows, which the Alto's Smalltalk did not do and which Apple patented. Hovey's mouse was a cheaper, single-button redesign. — [Folklore.org, "I Still Remember Regions"](https://www.folklore.org/I_Still_Remember_Regions.html); [Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2018/07/doing-windows-part-6-look-and-feel/); [Wikipedia, QuickDraw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QuickDraw); [Cult of Mac](https://cultofmac.com/94194/malcolm-gladwell-takes-on-steve-jobs-and-the-mouse)
  - The closer commercial ancestor of the Lisa and Mac desktop is arguably the **Star** (desktop, icons, documents), not the Alto's Smalltalk. That is why Xerox's 1989 suit centred on the Star. — [Smith et al., Byte 1982](https://worrydream.com/refs/Smith_DC_1982_-_Designing_the_Star_User_Interface.pdf); [UPI](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1990/03/23/Judge-throws-out-bulk-of-Xerox-suit-against-Apple/3021638168400/)

### Inferences
- A one-line thesis the sources support: Xerox invented much of the modern workstation, profited from the piece that fit its business, sold the rest to Apple for a peek and a stock stake, and lost the chance to own the PC. Apple's contribution was cheaper, faster implementation, not invention from nothing.
- Gladwell's piece is best used as the popular narrative to push back on. His "implementation over invention" thesis fits the mouse and regions stories well. It underplays that Xerox *did* implement and ship a GUI system, the Star, and failed on price and positioning, not imagination.

### Gaps
- The Gladwell article itself could not be fetched. The mouse numbers come from secondary summaries, and the costs may be build costs or unit costs; check the original.
- Sketchpad (Sutherland, 1963), a common "first GUI" rebuttal, was not verified in any source this session.
- Whether Lisa's pull-down menu bar, double-click and other features were original to Apple or seen at PARC was not researched.
- I found no source quantifying how much of the Lisa/Mac UI existed before December 1979 beyond the fall-1979 Mac documents. Atkinson's pre-visit Lisa UI photos, often cited, were not located.
