## 1. The CPU: a 16-bit microcoded TTL processor

### Takeaway
The Alto's "CPU" is a single microprogrammed 16-bit processor built from TTL, with a four-chip 74181 ALU, a ~170 ns microcycle (every microinstruction takes one cycle), and 32-bit horizontal microinstructions held in a 1K-word PROM plus optional RAM (up to 1K PROM + 3K RAM). The Data General Nova–style instruction set that programs see is just one microcode program (the "emulator") running on it; other emulators (BCPL/Mesa/Smalltalk) could be loaded into microcode RAM.

### Cited Findings
- **Clock / microcycle**: The Alto Hardware Manual says the whole machine is synchronous, with a clock interval of about 170 ns, and every microinstruction executes in one cycle — [Alto Hardware Manual, May 1979 (OCR)](https://ed-thelen.org/RestoreAlto/AltoHWRefPart1-4-ocr.pdf); the Thacker et al. paper, via search excerpt, likewise says all microinstructions take one 170 ns cycle — [Thacker, McCreight, Lampson, Sproull, Boggs, "Alto: A personal computer"](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm).
- ContrAlto uses 170 ns as its scheduler time step, with a code comment that 170 ns is roughly one Alto system clock cycle — [ContrAlto Scheduler.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Scheduler.cs).
- **The 170 ns clock is tied to the display**: a search excerpt says the 170 ns main clock was picked as an integral submultiple (224) of the display line period. (The excerpt came back with the Hardware Manual results, but I could not confirm which document it came from.) — [search result pointing to Alto Hardware Manual](https://ed-thelen.org/RestoreAlto/AltoHWRefPart1-4-ocr.pdf). ContrAlto backs this up independently: its scanline period is 38,080 ns, which is exactly 224 × 170 ns — [ContrAlto DisplayController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Display/DisplayController.cs).
- **ALU**: 16-bit add, subtract and Boolean operations are done by four 74181 4-bit ALU chips — [Ken Shirriff, "Y Combinator's Xerox Alto: restoring the legendary 1970s GUI computer"](http://www.righto.com/2016/06/y-combinators-xerox-alto-restoring.html). Search excerpts from the [74181 Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/74181) also call the Alto one of the first uses of the 74181.
- **Microinstruction format (32 bits)**, as decoded by ContrAlto: RSELECT 5 bits (bits 31–27), ALUF 4 bits, BS (bus source) 3 bits, F1 4 bits, F2 4 bits, LoadT 1 bit, LoadL 1 bit, NEXT 10 bits (next microaddress) — [ContrAlto MicroInstruction.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/MicroInstruction.cs). Every instruction names its successor through the 10-bit NEXT field (so there is no micro-PC incrementer). Branches OR condition bits into NEXT, which you can see in the microcode's paired-label declarations such as `!1,2,MOREB,NOMORE` — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).
- **ALU functions**: the 4-bit ALUF field selects 16 codes, 14 of them defined (BUS, T, BUS OR T, BUS AND T, BUS XOR T, BUS+1, BUS−1, BUS+T, BUS−T, BUS−T−1, BUS+T+1, BUS+SKIP, BUS AND T with a different carry setting, BUS AND NOT T), plus 2 undefined — [ContrAlto MicroInstruction.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/MicroInstruction.cs).
- **Constants**: a constant PROM, addressed by concatenating RSELECT and BS (8 bits), drives the bus when F1=7, F2=7 or BS>4. ContrAlto quotes the Hardware Manual on this — [ContrAlto MicroInstruction.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/MicroInstruction.cs).
- **Registers**: ContrAlto models 32 general R registers, 8 banks of 32 "S" registers, plus the T, L, M and IR registers — [ContrAlto CPU.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/CPU.cs).
- **Microcode store**: the control board holds 1024 microinstructions of 32 bits in eight 1K×4-bit PROM chips — [Shirriff, restoration day 5](http://www.righto.com/2016/09/xerox-alto-restoration-day-5-smoke-and.html). According to the Thacker et al. paper (search excerpt), the latest version has 4K words of microstore: 1K PROM and 3K RAM — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm). ContrAlto emulates microcode RAM "in 1K RAM, 1K RAM/2K ROM, or 3K RAM configurations" — [ContrAlto readme.txt](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/readme.txt). A secondary source says some Alto IIs had a second 1KW PROM and others three 1KW banks of RAM — [Computer History Wiki (gunkies), Xerox Alto](https://gunkies.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto). ContrAlto's bank enum is ROM0, ROM1, RAM0, RAM1, RAM2 — [ContrAlto UCodeMemory.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/UCodeMemory.cs).
- **Emulated instruction set**: the paper describes a microprogrammed processor that runs I/O devices and supports emulators for several instruction sets — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm). The standard ROM microcode's task-0 entry point is `NOVEM`, and the file has a section headed "NOVA EMULATOR". Its macro-op dispatch tables add Alto-only instructions beyond the Nova set: DIR/EIR (interrupts), BLT/BLKS (block transfer/store), SIO (start I/O), JMPR/RDRM/WTRM (jump into / read / write microcode RAM), MUL, DIV, DREAD/DWRITE/DEXCH (double-word), BITBLT, XMLDA/XMSTA (extended-memory load/store) — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).
- ContrAlto can disassemble both microcode and Nova-style instructions, and ships `altocode24.mu` (Alto I), `altoIIcode3.mu` (Alto II) and a Mesa ROM listing — [ContrAlto NovaDisassembler.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/NovaDisassembler.cs), [Disassembly folder](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/tree/master/Contralto/Disassembly).

### Inferences
- 170 ns per cycle works out to about 5.88 MHz, i.e. roughly 5.9 million microinstructions per second shared among every device and the user program. (Computed: 1/170 ns.)
- For software engineers, a useful picture: each microinstruction is a 32-bit "very wide" command that routes one bus source through the ALU into registers and, in the same cycle, picks the address of the next microinstruction. The Nova-like "machine code" is a program interpreted by this micro-machine.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable source for the **total chip count** or the **exact board count** of the CPU or the whole machine. (Shirriff's posts describe a card cage with separate control, ALU, RAM, memory, display, disk and Ethernet boards, but I could not fetch a count.) I also found no figure for how many microinstructions an average Nova instruction takes.
- The claim that the 3K RAM board is what provides the S-register banks is my reading of ContrAlto, not a documented statement.

## 2. The microcode task system: one processor acting as CPU plus all controllers

### Takeaway
The micromachine is time-shared among 16 fixed-priority hardware "tasks". The emulator (user program) is task 0 at the lowest priority; I/O tasks wake up when their device raises a request line. A task switch costs no cycles because each task has only its own micro-PC, and switching just picks the highest-priority requester through a priority encoder when the running microcode executes TASK. This is why the Alto has almost no controller hardware: the display, disk, Ethernet, memory refresh and mouse are all microcode running on the same ALU.

### Cited Findings
- The micromachine is shared by sixteen fixed-priority tasks. The emulator, which interprets the user's program, has the lowest priority; the others run the microcoded parts of I/O controllers and housekeeping — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt).
- Per the Hardware Manual, task switching costs very little and usually happens every few microseconds — [Alto Hardware Manual (OCR)](https://ed-thelen.org/RestoreAlto/AltoHWRefPart1-4-ocr.pdf) (search excerpt).
- **Mechanism**: a task gives up the processor by executing the TASK function. This loads the NTASK register from a priority encoder whose inputs are the 16 wakeup request lines, one per task. A device asks for service by asserting its task's request line — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt). The control board uses two priority-encoder chips for this — [Shirriff, restoration day 5](http://www.righto.com/2016/09/xerox-alto-restoration-day-5-smoke-and.html).
- The switch takes effect **after the next microinstruction**. ContrAlto keeps a `_nextTask` described as the task to switch to after the next microinstruction, and chooses it by scanning tasks from 15 down to 0, so a higher number means a higher priority — [ContrAlto CPU.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/CPU.cs).
- On reset, each task starts at the microaddress equal to its task number (ContrAlto quotes the Hardware Manual's "Initialization" section) — [ContrAlto Task.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/Tasks/Task.cs).
- **Task table (Alto II standard microcode)**. The reset-location declaration in the Xerox microcode is `!17,20,NOVEM,,,,KSEC,,,EREST,MRT,DWT,CURT,DHT,DVT,PART,KWDX,;` — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu). ContrAlto's `TaskType` enum matches it — [ContrAlto CPU.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/CPU.cs):

  | Task (dec / octal) | Name | Role |
  |---|---|---|
  | 0 / 0 | NOVEM – Emulator | Runs the Nova-like user instruction set; lowest priority |
  | 1 / 1 | (Orbit, optional) | Laser-printer (Orbit/Dover) raster hardware in ContrAlto |
  | 3 / 3 | (Trident output, optional) | Trident disk controller option |
  | 4 / 4 | KSEC – Disk sector task | Once per disk sector |
  | 7 / 7 | EREST – Ethernet task | Ethernet packet I/O |
  | 8 / 10 | MRT – Memory refresh task | DRAM refresh, plus mouse, interval timer, calendar clock, cursor fetch |
  | 9 / 11 | DWT – Display word task | Pushes bitmap words into the display FIFO |
  | 10 / 12 | CURT – Cursor task | Loads the cursor X position and cursor bits each line |
  | 11 / 13 | DHT – Display horizontal task | Once per scanline: advances the display-control-block chain |
  | 12 / 14 | DVT – Display vertical task | Once per field (60 Hz): restarts the display list, raises field interrupt |
  | 13 / 15 | PART – Parity/memory-error task | Memory error handling |
  | 14 / 16 | KWDX – Disk word task | One wakeup per disk word; highest standard priority |
  | 15 / 17 | (Trident input, optional) | Trident disk option |

  Task numbers 2, 5 and 6 are unused in the standard configuration.
- The MRT microcode comment lists its jobs: memory refresh, mouse handler, EIA (serial) handler, interval timer, calendar clock, and "part of the cursor" — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).
- **Fixed register allocation instead of context save**: the display tasks own fixed R registers (CBA, AECL, SLC, HTAB, YPOS, DWA, CURX, CURDATA), declared in the microcode. Tasks keep their state in these registers so that switching never has to save anything — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).
- **Display CPU cost**: according to the paper, a full-screen 808-line, 38-word-wide bitmap takes about half of the standard machine's main storage, and displaying it takes about 60% of the cycles — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt). Shirriff notes that the CPU can't run program instructions while the display task moves pixels, and programs run much faster with the display off — [Shirriff, "One-hour Mandelbrot"](http://www.righto.com/2017/06/one-hour-mandelbrot-creating-fractal-on.html) (search excerpt).
- The standard Alto has controllers for the disk, the display and the Ethernet, and the Ethernet uses a single task — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt).

### Inferences
- **Per-scanline cycle budget (my computation from the microcode)**: one scanline is 224 microcycles (38.08 µs ÷ 170 ns). The display word task's steady-state loop is 6 microinstructions per double-word: `MAR<-T<-DWA`, `L<-AECL-T-1`, `ALUCY,L<-2+T`, `DWA<-L`, `DDR<-MD,TASK`, `DDR<-MD`. That is 3 cycles per word, or about 114 cycles for 38 words, about 51% of the line. The horizontal task adds 11 cycles, or 17 when it moves to a new control block, per the microcode comment. The cursor task adds 2 cycles, and MRT adds roughly 10–15. This lands close to the paper's "about 60%" and leaves the emulator about 40% of the machine during active lines, with more during vertical blanking (about 35 of every ~438 lines per field).
- Why the switch is "zero overhead": each task has only a micro-PC; registers aren't saved, because every task uses its own R registers by convention. The priority encoder is combinational, so picking the next task happens in parallel with executing the current instruction.
- Why this replaced dedicated controllers: device-side hardware shrinks to small buffers (a 16-word display FIFO, a 16-word Ethernet FIFO, disk shift registers) and wakeup logic. All sequencing, address arithmetic and protocol logic is microcode on the shared ALU. The disk word task has the highest priority because a late disk word is lost for good, while the emulator can always wait.
- Ordering for animation (highest to lowest): disk word > parity > display vertical > display horizontal > cursor > display word > memory refresh > Ethernet > disk sector > emulator.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the Hardware Manual's own task-priority table or its exact wording about the one-instruction delay after TASK. The delay described here comes from the emulator implementation.
- I found no measured, published split of CPU time across all tasks. The ~51% display-word figure is my own count from the microcode.

## 3. Memory

### Takeaway
The standard Alto has 64K 16-bit words (128 KB) of semiconductor DRAM with an 850 ns cycle. The Alto I used Intel 1103 1-Kbit chips on 16 boards with parity. The Alto II moved to 4-Kbit and later 16-Kbit chips with ECC, and the Alto II XM bank-switches up to 256K words (512 KB) on four boards. Refresh is not done by hardware: a microcode task reads memory to refresh it.

### Cited Findings
- The Alto has 64K words of 16 bits and an 850 ns main memory cycle — [Alto Hardware Manual (OCR)](https://ed-thelen.org/RestoreAlto/AltoHWRefPart1-4-ocr.pdf) (search excerpt). A special double-word write cycle also writes Hamming-code check bits (same source, excerpt).
- According to the paper (search excerpt), main memory started at 64K 16-bit words built from 1-Kbit semiconductor RAM chips — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm).
- The original Alto used Intel 1103 1-Kbit DRAM, with 16 boards packed with chips giving 128 KB. The Alto II XM holds 512 KB on four boards, and each XM memory card carries eighty 4116 (16-Kbit) chips — [Shirriff, "Y Combinator's Xerox Alto"](http://www.righto.com/2016/06/y-combinators-xerox-alto-restoring.html) (search excerpt; the eighty-4116 figure is from the same excerpt set).
- Alto I memory was protected by parity. The Alto II used ECC, with 6 check bits plus a parity bit per double-word, and could reach 256 KW by bank switching versus 64 KW on the Alto I — [Computer History Wiki (gunkies)](https://gunkies.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto) and [Wikipedia, Xerox Alto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto) (search excerpts).
- **Refresh by microcode**: Xerox's 1977 change log reads "Modified MRT to refresh 16K chips". The MRT source comes in two variants, `AltoIIMRT4K.mu` (4K chips) and `AltoIIMRT16K.mu` (16K chips). In the loop, MRT issues `MAR<- R37` ("FIRST REFRESH CYCLE") and then a second MAR ("SECOND REFRESH CYCLE") and steps a refresh address. A comment says MRT wakes up about every 37 µs — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu). ContrAlto wakes MRT once per scanline — [ContrAlto DisplayController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Display/DisplayController.cs).
- **Address space**: RAM runs to 176777 octal, with the I/O page above that — [ContrAlto Memory.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Memory/Memory.cs). Search excerpts from the Hardware Manual likewise give addresses 0 to 176777B — [Alto Hardware Manual](https://ed-thelen.org/RestoreAlto/AltoHWRefPart1-4-ocr.pdf).
- **XM bank switching**: there are 16 bank registers at 177740 octal, one per task. Each holds a "normal" bank and an "alternate" bank (2 bits each) for up to four 64K banks, so every task (for example the display) can point at a different bank — [ContrAlto Memory.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Memory/Memory.cs). The standard microcode adds XMLDA/XMSTA instructions for alternate-bank access — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).
- **Microcode-visible timing (as ContrAlto models it)**: microcode starts a reference with `MAR<-`, and the data (`MD`) arrives some cycles later. On the Alto II a double-word fetch returns the two words on successive cycles, and the operation finishes at about cycle 6; on the Alto I it finishes at about cycle 7 — [ContrAlto MemoryBus.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Memory/MemoryBus.cs).

### Inferences
- 64K words × 16 bits = 1,048,576 bits = 128 KB. An 850 ns cycle is 5 microcycles of 170 ns. ContrAlto's 6–7-cycle operation windows are longer than that, possibly because they include pipeline and bus overhead, so an animation should say "about 5 microinstructions" rather than an exact count.
- The double-word fetch (one MAR, two MD reads) is why the display word task moves two words per memory reference.

### Gaps
- I couldn't confirm the Alto II's first chip type from a primary document (the microcode shows 4K refresh support, but not the part number), or whether any Alto I machines used 4K chips.
- The "256K words" XM maximum is supported by ContrAlto (4 × 64KW) and by gunkies and Wikipedia excerpts. I did not see it in the Hardware Manual text itself.

## 4. Display

### Takeaway
The Alto has a portrait, page-sized 1-bit raster display: 606 visible pixels × 808 lines, stored as 38 words (608 bits) per line. It is a 875-line interlaced raster at 30 frames/60 fields per second. There is no frame-buffer hardware: three microcode tasks walk a linked list of display control blocks and feed a 16-word FIFO from ordinary main memory. A full-screen bitmap is about 60 KB, roughly half of a standard machine's memory.

### Cited Findings
- **Resolution**: the paper (search excerpt) gives 808 visible scan lines of 608 picture elements, with the long side vertical and a screen about the size of a sheet of paper — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm). ContrAlto shows a 606 × 808 visible area with a comment that the display is actually 606 pixels wide, and clips the cursor at x < 606 — [ContrAlto AltoWindow.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/UI/AltoWindow.cs), [DisplayController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Display/DisplayController.cs). **Discrepancy**: 608 (the paper, and the bitmap word width 38 × 16) versus 606 (visible pixels, as in ContrAlto and most secondary sources). Both are right: memory holds 608 bits per line, about 606 are visible.
- **Scan standard**: an interlaced 875-line monitor at 30 frames/s (60 fields/s); the 30 Hz frame rate causes some flicker, which most users found acceptable except with fine detail that appears in only one field — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt). **Discrepancy**: another bwlampson.site excerpt mentions a "901 line TV monitor", apparently from an earlier design document — [Thacker et al. / bwlampson.site search excerpt](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm). The 38.08 µs line period and 60 Hz field rate give 875.4 lines per frame (my computation), which supports 875 for the machine as built.
- **Timing in ContrAlto**: scanline 38,080 ns (224 × 170 ns); horizontal blank about 6.08 µs; one display word about 842 ns; vertical blank about 34–35 scanlines per field (roughly 1.33 ms); fields alternate even/odd — [ContrAlto DisplayController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Display/DisplayController.cs).
- **Three tasks plus simple hardware**: the display is handled by three microcode tasks. Hardware wakes the word task whenever the FIFO isn't full, the line task when horizontal blanking starts, and the field task when vertical blanking starts; the line task runs every 38 µs and the field task 60 times a second, generating a 60 Hz interrupt — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpts). ContrAlto treats the FIFO as full at 15 or more words — [ContrAlto DisplayController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Display/DisplayController.cs). The separate cursor task is a fourth display-related task.
- **Memory bandwidth**: refreshing the display takes an average of 15 Mbit/s of memory bandwidth — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt).
- **Display list ("bands")**: a paintable bitmap is a contiguous block of words, described by a display control block. To mimic an 8.5×11" page, one control block can cover all 808 lines with no left margin at 38 words wide — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt). In the microcode, the field task (DVT) loads the first block's address from DASTART and raises the vertical-field interrupt. The line task (DHT, "11 cycles if no block change, 17 if new control block") counts down the scan-line count (SLC), follows the link to the next block, and extracts HTAB (a left-margin tab) and NWRDS (words per line). It also supports a HALFMODE (half-resolution) branch and even/odd field handling. The word task (DWT) first shifts out HTAB zero words (`DDR<-0`) and then the bitmap words — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu). DASTART is at 0x110 (octal 420) in low memory — [ContrAlto Debugger.cs reserved-location table](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/UI/Debugger.cs).
- **Cursor hardware (16×16)**: the cursor bitmap is 16 words, CURMAP0–CURMAP15, at 0x119–0x128 (octal 431–450), with CURSORX/CURSORY at 0x116/0x117 (octal 426/427) — [ContrAlto Debugger.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/UI/Debugger.cs). The cursor task is just two microinstructions, `XPREG<- CURX` and `CSR<- CURDATA`: it loads the X-position register and the 16-bit cursor shift register, which hardware mixes into the video. MRT fetches the cursor word for each line, and DVT sets the cursor up each field (comment: "HARDWARE EXPECTS X COMPLEMENTED") — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).
- **Why radical**: a full-screen bitmap takes about half of a standard machine's main storage, and displaying it about 60% of the cycles — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt).

### Inferences
- Full-screen bitmap size: 38 words × 808 lines = 30,704 words = 61,408 bytes, about 60 KB. That is 47% of a 64K-word (128 KB) machine and matches "about half". (Computed.)
- Bandwidth check: 38 words × 16 bits × 808 lines × 30 frames/s ≈ 14.7 Mbit/s, consistent with the paper's average of 15 Mbit/s. (Computed.)
- Because the display list can have fewer lines, narrower bands, or large HTAB margins, programs could reclaim both memory and CPU by shrinking the displayed area. That is why Alto software often showed a partial screen, and why turning the display off sped programs up.
- Suggested animation: a raster beam sweeping; each line the DHT flashes briefly; DWT bursts fill a 16-slot FIFO while the emulator runs in the gaps; once per field DVT resets to the head of the DCB chain.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the exact bit layout of the 4-word display control block (link, mode/HTAB/NWRDS word, bitmap address, line count) from a fetched primary document. The microcode shows the fields but not a formal spec. The Alto OS Reference Manual on bitsavers has it, but the host was blocked.
- I found no reliable source for the monitor's physical size, phosphor, or the manufacturer of the CRT.

## 5. BitBLT

### Takeaway
BitBLT, the bit-boundary block transfer that copies and combines rectangles of pixels, was created at PARC in November 1975 by Dan Ingalls, Larry Tesler, Bob Sproull and Diana Merry for Smalltalk-72. Ingalls then redesigned it and implemented it in Alto microcode, where it became a single emulator instruction (BITBLT) used by all Alto graphics and text.

### Cited Findings
- BitBLT was created by Dan Ingalls, Larry Tesler, Bob Sproull and Diana Merry at Xerox PARC in November 1975 for Smalltalk-72. Ingalls later implemented a redesigned version in microcode for Smalltalk-74 — [Wikipedia, "Bit blit"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_blit) (search excerpt); see also [Wikipedia, "Diana Merry"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diana_Merry).
- Search summaries of the Ingalls and Smalltalk pages say Ingalls combined earlier graphics routines into this one primitive and rewrote it in Alto microcode for speed — [Wikipedia, "Dan Ingalls"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Ingalls) (search excerpt, lower confidence).
- In the Alto II ROM microcode, BITBLT is one of the emulator's parameterless macro-ops. The file header records "modified by Ingalls, September 6, 1977 — BitBLT fixed (LREG bug) and extended for new memory". The routine includes "BITBLT SETUP - CALCULATE RAM STATE FROM AC2'S TABLE" and "BITBLT WORK - VERT AND HORIZ LOOPS WITH 4 SOURCES, 4 FUNCTIONS", with left-to-right and right-to-left paths (LTOR/RTOL) for overlapping copies — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).

### Inferences
- "4 sources × 4 functions" corresponds to BitBLT's source types (for example, source bitmap, complemented source, a gray/halftone pattern, and combinations) and combination rules (replace, paint/OR, invert/XOR, erase). This reading is from the microcode comment only; I didn't verify the exact list.
- BitBLT mattered because windows, proportional fonts, menus and scrolling all reduce to "move a rectangle of bits and combine it with what's there". On a bitmap display with no graphics hardware, the only way to make this fast was to hand-code it in microcode, and the Alto's writable control store made that possible.
- BitBLT runs as emulator microcode (task 0), so display, disk and Ethernet tasks preempt it constantly. It is also interruptible: the microcode keeps its state in a table the AC2 register points to.

### Gaps
- I could not access Ingalls' own accounts (e.g., "The Evolution of Smalltalk", HOPL IV 2020) to confirm the date and credits beyond the Wikipedia excerpt. Treat "November 1975" and the four names as well supported but secondary.
- I found no performance figures for BitBLT (pixels/second).

## 6. Disk

### Takeaway
The standard Alto disk is a Diablo Model 31 removable 14-inch cartridge drive holding about 2.5 MB (203 cylinders × 2 heads × 12 sectors × 256 words); the later Diablo 44 doubles the cylinders to about 5 MB. Each sector has separate header, label and data records. The label records the file ID and page number, so the file system can be rebuilt from the disk itself by the Scavenger. Two microcode tasks do the controller's work.

### Cited Findings
- The file system was implemented on a 2.5 MB moving-head, removable-media rigid disk, the Diablo Model 31 — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt).
- **Geometry**: Diablo 31 = 203 cylinders, 2 heads, 12 sectors; Diablo 44 = 406 cylinders, 2 heads, 12 sectors — [ContrAlto DiskPack.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/IO/DiskPack.cs).
- **Sector format**: a header of 2 data words plus 1 checksum word, a label of 8 data words plus 1 checksum, and data of 256 words plus 1 checksum, with preambles and postambles between records (header read delay 21 words, header preamble 34 words, inter-record preamble 3 words, and so on, quoted from AltoConsts23.mu) — [ContrAlto DiabloDrive.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/IO/DiabloDrive.cs), [DiskController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/IO/DiskController.cs).
- **Rotation / sector timing**: ContrAlto uses 40 ms per revolution divided into 12 sectors, about 3.33 ms per sector, and cites a table on page 43 of the Alto Hardware Manual — [ContrAlto DiskController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/IO/DiskController.cs).
- **Label concept**: each sector has separate header, label and data records. The controller checks the header, to confirm the hardware reached the right place, and then the label, to confirm the file-system software is consistent, before reading or writing data. The Scavenger rebuilds the index and directory structures from the file identifier and page number stored on every sector — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpts; Lampson's "Hints for computer system design" makes the same point: [bwlampson.site](https://bwlampson.site/33-Hints/WebPage.html)).
- **Tasks**: disk sector task KSEC (task 4) and disk word task KWDX (task 14, highest standard priority) — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).
- **Physical use**: booting a real Alto means loading a 14" disk pack into the front of the Diablo 31, waiting about 20 seconds for spin-up, then pressing Reset on the back of the keyboard — [ContrAlto readme.txt](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/readme.txt). ContrAlto also emulates the optional Trident controller with T-80/T-300 drives — [same](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/readme.txt).
- Shirriff's restoration day 3 covers the inside of the drive — [Shirriff, day 3](https://www.righto.com/2016/07/restoring-y-combinators-xerox-alto-day_11.html) (not fetched).

### Inferences
- Capacity: 203 × 2 × 12 = 4,872 sectors × 256 words × 2 bytes = 2,494,464 bytes, about 2.5 MB. The Diablo 44 has 9,744 sectors, about 4.99 MB. (Computed.)
- 40 ms per revolution is 1,500 rpm.
- ContrAlto divides each sector into 347 word time slots, about 9.6 µs per word, or about 1.66 Mbit/s raw. Treat this as an emulator approximation, not a drive spec. At about 56 microcycles per disk word, the disk word task needs only a few cycles per wakeup, but it must never be late, which explains its top priority.
- The label is a form of end-to-end checking: every sector says which file and page it belongs to, so a corrupted directory is recoverable. This is good material for a "robustness" beat in a video.

### Gaps
- I found no primary figure for the Diablo 31's official bit rate, average seek time or rpm (the 40 ms rotation comes from the emulator, citing the manual). Nor did I find a reliable date for when the Diablo 44 was introduced on Altos.

## 7. Ethernet interface

### Takeaway
The Alto's experimental Ethernet ran at 2.94 Mbit/s over coax. The odd rate follows from the Alto clock: one bit every two 170 ns cycles (340 ns). The interface is small hardware (Manchester encoding and 16-word FIFOs) driven by one microcode task. Ethernet first ran on November 11, 1973.

### Cited Findings
- Ethernet first ran on November 11, 1973 at 2.94 Mbit/s — [Wikipedia, "Ethernet"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethernet) (search excerpt).
- The 2.94 Mbit/s rate came from the Alto system clock (a secondary source says it was half the clock rate, with the clock itself set by DRAM access speed) — [akapugs, "3Mb Ethernet and Holy Wars"](https://akapugs.blog/2022/05/17/681/) (search excerpt; secondary, lower confidence).
- ContrAlto models Ethernet timing as about 5,400 ns to receive one 16-bit word and about 87,075 ns to send 16 words at "3mbit" — [ContrAlto EthernetController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/IO/EthernetController.cs).
- The Alto Ethernet uses Manchester encoding (a 1 is sent as "10", a 0 as "01"). Transmit and receive go through a 16-word FIFO built from four Intel 3101A RAM chips of 16×4 bits each, and much of the protocol is in microcode — [Shirriff, "Fixing the Ethernet board from a vintage Xerox Alto"](http://www.righto.com/2017/11/fixing-ethernet-board-from-vintage.html) (search excerpt).
- There is one Ethernet task (EREST, task 7). The microcode's post-and-wait loop relies on MRT to generate a wakeup about every 37 µs for timing — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu); the paper also says Ethernet uses one task — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt).
- Thacker is credited as co-inventor of Ethernet with Robert Metcalfe, David Boggs and Butler Lampson — [CACM, "In Memoriam: Charles P. 'Chuck' Thacker"](https://cacm.acm.org/news/218536-in-memoriam-charles-p-chuck-thacker-1943-2017/fulltext) (search excerpt).
- Shirriff built a BeagleBone gateway to the Alto's 3 Mb/s Ethernet — [Shirriff, "Xerox Alto's 3 Mb/s Ethernet"](http://www.righto.com/2018/01/xerox-altos-3-mbs-ethernet-building.html).

### Inferences
- 2 × 170 ns = 340 ns per bit, which is 2.94 Mbit/s exactly. This matches ContrAlto's ~5.4 µs per 16-bit word (16 × 340 ns = 5.44 µs). So the "odd" number is the Alto microcycle clock divided by 2. (Computed; consistent with the secondary sources.)
- One 16-bit Ethernet word arrives about every 32 microcycles. A 16-word FIFO gives the Ethernet task about 500 cycles (about 87 µs) of slack, which lets it sit below the display tasks in priority.

### Gaps
- I couldn't fetch Metcalfe and Boggs' 1976 CACM paper or Shirriff's full Ethernet post to confirm the coax details (cable type, taps, maximum length) or the Alto transceiver design. I have no verified source for the "coax" detail beyond general knowledge, so treat it as unverified here.

## 8. Input devices: keyboard, mouse, keyset

### Takeaway
The input devices need almost no hardware. The keyboard is a set of memory-mapped bits (4 words) that software polls; the 3-button mouse's motion is counted by the memory-refresh microcode task, which updates X/Y words in memory; the 5-key chord keyset from Engelbart's lineage shares a memory-mapped word with the mouse buttons.

### Cited Findings
- **Keyboard**: memory-mapped at 177034–177037 octal (4 words = 64 key bits, active-low), with no dedicated microcode task. ContrAlto can press "boot address key combinations" — [ContrAlto Keyboard.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/IO/Keyboard.cs). ContrAlto emulates the 61-key Alto II keyboard, which has special keys such as LF, BS, three blank keys and LOCK — [ContrAlto readme.txt](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/readme.txt).
- **Mouse processing in microcode**: MRT begins `SINK_ MOUSE, BUS; MOUSE DATA IS ANDED WITH 17B`, dispatches on the mouse-change bits, and updates coordinates in memory at MOUSELOC ("START THE FETCH OF THE COORDINATES" … "NOW RESTORE THE UPDATED COORDINATES") — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu). MOUSEX and MOUSEY live at 0x114 and 0x115 (octal 424/425) — [ContrAlto Debugger.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/UI/Debugger.cs). ContrAlto's `<-MOUSE` function moves the pointer one step toward its target on each read — [ContrAlto MouseAndKeyset.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/IO/MouseAndKeyset.cs).
- **Buttons / keyset share hardware**: the mouse buttons (left, middle, right) and the 5 keyset keys share one memory-mapped address — [ContrAlto MouseAndKeyset.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/IO/MouseAndKeyset.cs).
- **Keyset**: a 5-key chording keyboard was a standard peripheral early on but "never caught on" — [ContrAlto readme.txt](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/readme.txt). The Alto's keyset was nearly identical to Engelbart's, and while the mouse was an instant success, the keyset never became popular — [DigiBarn, "Mouse from Xerox Alto (with keyset)"](https://www.digibarn.com/collections/devices/alto-mouse/index.html) (search excerpt).
- **Mouse design history**: in 1972 Jack Hawley and Bill English designed a digital mouse for PARC that used a metal ball pressed against two rollers, replacing Engelbart's two perpendicular wheels. Hawley produced mice for the Alto from about 1975 — [Computer History blog, "The ball mouse and Xerox"](http://hascomuter.blogspot.com/2012/10/the-ball-mouse-and-xerox.html) and [Computermuseum UK, mice](https://www.computermuseum.org.uk/fixed_pages/mice.html) (search excerpts; secondary). Early Alto mice had three narrow buttons arranged top to bottom, and the Alto mouse used a steel ball before Xerox moved to optical mice (the Star in 1981 used an optical mouse) — [Low End Mac, "Mouse Design: 1963 to 1983"](https://lowendmac.com/2015/mouse-design-1963-to-1983/) and [oldmouse.com](https://www.oldmouse.com/mouse/xerox/alto.shtml) (search excerpts; secondary).

### Inferences
- The mouse needs no interrupt and no controller. Hardware latches a few quadrature/direction bits, and every ~38 µs the refresh task, which runs anyway, folds any motion into the X/Y words in memory. Software, or the cursor logic in DVT, reads those words. This is a good example for animation of how the Alto reuses one task for unrelated jobs.
- Keyboard polling: because keys are just bits in the I/O page, any program (and the boot logic) can read the whole keyboard state at once. That is what makes the multi-key "boot address" trick possible (see section 10).

### Gaps
- Sources disagree on whether the very first 1973 Alto mice used wheels or a ball. The 1972 Hawley/English ball mouse suggests ball mice from the start, but I found no primary PARC document confirming which mouse shipped with the first Altos. I also found no mouse resolution (counts per inch).

## 9. Physical form, Alto I vs Alto II, production

### Takeaway
An Alto is a floor-standing cabinet about the size of a dorm mini-fridge, usually under or beside a desk. The Diablo drive sits on top and the card cage and switching power supplies are below. The portrait display, keyboard, mouse and keyset sit on the desk. About 1,000 were in use by summer 1979 and roughly 2,000 were eventually built, mostly Alto IIs. The Alto was never sold as a product.

### Cited Findings
- The metal cabinet is about the size of a dorm mini-fridge, with a Diablo disk drive on top and a chassis of power supplies and circuit boards below. From the back you can see four blue switching power supplies with the card cage behind them — [Shirriff, "Y Combinator's Xerox Alto"](http://www.righto.com/2016/06/y-combinators-xerox-alto-restoring.html) and [restoration day 1](http://www.righto.com/2016/06/restoring-y-combinators-xerox-alto-day.html) (search excerpts).
- The display surface is almost exactly the size of a page — [Thacker et al. / bwlampson.site](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpt).
- **Dates and counts**: PARC designed the Alto in early 1973 as an experiment in personal computing, and by summer 1979 nearly a thousand Altos were in regular use by researchers, engineers and secretaries — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm) (search excerpts). Wikipedia says Xerox built about 2,000 Altos for its own use and for universities and research labs, never as a product. It also says an initial run of 80 units was produced by Clement Designlabs, and that about 1,000 were in Xerox labs and about 500 at universities by the late 1970s. The infobox (per search excerpt) gives 120 Alto I and 2,000 Alto II units — [Wikipedia, Xerox Alto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto) (search excerpts). **Discrepancy / softness**: "120 Alto I" versus the "80-unit pilot run" and the original plan of about 30 machines (dailytechnewsshow/Quora excerpts) aren't reconciled, so treat Alto I counts as uncertain.
- **Cost**: Lampson's December 1972 "Why Alto" proposal estimated about $10.5K per machine (search excerpt; this is a design-phase estimate) — [Lampson, "Why Alto"](https://bwlampson.site/38a-WhyAlto/WebPage.html).
- **First machine**: a widely repeated story says the first Alto came up in April 1973 and showed an animated Cookie Monster image digitized by Alan Kay's group. Another says Thacker bet an executive he could build it in three months and missed slightly — [Clot Magazine op-ed](https://clotmag.com/oped/why-cookie-monster-is-the-mascot-of-big-tech-and-has-been-since-the-1960s-by-lucy-hunter), [Daily Tech News Show](https://dailytechnewsshow.com/2024/09/05/about-xerox-alto/) (search excerpts; secondary and anecdotal, lower confidence).
- **Alto II changes**: memory expandable to 256 KW by bank switching (XM) versus 64 KW; ECC instead of parity; optional extra microcode PROM or three 1K RAM banks — [gunkies](https://gunkies.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto) (search excerpts). Denser memory chips: 4K, then 16K 4116s on four XM boards of 512 KB — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu), [Shirriff](http://www.righto.com/2016/06/y-combinators-xerox-alto-restoring.html). ContrAlto emulates both "Alto I and Alto II XM CPU" and models different memory timing for each — [ContrAlto readme.txt](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/readme.txt), [MemoryBus.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Memory/MemoryBus.cs). The Alto II microcode (`altoIIcode3.mu`) is derived from ALTOIICODE2.MU of 1976 — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).

### Inferences
- The Alto II appears to be mainly a manufacturable, denser re-engineering (fewer memory boards, ECC, more microstore) rather than an architectural change: task structure, clock and display are the same, and ContrAlto runs the same microcode families on both.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable figures for **power draw, weight, cabinet dimensions, noise or heat**. The mini-fridge comparison is the only size descriptor I found. I could also not pin down the exact year the Alto II was introduced (1976 is implied by the ALTOIICODE2 date of August 1976, but that is an inference).

## 10. Clever hardware tricks worth animating

### Takeaway
The Alto's best "reveal" moments are the places where one tiny piece of hardware plus a few microinstructions does a job that other computers used dedicated boards for. Examples: per-instruction task switching by priority encoder, DRAM refresh and the mouse handled by the same task, display lists walked by microcode into a 16-word FIFO, a clock chosen to fit the display line, a label on every disk sector, and booting over the network by holding down keys.

### Cited Findings
- **Per-instruction task arbitration**: TASK plus a priority encoder over 16 wakeup lines, and the switch lands after the next instruction — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm), [ContrAlto CPU.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/CPU.cs).
- **Display cycle stealing via FIFO**: DWT wakes whenever the FIFO isn't full, and the tight loop `DDR<-MD, TASK; DDR<-MD` moves a double-word per memory reference and offers to yield — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu), [ContrAlto DisplayController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Display/DisplayController.cs).
- **Refresh as a side effect of a task**: MRT does two dummy memory references per wakeup to refresh DRAM rows, and also services the mouse, interval timer, calendar clock and cursor — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu).
- **Clock locked to display**: 224 × 170 ns = one 38.08 µs scanline — [ContrAlto DisplayController.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Display/DisplayController.cs); the 224-submultiple claim comes from a search excerpt (see section 1).
- **Ethernet boot ("nose boot")**: pressing the boot button with BS (backspace) plus other keys held down starts the Ethernet boot loader. Other held keys select a boot file, and EtherBoot broadcasts its request unless a host is specified — [Alto docs, etherboot.tty (CHM Xerox Alto archive)](https://xeroxalto.computerhistory.org/_cd8_/altodocs/.etherboot.tty!2.html) (search excerpt). Each key sets one bit of the boot address, so users sometimes needed their nose to hold enough keys — [Hacker News comment](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=12198291) (search excerpt; anecdotal). ContrAlto has code to press "boot address key combinations" automatically — [ContrAlto Keyboard.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/IO/Keyboard.cs). A BOOT STARTF performs a "silent boot" that resets each task's micro-PC and chooses ROM or RAM microcode per task from the RMR register — [ContrAlto CPU.cs](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/CPU/CPU.cs).
- **Microcode you can rewrite**: JMPR/RDRM/WTRM let programs load and jump into microcode RAM, which is how Smalltalk, Mesa and BitBLT variants got custom microcode — [altoIIcode3.mu](https://github.com/livingcomputermuseum/ContrAlto/blob/master/Contralto/Disassembly/altoIIcode3.mu); on the 1K PROM + 3K RAM configuration see [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm).
- **Disk header/label/data checks** and the Scavenger — [Thacker et al.](https://bwlampson.site/25-Alto/25-AltoOCR.htm).

### Inferences
- Suggested animation beats, in order: (1) a single ALU with 16 "request lamps" and a priority arrow picking the winner each cycle. (2) One scanline as a 224-slot timeline: DHT at the start, DWT bursts, cursor, MRT, and the emulator filling the gaps, with disk-word interrupts punching through. (3) Memory as a 128 KB bar with ~60 KB highlighted as the screen. (4) A disk sector strip: header, label, data, with the label's file ID and page number lighting up during a Scavenger rebuild. (5) Ethernet bits clocked at every other microcycle. (6) The "nose boot" key chord.
- The "emulator gets the leftovers" framing is the key intuition: user programs are the lowest-priority task, so the machine is best seen as an I/O engine that runs your program in its spare time.

### Gaps
- I couldn't verify the internal hardware details of the cursor mixing logic or the display shift register beyond the microcode functions (XPREG, CSR, DDR). I also found no primary source quantifying task-switch frequency beyond the manual's "typically every few microseconds".
