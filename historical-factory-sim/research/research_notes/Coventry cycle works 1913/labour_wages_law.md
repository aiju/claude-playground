# Labour, wages, working time and employment law in a Coventry cycle/engineering works, c. 1913

Source key (full URLs are repeated inline below):
- **Carr 1978**: F. W. Carr, *Engineering workers and the rise of Labour in Coventry 1914–1939*, PhD thesis, Warwick, 1978. Its chapter 1 covers the years before 1914 and draws on the Coventry Trades Council Annual Report 1913, Workers' Union annual reports, the minutes of the Coventry and District Engineering Employers' Association (CDEEA) and ASE district minutes. Page numbers are the thesis's own; PDF page = thesis page + 6.
- **Weekes 1970**: B. C. M. Weekes, *The Amalgamated Society of Engineers 1880–1914*, PhD thesis, Warwick, 1970.
- **NI Act 1911**: the full text of the National Insurance Act 1911 (1 & 2 Geo. 5 c. 55) as reproduced by the Socialist Health Association.
- **Bowstead 1901**: W. Bowstead, *The law relating to factories and workshops, as amended and consolidated by the Factory and Workshop Act, 1901* (Stevens & Sons, 1901). It contains the annotated Act and, in Appendix B, the Truck Acts 1831–1896.

- **Williams 1915**: Alfred Williams, *Life in a Railway Factory* (1915), a first-hand account of the GWR works at **Swindon**, c. 1900–1914. It is not Coventry, and is used only for shop-floor routine.
- **Cadbury et al. 1906**: E. Cadbury, M. C. Matheson and G. Shann, *Women's Work and Wages* (1906). It covers **Birmingham**, c. 1904–05.

## 1. Working time: weekly hours, daily timetable, holidays, overtime, night shifts

### Takeaway
Coventry engineering in 1910–1913 worked a nominal **53- or 54-hour week**, with Saturday ending at noon. The CDEEA–union agreement of August 1910 says 54 hours for day workers and 45 for night workers. The Trades Council's 1913 directory and the ASE's 1909 standard rates both say 53 hours. Overtime was heavy in the spring and autumn peaks: up to 15 hours a week, against a "best efforts" limit of 32 hours a month, giving a permitted maximum of 69 hours. Holidays were the bank holidays plus customary closures, and the evidence points to them being **unpaid**.

### Cited Findings
- **The 1910 Coventry agreement on hours and overtime.** In August 1910 the CDEEA and the five principal engineering unions in the city agreed: "In view of the special conditions of trade in Coventry, the Employers' Association are unable to agree to a limitation of overtime in all cases to 32 hours per month, but are prepared to agree to a maximum of 15 hours in any one week, and that systematic overtime is to be deprecated … and that an effort shall be made to observe as far as possible a limit of 32 hours per month." — [Carr 1978, p. 26](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - The same agreement "stated that the basic week for day workers was one of 54 hours, with work ending at noon on Saturday, and 45 hours for night workers. Thus the maximum hours permitted by the agreement were 69 a week." — [Carr 1978, p. 26](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - Its title was "Terms of Working Conditions to be Maintained in Workshops", signed with the Coventry District Committees of the ASE, the Amalgamated Society of Toolmakers, the United Machine Workers' Association, the United Patternmakers' Association and the Steam Engine Makers' Society (Carr's footnote 35). — [Carr 1978, notes to ch. 1](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Overtime in peak seasons.** "A major contributor to high earnings was excessively long hours; in spring and autumn, peak production times in the vehicle industry, great pressure was put on workers to regard overtime as compulsory." The national agreement between the engineering unions and the Engineering Employers' Federation (EEF) "was supposed to limit the hours of overtime to … 32 hours a month, but allowed work to fixed delivery dates or emergency work to carry unlimited overtime." — [Carr 1978, pp. 25–26](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - Carr adds that the excessive overtime "has to be seen in the context of systematic short-time working and layoffs in times of low demand". — [Carr 1978, p. 26](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **53 hours in 1913.** The Coventry Trades Council Annual Report for 1913 gives "engineering workers … a 53 hour basic week". Adult shop assistants worked up to 62 hours exclusive of meal breaks. — [Carr 1978, p. 40](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **53 hours in 1909.** The ASE's recognised "usual working hours" for Coventry (General) at 1 October 1909 were **53 per week**. — [BoT 1909, Engineers table, pp. 44–45](https://archive.org/details/wagesstandardtim00grearich)
- **54 hours for labourers.** The May 1912 Workers' Union settlement gave unskilled men "27/- for a 54 hour week", while the "fitters' and turners' rate [was] 38/- for 53 hours". — [Carr 1978, pp. 33–34](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Legal limits for women and young persons (under 18) in non-textile factories** (Factory and Workshop Act 1901, s. 26). These rules constrain any timetable that includes women or girls. — [Bowstead 1901, pp. 38–39](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
  - Weekday period of employment: 6 a.m.–6 p.m., 7 a.m.–7 p.m. or 8 a.m.–8 p.m.
  - Saturday: 6 a.m.–2 p.m., 7 a.m.–3 p.m. or 8 a.m.–4 p.m.
  - Meals: at least 1½ hours on weekdays, of which at least 1 hour falls before 3 p.m., and at least ½ hour on Saturday.
  - No spell of more than 5 hours without at least ½ hour for a meal.
- **Posted hours and the official clock.** The occupier had to fix the period of employment and the meal times and post them in a notice in the works (s. 32). A change required prior notice to the inspector. — [Bowstead 1901, p. 45](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
  - Section 128 required a posted "notice of the clock (if any) by which the period of employment and times for meals in the factory or workshop are regulated". — [Bowstead 1901, p. 160](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
- **Statutory holidays for women, young persons and children** (s. 35, England): "Christmas Day, Good Friday, and every Bank holiday, unless, in lieu of any of those days, another whole holiday or two half holidays, fixed by the occupier, be allowed." — [Bowstead 1901, p. 47](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
- **Bank holidays.** The Bank Holidays Act 1871 made the English bank holidays Easter Monday, Whit Monday, the first Monday in August and 26 December. Good Friday and Christmas Day were traditional days of rest. — [Wikipedia, Bank Holidays Act 1871](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_Holidays_Act_1871)
- **Holidays were unpaid at Swindon (railway works, flagged).** The works' annual "Trip" in July lasted about eight days. "At the end of the first week after the Trip holiday there will be no money to draw … the toilers march past the pay-table and receive nothing." At Whitsun and the August bank holiday "more than one day's leave is seldom granted". — [Williams 1915, ch. "The Factory Year – Holidays – Trip"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
- **Overtime and holiday rates under the London ASE rules of July 1889 (flagged: London, and a union circular rather than a negotiated agreement).** — [Weekes 1970, ch. 1](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/55860/1/WRAP_thesis_Weekes_1970.pdf)
  - "54 hours constitutes a week's work"; 9 hours a day.
  - Overtime at "not less than time and a quarter for the first two hours, after that time and a half; each day to stand for itself".
  - Sunday work at not less than time and a half; night shifts at not less than time and a half.
  - Chargemen at least 2s a week above the ordinary rate.
  - District holidays: Good Friday, Easter Monday and Tuesday, Whit Monday and Tuesday, the August bank holiday, and three days at Christmas.
  - Christmas Day at double time; Easter Monday, Whit Monday, the first Monday in August and Boxing Day at not less than time and a quarter.
- **Office hours (Swindon, flagged).** The principal office staff "do not begin work at all before nine o'clock in the morning and finish at five-thirty in the afternoon". Shop clerks arrived a little after the workmen and left a little before them. — [Williams 1915, ch. on morning arrival](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)

### Inferences
- **A 54-hour timetable** that fits both the 1910 agreement and the Factory Act limits:
  - Monday to Friday: 6:00–8:15, breakfast 8:15–9:00, 9:00–1:00, dinner 1:00–2:00, 2:00–5:30. That is 9¾ hours a day, or 48¾ hours.
  - Saturday: 6:00–8:15, breakfast, 9:00–12:00. That is 5¼ hours.
  - Total: **54.0 hours**. The 9:00 restart after breakfast matches Williams's "losing a quarter" practice (section 2).
- **A 53-hour variant**:
  - Monday to Friday: 6:00–8:00, breakfast 8:00–8:30, 8:30–12:30, dinner 12:30–1:30, 1:30–5:00. That is 9½ hours a day, or 47½ hours.
  - Saturday: 6:00–8:00, 8:30–12:00. That is 5½ hours.
  - Total: **53.0 hours**.
  - Both timetables are my reconstructions, not documented Coventry timetables.
- **The legal ceiling for women and young persons** was 10½ hours on weekdays and 7½ on Saturday, so 60 hours a week. A 53- or 54-hour works therefore had legal room only for modest overtime for its women and girls. In my reading, women's overtime under the special exceptions (ss. 49–50) did not normally cover metal works. This is not verified (see Gaps).
- The 53 versus 54 discrepancy probably reflects different groups or dates: 53 hours for skilled time-workers (ASE 1909, Trades Council 1913) and 54 hours under the 1910 general agreement and the 1912 labourers' settlement. A simulation could use 53 for craftsmen and 54 for labourers and production hands, or simply choose one.
- The night shift (45 hours) was worked only by adult men, since the Factory Act periods of employment exclude night work for women and young persons.

### Gaps
- I found no documented Coventry daily timetable (start time, meal times, finish) for 1913. The Board of Trade *Standard Time Rates* 1913 and the *Labour Gazette* 1912–14 were not accessible online.
- Coventry overtime premiums (for example time and a quarter or time and a half) are not confirmed. Only the London 1889 ASE rules and the Carlisle clause "overtime … as already prevail in each workshop" were found.
- I found no direct evidence on whether any Coventry firm paid holidays in 1913.
- Whether women in cycle works could be worked overtime under FWA s. 49 (press of work) was not checked.

## 2. Timekeeping: checks, time recorders, the gate, lateness and absence

### Takeaway
The standard British system c. 1913 was a **numbered brass check** (token) for each worker. The worker dropped it into a check box at the gate or shop on arrival, and the timekeeper reconciled the boxes against a register. Mechanical "time check" machines and card time recorders (Bundy, Gledhill-Brook) existed and were spreading. Lateness was punished by docking a quarter hour or more of pay after a few minutes' grace, and by locking out latecomers until the next meal break ("losing a quarter").

### Cited Findings
- **The brass check (Swindon, flagged).** "Every workman is provided with a brass check or 'ticket,' round in shape like a penny, or oblong, with a number stamped upon it, corresponding to his name in the register. This has to be placed in the check-box each time the man enters the shed, and it is the only accepted proof of his attendance at work or absence from it." — [Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
  - A lost or mislaid check cost "a sum equal to half an hour's wages … fourpence or fivepence for a portion of metal that is worth no more than a farthing". — [Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
- **Lateness fines and lock-out (Swindon, flagged).** "after the five minutes' grace, whoever is late is charged quarter of an hour for the first five minutes, and half an hour for the next, i.e., till six-fifteen … No workman is admitted later than that hour, but must wait till the re-start after breakfast." — [Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
  - When the hooter stopped, "the ponderous doors are closed with a loud bang". — [Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
  - "If a workman oversleeps in the morning and is too late for admittance before breakfast, he may start at nine o'clock. This is called 'losing a quarter.' There are those … noted for losing quarters; they are usually absent … before breakfast once or twice a week." — [Williams 1915, ch. "Pay-day – Losing a quarter"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
- **"Quartering" in 1832 textiles (flagged: early-19th-century textile mills).** In Elizabeth Bentley's evidence to the Sadler Committee, being "quartered" meant losing half an hour's pay for being a quarter of an hour late. I saw this only in a search-result summary, not on a fetched page. — [Victorian Web, Sadler Committee evidence](https://www.victorianweb.org/history/workers1.html)
- **Mechanical recorders.**
  - Frank Brook invented "a machine to record the 'checks' deposited by workers on arrival and departure". His first successful patent dates from 1893. The 'Paragon' time check machine was marketed by the Brook Time Checking Clock Company from 1896, and Brook also sold US-made Bundy key and card recorders (1899–1907). — [workclocks.co.uk, Brook Time Check Company](https://workclocks.co.uk/brook-time-check-company/)
  - Gledhill-Brook Time Recorders Ltd (Huddersfield) was registered in **1912** to make card time recorders. It exhibited "time checking and costing systems" at the Business Exhibition at Olympia in 1912. — [Grace's Guide, Gledhill-Brook Time Recorders](https://gracesguide.co.uk/Gledhill-Brook_Time_Recorders)
- **The legal basis for fines.** Fines and deductions were lawful under the Truck Act 1896 s. 1 only if all of the following held. — [Bowstead 1901, App. B, Truck Act 1896](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
  - The terms were in a notice "kept constantly affixed" where workmen could see, read and copy it, or in a written contract signed by the workman.
  - The contract specified the acts or omissions and the amount of the fine.
  - The fine was for an act that "causes or is likely to cause damage or loss to the employer, or interruption or hindrance to his business".
  - The amount was "fair and reasonable".
  - "Particulars in writing … are supplied to the workman on each occasion".
  - The employer kept a **register of deductions/fines** open to the factory inspector (s. 6(3)).
  - The worker could recover unlawful deductions within six months (s. 5).
- **The general register** (FWA 1901 s. 129) recorded children and young persons employed, lime-washing, every notifiable accident and every special exception used. — [Bowstead 1901, p. 161](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)

### Inferences
- **A model for the Coventry works:**
  - Each employee carries a works number stamped on a brass check.
  - The check goes into a box at the gatehouse or time office at each start (morning, after breakfast, after dinner).
  - The gate shuts at the hooter. A few minutes' grace is allowed, then a quarter hour or more is docked.
  - Anyone arriving more than about 15 minutes late is shut out until the next spell.
  - A posted rule-book ("works rules"), required by the Truck Act for fines to be lawful, lists the fines.
- Card recorders were commercially available in Britain by 1912. A larger Coventry firm might plausibly have one in the time office, but checks were probably still the norm in 1913. This is inferred; I have no Coventry evidence.
- The general register covered only children and young persons; adult men's attendance was recorded in the employer's own time books. Absences would show as a missing check, entered by the timekeeper.

### Gaps
- I found no Coventry-specific evidence on time recorders versus checks, lateness rules or works rules. Coventry Archives and the Modern Records Centre company records (Rudge-Whitworth, Triumph, Singer, Humber) were not examined online.

## 3. Hiring and leaving

### Takeaway
Hiring and firing were in the **foreman's hands** and very fluid. Coventry's trade was seasonal, and men were laid off "as soon as their jobs were finished". The labour force was largely migrant and young. From 1912 the state added paperwork: a health-insurance contribution card for every manual worker aged 16 or over, and an unemployment book for workmen in insured trades. Labour Exchanges (from 1910) were available but optional.

### Cited Findings
- **The foreman's authority.** "Even after the War, the foreman was a person of considerable authority." Rationalisation meant "a shift in power away from the chargehand to the foreman and later the manager". — [Carr 1978, p. 11](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Lay-offs between jobs, and dismissal for asking for the rate (Coventry Ordnance Works, 1907).** "men asking for the district rate were being sacked … 'as soon as their jobs were finished out they had to go till fresh jobs were ready.'" — [Carr 1978, p. 29](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - In 1907 the Workers' Union said unskilled men were "open to the danger of all being cleared out en bloc as men of their class were easy to obtain". — [Carr 1978, p. 32](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **A migrant, young, male workforce.** — [Carr 1978, pp. 13–14](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - Coventry's population rose from 69,978 (1901) to 106,349 (1911), "the fastest growth for any County Borough in the Kingdom", and was an estimated 119,000 in 1914.
  - Two-thirds of the 1901–11 increase came from migration: 10,600 from rural Warwickshire and 5,600 from Birmingham.
  - By 1911 over 60% of inhabitants were under 30, and males outnumbered females by 2,200.
  - Housing was short, so many workers lodged or travelled in daily.
- **Labour Exchanges.**
  - The Labour Exchanges Act 1909 created state-funded labour exchanges. — [Wikipedia, Labour Exchanges Act 1909](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Exchanges_Act_1909)
  - The first exchanges opened on 1 February 1910. This comes from a secondary summary. — [schoolshistory.org.uk](https://schoolshistory.org.uk/topics/british-history/modern-britain/liberal-reforms/labour-exchange-act-1909/)
  - The NI Act 1911 s. 99 let the Board of Trade arrange with an employer for "workmen engaged by him through a labour exchange" to have the employer's insurance duties performed by the exchange. — [NI Act 1911, s. 99](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
- **The papers attached to an engaged worker under the NI Act 1911.** — [NI Act 1911](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
  - Health (Part I): every employed person aged **16 or over** under a contract of service or apprenticeship (s. 1, Sched. 1). Non-manual employees paid over **£160 a year** were excepted (Sched. 1 Part II (g)).
  - Unemployment (Part II): a "workman" is any person **aged 16 or over** employed "wholly or mainly by way of manual labour" in an insured trade, "but does not include an indentured apprentice" (s. 107).
  - Regulations governed contributions "by means of adhesive or other stamps affixed to or impressed upon books or cards" and the "issue, sale, custody, production, and delivery up of books or cards" (s. 7 for Part I, s. 85(5) for Part II).
  - Employers bought stamps through the Post Office, deducted the employee's share and fixed the stamps to contribution cards. — [Wikipedia, National Insurance Act 1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Insurance_Act_1911); [GBPS, Health Insurance Stamps 1912–48](https://gbps.org.uk/displays/competition-entries/2023/health-insurance-stamps-1912-48)
- **Leaving and unemployment benefit.**
  - A workman who "loses employment through misconduct or who voluntarily leaves his employment without just cause" was disqualified from unemployment benefit for **six weeks** (s. 87(2)).
  - Workers laid off by a trade dispute at their own works were disqualified while the stoppage lasted (s. 87(1)).
  - A claimant could refuse a job vacant because of a trade dispute, or one below his usual district rate, without losing benefit (s. 86).
  - — [NI Act 1911](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
- **Casual taking-on (Swindon, flagged).** When the broad gauge was converted, "great numbers of men from all quarters were put on at the works … and were again distributed over the countryside", and some remained "numbered among the 'casuals'". — [Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
- **Certificates of fitness.** Under-16s needed a certificate of fitness within seven working days of starting (section 6). — [Bowstead 1901, s. 63](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)

### Inferences
- **A plausible engagement sequence for 1913:**
  1. The man applies at the gate or time office, or is sent by the Labour Exchange, or is "spoken for" by an existing hand.
  2. The foreman of the relevant shop decides.
  3. The time office enters him in the wages book and allots a works number and brass check.
  4. He hands over his health-insurance card, and for insured trades his unemployment book, to be stamped weekly.
  5. A youth under 16 is sent to the certifying surgeon within 7 working days.
- Engineering employment was at will. Most hourly-paid men could be stopped at short notice, at the end of a job or a day or a week. This is inferred from Carr's "had to go till fresh jobs were ready"; I found no documented Coventry notice rule.

### Gaps
- I found no source for customary **notice periods** (an hour's notice or a week's notice) in Coventry engineering in 1913, nor for the use of written **references** or "characters".
- I could not establish the opening date or location of the **Coventry Labour Exchange**, or its use by cycle firms.
- I found nothing on whether firms used **apprenticeship indentures** or charged premiums in Coventry cycle works.

## 4. Wages: rates by trade, piecework and premium bonus, payment and deductions

### Takeaway
Coventry was a high-wage district. In 1912–13 the documented rates were:
- **labourers 6d/hr** (27s for 54 hours), the district rate won by the Workers' Union in May 1912;
- **machine hands about 7d**;
- **skilled production workers 8d**;
- **fitters and turners about 38s for 53 hours** (about 8½–8¾d);
- **toolroom 9d**;
- **coppersmiths 9½d**;
- **coachmakers and panel-beaters 9–10d**;
- **patternmakers 10¼d**, the top rate.

Women in the Birmingham cycle trade around 1905 averaged about 10s 6d a week. Production work was increasingly on piecework or premium bonus. Wages were paid weekly in cash.

### Cited Findings
- **The Coventry Trades Council directory of hourly rates, 1913.** Checked against the page image of the thesis. — [Carr 1978, p. 40](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - Patternmakers: 10¼d an hour (the highest-paid group in the city).
  - Coachmakers and panel-beaters: 9d to 10d.
  - Coppersmiths: 9½d.
  - Woodcutting machinists: 9d to 10d.
  - Toolroom workers: 9d.
  - Skilled production workers: 8d.
  - Brassworkers: 8–8½d.
  - Machine hands: approximately 7d.
  - Labourers: 6d.
  - "In fact many production workers would get higher pay, through payment by results schemes."
  - Electricians were paid the same as skilled production workers.
  - Outside engineering: skilled building workers 9¾d, Corporation employees up to 6½d, adult shop assistants 4½d to 7d.
- **The May 1912 settlement.** The Workers' Union campaigned "for a minimum wage of 6d. an hour for unskilled engineering workers (… about 70% of the skilled rate)". A short strike at three of the largest Federated factories in May 1912 won "27/- for a 54 hour week" and local recognition. — [Carr 1978, pp. 32–34](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - The rate was "still well below the fitters' and turners' rate of 38/- for 53 hours".
  - It gave "some men … an increase of up to 6/9d a week" and established "for the first time … a single district rate for labourers".
  - John Beard claimed "6d per hour minimum in the cycle, motor and engineering trades".
- **Earlier rates.** In 1907 "the district rate for skilled workers … was 8½d an hour, but men in the gun bays at the [Coventry] Ordnance were only getting 8d." — [Carr 1978, p. 29](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **ASE standard weekly rates for Coventry (General), 1 October 1909, for a 53-hour week.** — [BoT 1909, pp. 44–45](https://archive.org/details/wagesstandardtim00grearich)
  - Turners 36s 0d; fitters 36s 0d; smiths 36s 0d; millwrights 36s 0d.
  - Patternmakers 42s 6d and 45s 0d.
  - Coppersmiths 38s 3d; brass finishers 36s 0d.
  - One machine grade (borers, planers or millers; the OCR column alignment is unclear) 32s 0d.
  - Birmingham (General) was 37s for turners and fitters on the same date.
- **Men undercutting the rate.** In 1913 the ASE's George Ryder complained of "fitters, turners, smiths, etc., who have been content to work for from two to five shillings under rate". — [Carr 1978, p. 35](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Piecework in Coventry.** "the move [to piecework] in Coventry was particularly rapid. Often it was gang piece-work". Prices were set individually ("mutuality"). Skilled setters and toolmakers stayed on time rates while semi-skilled machine operators went onto piece rates. "for most unskilled and semi-skilled workers, piece-work preceded effective trade union organisation." — [Carr 1978, pp. 23–25](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - Coventry foundries already paid by results and typically made no payment for "scrapped castings". — [Carr 1978, pp. 24–25](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **The premium bonus at Daimler.** In 1907 "the tinsmiths at Daimler went on strike against a premium bonus system, but although they were out for seven weeks they [lost]". — [Carr 1978, pp. 24, 40](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - Under it, "workers were nominally paid day rates, but were given a bonus if they did a job in shorter time than that allowed … The worker was allowed only a portion of the time saved, hence its unpopularity compared to straight piece-work." — [Carr 1978, n. 33](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **The Rowan and Halsey systems.** — [Weekes 1970, pp. 175–178](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/55860/1/WRAP_thesis_Weekes_1970.pdf)
  - **Rowan** (J. Rowan, Glasgow, first used 1898): bonus = (time saved ÷ time allowed) × time worked. Example: 10 h allowed, done in 7, gives a bonus of 3/10 × 7 = 2.1 h, so 9.1 h pay. "it was never possible to earn double time."
  - **Halsey** (introduced into Britain about 1890): time rate plus a percentage of the time saved, commonly one-third. Example: 10 h allowed, done in 7, gives 7 + 1 = 8 h pay.
- **The Carlisle agreement.** The ASE Executive and the EEF signed it after a conference at Carlisle on 19–20 August 1902. Its four clauses were: — [Weekes 1970, pp. 178–180](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/55860/1/WRAP_thesis_Weekes_1970.pdf)
  1. "The time rate for wages … should in all cases be paid."
  2. "Overtime and nightshift to be paid on the same conditions as already prevail in each workshop."
  3. "A time limit, after it has been established, should only be changed if the method or means of manufacture are changed."
  4. "No firm should establish the Bonus System without intending to adhere to it."
  - Weekes adds: under the 1898 Terms of Settlement, employers were entitled to introduce any system of payment they desired.
  - In 1909 the TUC set up a committee to co-ordinate opposition to the premium bonus.
- **Women's wages in the Birmingham cycle trade, c. 1905 (flagged: Birmingham).** — [Cadbury et al. 1906, pp. 121, 128, 313](https://archive.org/details/womensworkwagesp00cadbrich)
  - Average for women over 21: **10s 6d** a week.
  - Married women averaged **11s**. "Nearly 50 per cent" of the over-21s were married, and married women "always get more on piece-work".
  - Ages 17–21: **9s 2d**. Under 17: **7s 1d**. The "1d" is uncertain in the OCR.
- **Process wages in the cycle trade (same source, Appendix table; columns: minimum / maximum / average under 18 / average 18 and over / married women).**

  | Process | Minimum | Maximum | Avg under 18 | Avg 18+ | Married women |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Press work | 4s 6d | 16s 10d | 8s 3d | 10s 0d | – |
  | Polishing | 6s 0d | 16s 0d | 8s 0d | 10s 0d | 10s 8d |
  | Enamelling and japanning | 4s 6d | 16s 0d | 5s 6d | 10s 4d | 11s 0d |

  - Other rows in the table are hard to read in the OCR: valves, testing, hub turning, wheel webbing, packing, lathe work, drilling, plating.
  - The authors estimated that "at least four weeks must be reckoned for short time" per year.
  - — [Cadbury et al. 1906, p. 313](https://archive.org/details/womensworkwagesp00cadbrich)
- **Payment day and pay tins (Swindon, flagged).** Pay-day was Friday. — [Williams 1915, ch. "Pay-day"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
  - "each man is furnished with a metal check having a number, corresponding with his name in the register … issued to the men as they enter the shed after dinner."
  - "Each man's wages are put up in a tin box, which is also stamped with his number. The foreman takes his position at the head, and two clerks stand behind the table … one calls out the number upon the box and the other takes it and claps it sharply on the table … two hundred may be paid in about five minutes."
  - "Extras for piecework are paid fortnightly. Whatever stoppages and contributions are due for the local Sick and Medical Fund, coal, wood, and other charges, are deducted on the normal week, and this is called 'stoppage week'"; the other week was "balance week".
- **Deductions allowed by law in 1913.**
  - National Insurance: the employer paid the full stamp and recovered the worker's share "by means of deductions from the wages … and not otherwise". The deduction could only come from wages for the same period. "the employer shall not be entitled to deduct from the wages … the employer's contribution." — [NI Act 1911, Third Schedule](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
  - Truck Act 1896: deductions for **bad work or damaged materials** and for **materials, tools, standing room, light or heat** needed a posted notice or signed contract and written particulars on each occasion. The amounts could not exceed the actual loss or cost, or a "fair and reasonable" charge. — [Bowstead 1901, App. B, Truck Act 1896 ss. 2–3](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
  - The Truck Act 1831 is printed in the same appendix; it required wages to be paid in current coin. — [Bowstead 1901, App. B](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)

### Inferences
- **Weekly wages implied by the documented rates, in pre-decimal money (12d = 1s):**

  | Grade | Rate | Hours | Weekly wage |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Labourer | 6d | 54 | 27s 0d |
  | Machine hand | 7d | 53 | 30s 11d |
  | Skilled production worker | 8d | 53 | 35s 4d |
  | Fitter or turner | 8½d | 53 | 37s 6½d (recorded as 38s) |
  | Toolroom worker | 9d | 53 | 39s 9d |
  | Patternmaker | 10¼d | 53 | 45s 3¼d |

  - Pieceworkers would typically earn 15–33% over their time rate. This uplift is my assumption; Carr says only that they earned "higher pay".
- **Rough rates for trades not named in Coventry sources:**
  - brazers, platers, enamellers and wheel-builders (men): machine-hand to skilled-production range, about 7–8d/hr plus piece;
  - women (Coventry, 1913): perhaps 12–15s a week, inferred from the Birmingham 1905 figures plus Coventry's high-wage premium;
  - boys of 14–16: perhaps 6–10s a week.
  - All of these are estimates.
- Coventry pay day was probably Friday or Saturday. A numbered tin or paper packet handed out at a pay window or table by works number is the likely method. Whether wages were held a "week in hand" in Coventry is unknown.

### Gaps
- **Clerks, foremen and managers:** no salary data was found. Only the London 1889 rule of chargemen at least 2s a week above the rate is documented.
- I found no **Coventry-specific women's or boys' rates** for 1913, and no explicit Coventry rates for brazers, enamellers, polishers, platers, wheel-builders or assemblers.
- I could not access the **Board of Trade 1906 Earnings and Hours Inquiry** (metal and engineering volume, which includes cycles) or the **Standard Time Rates 1913**.
- I found no evidence on whether any Coventry cycle firm used the Rowan or Halsey system in 1913. Daimler's 1907 premium bonus is the only Coventry case found.

## 5. Women and juveniles in the Coventry cycle trade

### Takeaway
Cycle-making was the **largest manufacturing employer of women in Coventry** in 1911: 1,476 women, against 11,988 men in cycle, motor and mechanic occupations. Women did light press work, polishing, enamelling, assembly and similar jobs at roughly half to two-thirds of male pay, and sometimes displaced men outright. Under-18s were protected by the Factory Act hours rules, and under-16s needed a surgeon's certificate. Children under 12 were barred, and children under 14 could work only half-time.

### Cited Findings
- **Coventry census of 1911** (occupied persons aged 10 and over). — [Carr 1978, pp. 16–17](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - Males: 37,322 occupied. "Cycle makers, motor car makers and mechanics" 11,988; general engineering and machine making 5,602; toolmakers 1,490; commercial clerks 1,584.
  - Females: 13,060 occupied. Textile manufacture 2,844; domestic indoor servants 1,741; **cyclemaking 1,476**; commercial and other clerks 963.
  - Carr: the table "shows the importance of cycle production to female workers".
- **Coventry's share of the cycle industry.** It fell "from 49 per cent in 1891 to 26 per cent in 1911, as the motor industry took a permanent hold". — [Carr 1978, p. 9](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Women organised slowly.** "the majority of women workers in the city … remained untouched by trade unionism before 1914." In 1913 the Workers' Union women's branch (founded 1912) had 110 affiliated members and the National Federation of Women Workers 450. — [Carr 1978, pp. 38–39](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Women displacing men (Birmingham, flagged).** "In one section of a large cycle works the men employed in 1902 numbered 80; in 1905 men employed numbered 20, women having displaced the rest. The wages of the men were from 30s. to 40s. per week. The highest wages of women on identically the same machines and doing the same work is 18s … When the men were dismissed they were told to go home and send their wives back in their places." — [Cadbury et al. 1906, p. 130 n.](https://archive.org/details/womensworkwagesp00cadbrich)
  - The authors also found a case "in the Cycle trade where a woman displaced her own husband at half his wage". — [Cadbury et al. 1906, p. 133](https://archive.org/details/womensworkwagesp00cadbrich)
- **Busy-season working (Birmingham, flagged).** Some cycle-trade employers opposed limits on hours because, "during the busy season, men and youths have to do the women's work after the women have left". — [Cadbury et al. 1906, p. 38](https://archive.org/details/womensworkwagesp00cadbrich)
- **Legal age categories** (FWA 1901). — [Bowstead 1901, pp. 79–80, 41](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
  - "A child becomes a young person at the age of 14 years, or, being between the age of 13 and 14 years, when he obtains the certificate of proficiency or attendance at school". A young person is under 18.
  - "A child under the age of twelve years must not be employed in a factory or workshop" (s. 62).
  - Children (12–14) could be employed only in morning or afternoon "sets" or on alternate days (s. 27). They could not work more than 5 hours without a ½-hour meal.
- **Certificates of fitness** (s. 63). In a factory "a young person under the age of sixteen years or a child must not be employed for more than seven, or if the certifying surgeon … resides more than three miles from the factory thirteen, work days, unless the occupier … has obtained a certificate … of the fitness". The certificate had to be produced to an inspector on demand. — [Bowstead 1901, pp. 79–80](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
  - The surgeon's fee, where none was agreed (Fifth Schedule), was **2s 6d per visit plus 6d per person after the first five** for examination at the factory, plus 6d per extra half mile beyond one mile. Examination at the surgeon's residence or an appointed place cost 6d per person. — [Bowstead 1901, Fifth Schedule](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
  - A certified copy of a birth entry to prove age cost 6d (s. 134). — [Bowstead 1901, pp. 163–164](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
- **Childbirth.** No woman could knowingly be employed "within four weeks after she has given birth to a child" (s. 61). — [Bowstead 1901, p. 79](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
- **Juveniles and National Insurance.**
  - Health insurance began at 16.
  - Unemployment contributions for workmen under 18 were 1d (worker) plus 1d (employer). No unemployment benefit was paid under 17, and only half rate from 17 to 18.
  - Indentured apprentices were excluded from Part II.
  - — [NI Act 1911, s. 1, s. 107, Seventh and Eighth Schedules](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)

### Inferences
- Women made up roughly 11% of Coventry's combined cycle and motor workforce in 1911 (1,476 against 11,988). The male category includes motor workers, so the female share *within cycle-making alone* was higher, perhaps 20–30%. This estimate is mine and unverified.
- **Suggested women's jobs for the simulation:** press work, polishing, enamelling and japanning (with lead risk), hub and bearing assembly, wheel-webbing (lacing), valve and fitting assembly, testing, packing and wrapping, lathe and drilling hands. Men remained on brazing, frame-building, heavy turning, toolmaking and labouring. This draws on the Birmingham process list; the Coventry division of labour is not documented.
- Boys of 14 and over (school-leavers) are likely as errand boys, machine minders and apprentices. Each would need a surgeon's certificate, an entry in the general register, and (after 16) an insurance card.

### Gaps
- I found no direct Coventry source on women's or girls' tasks or wages in cycle works in 1913, and no proportion of women in a named Coventry cycle firm.
- I found no evidence of Coventry cycle firms employing half-time children (12–14) in 1913.

## 6. Law: the Factory and Workshop Act, Workmen's Compensation, National Insurance, Truck, Shop Clubs, Trade Disputes

### Takeaway
By 1913 a cycle works carried a dense set of legal paperwork:
- **under the Factory and Workshop Act 1901:** a posted abstract of the Act, the inspector's and certifying surgeon's names, the clock notice, the hours and meals notice and the general register;
- **under the Notice of Accidents Act 1906:** written accident notices to the inspector;
- **under the Workmen's Compensation Act 1906:** no-fault compensation of up to half wages, with a £1 a week cap;
- **under the National Insurance Act 1911, Part I:** health stamps of 7d (men) or 6d (women) per week, from 15 July 1912;
- **under the National Insurance Act 1911, Part II:** unemployment stamps of 2½d + 2½d, if the work counted as an insured trade;
- **under the Truck Acts:** a register of fines.

### Cited Findings

#### Factory and Workshop Act 1901 (in force 1902)
- **Posted documents (s. 128).** "There shall be affixed at the entrance of every factory and workshop … and be constantly kept so affixed …":
  - "(a) The prescribed abstract of this Act;
  - (b) a notice of the name and address of the prescribed inspector;
  - (c) A notice of the name and address of the certifying surgeon …;
  - (d) a notice of the clock (if any) by which the period of employment and times for meals … are regulated;
  - (e) Every notice and document required by this Act to be affixed."
  - The penalty for breach was a fine not exceeding 40s. — [Bowstead 1901, pp. 160–161](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
- **The general register (s. 129)** recorded:
  - children and young persons employed;
  - lime-washing;
  - every notifiable accident;
  - every special exception used.
  - It was open to the certifying surgeon. Extracts went to the inspector on request. The fine for default was up to £5. — [Bowstead 1901, pp. 161–162](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
- **Returns of persons employed (s. 130).** Occupiers sent periodic returns to the Chief Inspector, every one to three years, giving the number of persons employed by age, sex and occupation. The fine for default was up to £10. — [Bowstead 1901, p. 162](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
- **Fencing (s. 10).** "Every hoist or teagle, and every fly-wheel directly connected with the steam or water or other mechanical power … and every part of any … engine worked by any such power, must be securely fenced". "All dangerous parts of the machinery, and every part of the mill gearing, must either be securely fenced, or be in such position or of such construction as to be equally safe". — [Bowstead 1901, s. 10](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)
- **Hours, meals and holidays** for women and young persons (ss. 26, 27, 32, 35): see sections 1 and 5 above. — [Bowstead 1901, pp. 38–48](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)

#### Accident reporting: the Notice of Accidents Act 1906, in force from 1 January 1907, which replaced FWA s. 19
Written notice "in such form and accompanied by such particulars as the Secretary of State prescribes, shall forthwith be sent to the inspector of the district" for any of the following accidents (s. 4):
- (a) fatal accidents;
- (b) accidents "due to any machinery moved by mechanical power, or to molten metal, hot liquid, explosion, escape of gas or steam, or to electricity", disabling a worker "as to cause him to be absent throughout at least one whole day from his ordinary work";
- (c) special causes named by order;
- (d) any accident "disabling for more than seven days a person … from working at his ordinary work".

Further rules:
- Cases (a) and (b) were also notified to the **certifying surgeon**.
- A later death had to be notified.
- The fine was up to £10.
- — [legislation.gov.uk, Notice of Accidents Act 1906, s. 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/6/53/enacted)
- The original 1901 threshold, injury preventing 5 hours' work on any of the next 3 working days, was superseded. — [Bowstead 1901, p. 30](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)

#### Workmen's Compensation Act 1906 (in force 1907)
- **Who is covered.** The employer is liable for "personal injury by accident arising out of and in the course of the employment". — [Willis 1913](https://archive.org/details/workmenscompensa00willrich)
  - "Workman" excludes non-manual employees paid over **£250 a year**. — [Willis 1913](https://archive.org/details/workmenscompensa00willrich)
  - Injury "attributable to the serious and wilful misconduct of that workman" bars compensation, "unless the injury results in death or serious and permanent disablement". — [Willis 1913, s. 1(2)(c)](https://archive.org/details/workmenscompensa00willrich)
- **Notice and claim (s. 2).** Notice of the accident "as soon as practicable after the happening thereof and before the workman has voluntarily left the employment"; the claim "within six months". — [Willis 1913, s. 2](https://archive.org/details/workmenscompensa00willrich)
- **Compensation scale (First Schedule).** — [Willis 1913, First Schedule](https://archive.org/details/workmenscompensa00willrich)
  - **Incapacity:** a weekly payment "not exceeding fifty per cent of his average weekly earnings during the previous twelve months … such weekly payment not to exceed one pound".
  - "if the incapacity lasts less than two weeks no compensation shall be payable in respect of the first week."
  - For a worker **under 21 earning under 20s a week**, 100% of earnings, capped at **10s**.
  - **Death, with wholly dependent dependants:** three years' earnings or £150, whichever is larger, capped at **£300**. The earnings are deemed to be 156 × average weekly earnings if employed less than three years.
  - **Death, no dependants:** medical and burial expenses up to **£10**.
- **Medical referees and industrial disease.** Medical referees decided disputed medical questions. Section 8 covered scheduled industrial diseases; the case notes include lead poisoning claims. — [Willis 1913](https://archive.org/details/workmenscompensa00willrich)

#### National Insurance Act 1911, Part I (health)
- **Contributions (Second Schedule).** — [NI Act 1911, Second Schedule](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
  - Men: 7d a week, of which the employer pays 3d and the worker 4d.
  - Women: 6d a week, of which the employer pays 3d and the worker 3d.
  - Low-paid adults (21 and over, no board or lodging):

    | Daily wage | Employer (men / women) | Worker | State |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | 1s 6d or less | 6d / 5d | nothing | 1d |
    | 1s 6d to 2s | 5d / 4d | 1d | 1d |
    | 2s to 2s 6d | 4d / 3d | 3d | – |
- **The contribution week (Third Schedule).** One contribution was due per calendar week, "from midnight on one Sunday to midnight on the following Sunday". It fell on the first employer in that week. None was due for a week with no work and no pay, or a week on sickness benefit. — [NI Act 1911, Third Schedule](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
- **Benefits.** — [NI Act 1911, s. 8, Fourth Schedule](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
  - Sickness benefit "from the fourth day": **10s a week** for men and **7s 6d** for women, for up to 26 weeks.
  - Disablement benefit 5s.
  - Maternity benefit **30s**.
  - Lower rates for unmarried minors: males 6s then 5s; females 5s then 4s.
  - Married women: 5s then 3s.
  - Medical benefit (panel doctor) and sanatorium benefit.
- **Approved societies.** Benefits were administered through "approved societies" (friendly societies, trade unions, insurance companies' societies) chosen by the worker. — [Wikipedia, National Insurance Act 1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Insurance_Act_1911)
- **Commencement.** The Act's commencement date was 15 July 1912. "Collection of contributions began in July 1912, and payments on 15 January 1913." — [Wikipedia, National Insurance Act 1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Insurance_Act_1911)

#### National Insurance Act 1911, Part II (unemployment)
- **Insured trades (Sixth Schedule).** — [NI Act 1911, Sixth Schedule](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
  1. building;
  2. construction of works;
  3. shipbuilding;
  4. "**Mechanical engineering**, including the manufacture of ordnance and firearms";
  5. "Ironfounding";
  6. "**Construction of vehicles**; that is to say, the construction, repair, or decoration of vehicles";
  7. sawmilling.
- **The umpire's demarcation.** In May 1912 the umpire decided "that contributions are not payable by persons engaged in the manufacture of rubber tyres for motor cycles and other vehicles". Ten demarcation applications had been listed in the *Labour Gazette*, April 1912. — [Hansard, written answer, 9 May 1912](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1912/may/09/unemployment-umpire)
- **Contributions (Eighth Schedule).** — [NI Act 1911, Eighth Schedule](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
  - 2½d from the workman and 2½d from the employer per week. Under 18, 1d each.
  - Employment of 1 day or less: 1d each. Up to 2 days: 2d each.
  - Parliament added one-third of the combined contributions (s. 85(6)).
- **Benefit (Seventh Schedule).** — [NI Act 1911, Seventh Schedule](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
  - **7s a week** from the second week of unemployment.
  - At most 15 weeks in any 12 months, and one week of benefit per five contributions.
  - Conditions: employed in an insured trade in at least 26 separate weeks in the preceding five years; application in the prescribed manner; capable of work.
- **Refunds.** — [NI Act 1911, ss. 94–96](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
  - Employers recovered **one-third** of their own contributions for any workman "continuously in his service" for the year with at least 45 contributions (s. 94).
  - Workmen with 500 or more contributions were refunded, with interest, at **age 60**, any excess of contributions over benefit drawn (s. 95).
  - Employers who absorbed workers' contributions during systematic short time could claim them back (s. 96).
- **Coverage.** By 1913 about 2.3 million workers were insured for unemployment and almost 15 million for sickness. — [Wikipedia, National Insurance Act 1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Insurance_Act_1911)

#### Truck Acts 1831–1896
- See section 2 (fines) and section 4 (deductions). Fines and deductions needed a posted notice or signed contract, written particulars each time, and a register of fines open to the inspector. — [Bowstead 1901, App. B](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)

#### Shop Clubs Act 1902
- It became "illegal for an employer to require a worker to resign his membership in a friendly society as a condition of employment, or to prohibit him from joining a friendly society other than the workplace shop club". — [Wikipedia, Shop Clubs Act 1902](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shop_Clubs_Act_1902)
  - Compulsory membership of a shop club or thrift fund was allowed only if it was registered under the Friendly Societies Act 1896 and certified.
  - Certification required substantial benefit to members, an employer contribution and a permanent character.
  - At least three-quarters of the employees had to want the club.
  - A leaver could stay a member or take back an appropriate share of the funds.

#### Trade Disputes Act 1906
- Unions "could not be sued for damages resulting from industrial action". It added the rule that an act done "in contemplation or furtherance of a trade dispute" by a combination is not actionable unless it would be actionable if done by one person. It reversed *Taff Vale* (1901). — [Wikipedia, Trade Disputes Act 1906](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Disputes_Act_1906)

### Inferences
- **A paperwork set per worker** that a simulation could model:
  - **Wages book:** works number, name and rate.
  - **Brass check.**
  - **Health insurance card**, with a weekly 7d or 6d stamp and approved society membership. Men and women aged 16 and over.
  - **Unemployment book**, with a 5d stamp (2½d + 2½d), or 2d for under-18s. Manual workmen aged 16 and over in insured trades.
  - **Certificate of fitness**, for under-16s.
  - **General register entries**, for under-18s and accidents.
  - **Accident notice**, sent to the inspector (and to the surgeon for machinery accidents).
  - **Workmen's Compensation claim and weekly payment record.**
  - **Fines register.**
- **Was a cycle works an insured trade under Part II?** The plain words of the Sixth Schedule, "construction of vehicles" and "mechanical engineering", suggest that frame-building and the machine shops of a cycle works were insured. The umpire, however, demarcated borderline sub-trades: rubber tyres for motor cycles were held not insured, so component and accessory work may have fallen outside. The safest simulation design treats the machine and fitting shops as insured and flags packing, warehouse and tyre work as uncertain. This is inference.
- The Workmen's Compensation Act did not itself make insurance compulsory. Employers usually insured with an insurance company or a mutual, but I did not find a source stating this (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- I did not find an explicit umpire or Board of Trade decision on whether **cycle manufacture** was within "construction of vehicles" in 1912–14. The National Archives' PIN 29 umpire records and the *Labour Gazette* would settle it.
- I did not obtain the prescribed **form of the Factory Act abstract** or of accident Form 43, nor the frequency of **factory inspectors'** visits to Coventry (Chief Inspector's Annual Reports 1912–13 not accessed).
- I did not research the FWA **sanitary provisions** (sanitary conveniences, ventilation, temperature, lime-washing every 14 months).
- I did not verify a primary source on **WCA employer insurance** practice.
- I found no specific text for the **Employment of Children Act 1903**. It mainly empowered local by-laws on children's employment outside factories and is of limited relevance to the works itself.

## 7. Organised labour in Coventry engineering, 1912–1914

### Takeaway
Coventry engineering in 1913 had a strong craft-union core: the ASE and the Toolmakers (about 2,600–2,700 members each), plus smaller craft societies. It also had a fast-growing **Workers' Union** for the semi-skilled and unskilled, which won the 6d labourers' rate and recognition from the CDEEA in May 1912 and had over 3,000 members by 1914. Shop stewards existed in embryo, mainly as dues collectors. The 1913 Black Country strikes for a 23s minimum were led by the same union.

### Cited Findings
- **The Trades Council.** Affiliated membership grew from under 3,000 in 22 branches (1907) to 7,491 (1911) and **12,847 in 64 branches (1913)**. About 8,500 of the 12,800 came "from the vehicle, engineering, and metal industries". — [Carr 1978, p. 40](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Union membership in Coventry, 1913** (Trades Council Annual Report). — [Carr 1978, pp. 27, 36–40](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)

  |---|---|
  | Amalgamated Society of Engineers (ASE) | 2,670 |
  | Amalgamated Society of Toolmakers (AST) | 2,634 (1,366 affiliated to the Trades Council) |
  | Workers' Union (6 branches) | 2,166 affiliated |
  | United Kingdom Society of Coachmakers | 800 (its largest branch) |
  | Brassworkers and Metal Mechanics | 590 |
  | Steam Engine Makers | 400 |
  | United Machine Workers' Association | 400 |
  | Birmingham Operative Tinplate and Sheet Metal Workers | 400 |
  | National Union of Railwaymen | 370 |

  | National Federation of Women Workers | 450 |

  - The Workers' Union's finances suggest "over 2,000 new members joined in that year". By August 1914 it had "over 3,000 paid up members, and was the largest union in the city".
- **Craft attitudes.** The ASE and AST both organised fitters and turners. "Both were very conscious of their craft status, and their officials tended to be conservative." — [Carr 1978, p. 26](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Employers.** The employers' body was the **Coventry and District Engineering Employers' Association (CDEEA)**. It signed the 1910 working-conditions agreement and recognised the Workers' Union in 1912. — [Carr 1978, pp. 26, 33](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **The Workers' Union.** It was founded in 1898, with a Coventry branch from October 1899. It had about £90 income in 1907 ("a couple of hundred paid up members"), 4 branches by the end of 1912 (one for women), and recognition by the CDEEA in 1912. — [Carr 1978, pp. 31–33](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - The ASE's Ryder in 1913 described joining as "a shilling entrance fee and threepence a week contribution". — [Carr 1978, p. 35](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - National membership was 18,000 at the end of 1911, **91,000 at the end of 1913** and 143,000 by mid-1914. The growth came on "the recruitment of large numbers of semi-skilled engineering workers in the West Midlands" by John Beard, Arthur Ellery and Julia Varley. — [Wikipedia, Workers' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers%27_Union)
- **The 1913 strikes.** "in 1913, the Workers' Union was in the forefront of the Black Country strikes, where the demand was for a weekly rate of 23/- for unskilled workers." Coventry activists helped. The ASE's Ryder attacked the strikers: "The Workers' Union is not so much directing the strikes as following them, and is making members by the thousand." — [Carr 1978, pp. 34–35](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - In **December 1913** a Workers' Union strike at Courtaulds (Foleshill, about 2,500 workers, mostly women) began over a supervisor's behaviour and became a recognition dispute. Over 600 stayed out for more than a month and lost. — [Carr 1978, p. 37](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Shop stewards.** Before the war ASE stewards in Coventry "were little more than collectors of union dues, but they gradually extended their activities to holding a watching brief … on working conditions". — [Carr 1978, pp. 28–29](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - A 1907 survey found 21 stewards in the district, of whom 14 were active. Four were at the Coventry Ordnance Works.
  - In July 1907 the ASE tried a system of "one steward for each department or one for about 50 members" at the Humber New Works and the Ordnance, without lasting success.
  - "Advanced factories had set up stewards' or works' committees before the War".
  - In 1907 the craft unions used a local Federation of Engineering Unions (ten unions) and overtime bans as weapons.
- **The premium bonus as a union issue.** The Birmingham Operative Tinplate and Sheet Metal Workers "had been the only one to fight the premium bonus system at the Daimler in a long and ultimately unsuccessful strike in 1907". — [Carr 1978, p. 40](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - In 1909 the TUC set up a committee to co-ordinate opposition to the premium bonus. — [Weekes 1970](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/55860/1/WRAP_thesis_Weekes_1970.pdf)
- **Law on strikes.** The Trade Disputes Act 1906 protected union funds from tort actions over strikes. — [Wikipedia, Trade Disputes Act 1906](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Disputes_Act_1906)
  - Strikers were disqualified from unemployment benefit during a stoppage at their own works. — [NI Act 1911, s. 87](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)

### Inferences
- **Union membership in a fictional 1913 cycle works of a few hundred hands:**
  - the toolroom and skilled fitters largely ASE or AST;
  - brass and plating workers possibly Brassworkers' society;
  - machine hands and labourers partly Workers' Union (recently recruited, turnover high);
  - women largely non-union (a few in the Workers' Union women's branch or the NFWW);
  - clerks possibly National Union of Clerks.
- One or two ASE "stewards" would collect dues rather than negotiate.

### Gaps
- Richard Hyman's *The Workers' Union* (1971), Richardson's *Twentieth-Century Coventry* and Thoms and Donnelly were not accessible online. Details of any 1913 strikes **at Coventry cycle firms specifically** were not found.
- I did not establish the CDEEA's membership list or its formal relationship to the national EEF.

## 8. Welfare: sick clubs, benefit funds, outings, canteens, first aid, apprenticeships

### Takeaway
Pre-1914 works welfare consisted mainly of:
- **sick and medical funds** or shop clubs, with deductions from pay (regulated by the Shop Clubs Act 1902);
- the new state health insurance from 1912;
- occasional **paternalist outings and parties**.

Mess rooms, canteens and first aid were rudimentary and are poorly documented for Coventry.

### Cited Findings
- **Sick fund deductions (Swindon, flagged).** Deductions for "the local Sick and Medical Fund, coal, wood, and other charges" were taken fortnightly in the "stoppage week". — [Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
- **The legal frame for works clubs.** Under the Shop Clubs Act 1902, compulsory works clubs were lawful only if registered and certified, with an employer contribution and the consent of three-quarters of the workforce. — [Wikipedia, Shop Clubs Act 1902](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shop_Clubs_Act_1902)
- **Extra benefits under National Insurance.** From 1912, approved societies could offer "additional benefits" from any surplus (NI Act, Fourth Schedule Part II). These included dental treatment, convalescent homes, sickness benefit from the first day, increased maternity benefit, and "payments to members not allowed to attend work on account of infection". — [NI Act 1911, Fourth Schedule](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
- **Paternalism in Coventry.**
  - Thomas Smith moved his forging works to Red Lane, Coventry, in 1896. An employee recalled men and lads fishing along the canal on summer evenings and "our happy family atmosphere … Mr. Smith was like a father to us". — [Carr 1978, p. 5](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
  - Carr: "Eventually, large-scale production destroyed paternalism." — [Carr 1978, p. 5](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Works party (Coventry, date not given; pre-war).** The Daimler company gave a party "in honour of its newly-patented engine, the 'Silent Knight'", at the Drill Hall for "Two thousand hands or more". It is satirised in verse in the *Coventry Sentinel*. — [Carr 1978, p. 31](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Works outings (Swindon, flagged).** The annual unpaid "Trip" was organised by the works, with special trains. "Every year five or six thousand are conveyed to" Weymouth. — [Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)
- **Supervisors as a grievance.** Unions campaigned against "the tyrannies of some of the foremen and foremistresses" (NFWW speech, Coventry, c. 1912). — [Carr 1978, p. 38](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)
- **Apprentices.** Apprenticeship is recognised in law: health insurance covers "any contract of service or apprenticeship", while indentured apprentices are excluded from unemployment insurance. — [NI Act 1911, First Schedule; s. 107](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)
  - Carr's thesis has a section "Apprentices and Machines" (p. 189), but it covers the inter-war period and was not consulted. — [Carr 1978, contents](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Welfare provision for a simulated 1913 Coventry cycle works:**
  - a works sick club or benefit society, voluntary or certified under the 1902 Act, with a weekly deduction of a few pence;
  - state health insurance on top;
  - an annual works outing or dinner;
  - a first-aid box and a man trained by the St John Ambulance in the larger shops;
  - perhaps a mess room where workers could warm food.
- These are plausible but unsourced for Coventry. Canteens became widespread only during the war, under the munitions welfare drive. That last point comes from general knowledge and is not sourced here.

### Gaps
- I found no Coventry 1913 sources on canteens or mess rooms, first-aid provision, named works sick clubs (for example at Rudge-Whitworth, Triumph, Singer, Humber or Swift), apprenticeship indentures, terms or premiums, or works outings.
- The Modern Records Centre and Coventry Archives company collections were not searched in depth.
