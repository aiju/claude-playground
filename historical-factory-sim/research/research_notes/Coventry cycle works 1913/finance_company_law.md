# Finance, company law, bookkeeping, banking, tax and insurance of a mid-sized Coventry cycle company, c. 1913

Scope note: the target is a fictional Coventry cycle company with roughly 300–500 workers in 1913. Statute texts are quoted from the enacted versions. The accountancy manuals are editions from 1893 to 1912 and are flagged by date. Anything marked **(estimate)** or placed under "Inferences" is my own reasoning, not a documented fact. Several downloads (legislation XML, archive.org full texts, Harrison's 1977 PhD thesis) were read in full text, so the quotations are exact apart from OCR errors.

## 1. Company law under the Companies (Consolidation) Act 1908: obligations of private vs public limited companies, statutory books, the secretary

### Takeaway
The 1908 Act, in force through 1913, required every limited company to have a registered office and to show its name outside every place of business, on its seal and on all cheques, invoices and letters. It also required a register of members, an annual list and summary sent to the Registrar, minute books, a register of directors, a register of mortgages and charges, an AGM every calendar year (no more than 15 months apart), auditors appointed at each AGM, and an auditors' report on the balance sheet. A **private company** (one that restricts share transfers, has no more than 50 members excluding employees, and makes no public issue) did **not** have to file a balance sheet with its annual summary. A public company did. That is the main practical difference for a mid-sized family-controlled Coventry firm.

### Cited Findings
**Definition of a private company**
- s.121: a "private company" is one whose articles "(a) restricts the right to transfer its shares; and (b) limits the number of its members (exclusive of persons who are in the employment of the company) to fifty; and (c) prohibits any invitation to the public to subscribe for any shares or debentures". It can convert to a public company by special resolution and by filing a statement in lieu of prospectus. — [CA 1908 s.121](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/121/enacted)
- Coventry cycle firms did go private in this period. Singer & Co. "1909 Became a private company" — [Grace's Guide: Singer and Co](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Singer_and_Co). Raleigh (Nottingham) "went private" in 1908 after a financial crisis, when Frank Bowden took complete ownership — [Grace's Guide: Raleigh Cycle Co](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Raleigh_Cycle_Co).

**Registered office and nameplate**
- s.62: "Every company shall have a registered office to which all communications and notices may be addressed". The Registrar must be notified of its location and of any change. The fine is up to £5 for each day the company trades without complying. — [CA 1908 s.62](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/62/enacted)
- s.63: every limited company must "paint or affix, and keep painted or affixed, its name on the outside of every office or place in which its business is carried on, in a conspicuous position, in letters easily legible". The name must be engraved on its seal. It must appear on "all bills of exchange, promissory notes, endorsements, cheques, and orders for money or goods … and in all bills of parcels, invoices, receipts, and letters of credit". Penalties: up to £5 a day for a missing nameplate. Up to £50 for an officer who signs a cheque or bill without the name, and that officer is also personally liable for the amount unless the company pays. — [CA 1908 s.63](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/63/enacted)

**Register of members, inspection, share numbering**
- s.25: every company keeps "in one or more books a register of its members". It records names, addresses and occupations, the shares held by each member ("distinguishing each share by its number"), the amount paid up, the date the person was entered, and the date they ceased to be a member. Fine up to £5 a day. — [CA 1908 s.25](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/25/enacted)
- s.22: shares are personal estate, transferable "in manner provided by the articles", and "Each share … shall be distinguished by its appropriate number". — [CA 1908 s.22](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/22/enacted)
- s.30: the register is kept at the registered office. During business hours (at least two hours a day) it is open free to members and to anyone else for 1s per inspection. Copies cost 6d per hundred words. Fine £2, plus £2 a day while a refusal continues. — [CA 1908 s.30](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/30/enacted)

**Annual list and summary (the "annual return")**
- s.26(1)–(2): "once at least in every year", a list of all persons who are members on the 14th day after the first ordinary general meeting of the year, and of everyone who has ceased to be a member since the last return. It must give names, addresses, occupations, shareholdings and transfers. It also needs a summary: nominal capital and number of shares, shares taken up, amount called per share, calls received and unpaid, commissions or discounts paid, shares forfeited, share warrants, names and addresses of directors, and "the total amount of debt due from the company in respect of all mortgages and charges" that must be registered. — [CA 1908 s.26](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/26/enacted)
- s.26(3): "The summary must also (**except where the company is a private company**) include a statement … in the form of a balance sheet, audited by the company's auditors, and containing a summary of its share capital, its liabilities, and its assets … and how the values of the fixed assets have been arrived at, **but the balance sheet need not include a statement of profit and loss**." — [CA 1908 s.26](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/26/enacted)
- s.26(4)–(5): the list and summary go in a separate part of the register of members and must be completed "within seven days after the fourteenth day aforesaid". The company must "forthwith forward to the registrar of companies a copy signed by the manager or by the secretary". Default costs up to £5 a day. — [CA 1908 s.26](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/26/enacted)

**Meetings and minutes**
- s.64: "A general meeting of every company shall be held once at the least in every calendar year, and not more than fifteen months after the holding of the last preceding general meeting". Fine up to £50 on the company and on officers who knowingly default. Any member can ask the court to call the meeting. — [CA 1908 s.64](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/64/enacted)
- s.65: a company limited by shares registered on or after 1 January 1901 must hold a "statutory meeting" between one and three months after it is entitled to commence business. Members receive a statutory report, certified by two directors, at least seven days beforehand. The report covers shares allotted, cash received, an abstract of capital receipts and payments, and preliminary expenses. — [CA 1908 s.65](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/65/enacted)
- s.71: "Every company shall cause minutes of all proceedings of general meetings and … of its directors or managers to be entered in books kept for that purpose". A minute signed by the chairman is evidence of the proceedings. — [CA 1908 s.71](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/71/enacted)
- Table A, the default articles: at least seven days' notice of meetings (art. 49). Quorum is "three members personally present" (art. 51). Business at an ordinary meeting other than accounts, dividends, election of directors and auditors is "special" (art. 50). — [CA 1908 First Schedule, Table A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/schedule/FIRST/enacted)

**Directors, register of directors, seal**
- s.75: "Every company shall keep at its registered office a register containing the names and addresses and the occupations of its directors or managers". A copy goes to the Registrar, who must be told of any changes. Fine up to £5 a day. — [CA 1908 s.75](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/75/enacted)
- Table A: a director's qualification is "at least one share" (art. 70). Directors' pay is set by the general meeting (art. 69). The seal may be used only by board resolution and "in the presence of at least two directors and of the secretary", and those present sign (art. 76). — [Table A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/schedule/FIRST/enacted)

**Mortgages, charges and debentures**
- s.93: any mortgage or charge created after 1 July 1908 must be registered with the Registrar within 21 days or it is "void against the liquidator and any creditor". This covers charges securing debentures, charges on uncalled capital, bill-of-sale-type charges, charges on land or book debts, and floating charges. — [CA 1908 s.93](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/93/enacted)
- s.100: every limited company also keeps its own "register of mortgages", listing each charge, the property charged, the amount and the mortgagees. Fine up to £50 on an officer who knowingly omits an entry. — [CA 1908 s.100](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/100/enacted)

**Share certificates and transfers (Table A defaults)**
- Every member is entitled "without payment" to a certificate under the common seal showing the shares and the amount paid up. A replacement costs at most 1s (arts. 6–7). — [Table A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/schedule/FIRST/enacted)
- A transfer must be executed by both transferor and transferee. The transferor remains the holder until the transferee is entered in the register. Directors may refuse transfers of partly paid shares and "may also suspend the registration of transfers during the fourteen days immediately preceding the ordinary general meeting". A transfer fee of up to 2s 6d may be charged (arts. 18–20). — [Table A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/schedule/FIRST/enacted)

**Accounts, dividends, reserves (Table A defaults)**
- "The directors shall cause true accounts to be kept" of receipts, expenditure, assets and liabilities. The books are kept at the registered office or where the directors decide. Members who are not directors have no right of inspection unless the board or the general meeting grants one (arts. 103–105). — [Table A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/schedule/FIRST/enacted)
- "Once at least in every year" the directors lay before the general meeting a profit and loss account and a balance sheet "made up to a date not more than six months before such meeting". A directors' report accompanies them, stating the recommended dividend and any transfer to reserve. Copies go to members seven days before the meeting (arts. 106–108). — [Table A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/schedule/FIRST/enacted)
- "The company in general meeting may declare dividends, but no dividend shall exceed the amount recommended by the directors". Directors may pay interim dividends. "No dividend shall be paid otherwise than out of profits". Directors may first set aside reserves "for meeting contingencies, or for equalising dividends" (arts. 95–99). — [Table A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/schedule/FIRST/enacted)

**Auditors**
- s.112: the auditor is appointed at each AGM. If none is appointed, the Board of Trade may appoint one. "A director or officer of the company shall not be capable of being appointed auditor". Shareholders proposing a new auditor must give 14 days' notice. Pay is fixed by the general meeting. — [CA 1908 s.112](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/112/enacted)
- s.113: auditors have "a right of access at all times to the books and accounts and vouchers". Their report to shareholders states "(a) whether or not they have obtained all the information and explanations they have required; and (b) whether, in their opinion, the balance sheet … is properly drawn up so as to exhibit a true and correct view of the state of the company's affairs … as shown by the books". The balance sheet is "signed on behalf of the board by two of the directors". The report is attached or referenced and read at the general meeting. Any shareholder may have a copy at up to 6d per hundred words. A balance sheet circulated unsigned or without the report draws a fine of up to £50. — [CA 1908 s.113](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/113/enacted)

**Limited liability in winding up**
- s.123: in a winding up, members of a company limited by shares contribute no more than the amount unpaid on their shares. Past members are liable only for one year after leaving and only for debts contracted while they were members. — [CA 1908 s.123](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/123/enacted)

**Which books the secretary kept (contemporary practice)**
- Dicksee, *Bookkeeping for Company Secretaries* (3rd ed., c.1904, written under the 1862–1900 Acts), lists the books "required to be kept by Companies": "Register of Members; Annual List of Members and Summary; Register of Mortgages (which must include debentures, if secured by a charge on the assets); Minute Book". Dicksee notes that "it is usual to provide that the Share Ledger shall contain such particulars as are required to be contained in the Register of Members, so as to obviate the necessity for keeping a separate register". He calls these "Statistical" books, as distinct from the double-entry "Financial" books, and includes the "Register of Transfers". His suggested internal routine has "The Minute Books to be fully entered up and kept indexed to date" and "The various books required by the Companies Acts to be written up, and the necessary returns made to the Registrar". — [Dicksee, Bookkeeping for Company Secretaries](https://archive.org/details/bookkeepingforco00dickrich)
- Dicksee's specimen manufacturing company (6th ed., 1909) runs the share side through an "Applications and Allotments Book", "Letters of Allotment", a "Share Ledger" and a "Transfer Journal" or "Register of Transfers". — [Dicksee, Bookkeeping for Accountant Students, 6th ed. 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft); [Bookkeeping for Company Secretaries](https://archive.org/details/bookkeepingforco00dickrich)
- Directories from 1914 list a named "Secretary" in many company entries. Example: Chapman's (wallpaper), a private company incorporated 1907, has "Secretary: S. G. Quilter". — [Grace's Guide, 1914 Who's Who in Business: Company C](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_C)

### Inferences
- For the simulation, the most realistic setup is a **private limited company** (for example a family firm floated privately, or a 1890s public company that later went private, like Singer in 1909). It then has no public balance-sheet filing. It still holds an AGM every calendar year, appoints chartered-accountant auditors who report on the balance sheet, sends an annual list of members and summary to the Registrar (Somerset House, London) about three weeks after the AGM, and registers any debenture or floating charge within 21 days.
- Statutory books for the secretary's office: Register of Members (often combined with the Share Ledger), Register of Transfers, Register of Directors (s.75), Register of Mortgages and Charges (s.100), Minute Books (one for general meetings, one for the board), the Seal and a seal register (common practice; Table A art. 76 implies who must attend sealing), a share certificate book with counterfoils, and a dividend list. Dividend lists and seal books are period practice I have **not** directly sourced.
- Visible objects for a 3D scene: a painted name board "THE ______ CYCLE COMPANY LIMITED" on the building (s.63); the company seal in a press, in the secretary's or board room; letterheads, invoices and cheques printed with "Limited".

### Gaps
- Exact Registrar's filing fees in 1913 (for example the fee on the annual summary) were not retrieved; the Act's fee table did not come through cleanly. Somerset House as the place of filing is from general knowledge, not from a retrieved source.
- I did not confirm whether a *private* company was exempt from the s.65 statutory meeting and statutory report in 1913.
- I found no Coventry-specific secretarial records, for example an actual cycle-company minute book.

## 2. Bookkeeping: the books of a manufacturing company, double entry, closing, final accounts, depreciation, stock-taking, audit

### Takeaway
A 1909–1913 manufacturing company kept a standard set of books. The prime-entry books were the Cash Book (with bank columns), Petty Cash Book, Purchases (Bought) Book, Sales Day Book, purchase and sales returns books, Bills Receivable and Bills Payable Books, Journal and Wages Book. These fed a ledger split into Sold, Bought and General ledgers, with the General ledger often split again into Nominal and Private. Manufacturers added stores and cost ledgers. Books were balanced through a trial balance. Final accounts were a Trading Account, a Profit & Loss Account (with an appropriation section) and a Balance Sheet in the British layout, liabilities on the left and assets on the right. Machinery was typically depreciated at **7½% on the reducing balance** (10% as an outside limit), loose tools at **25–35%**, and patterns at **25–33⅓%**. Chartered accountants audited the accounts annually, or continuously in larger concerns.

### Cited Findings
**Division of books and ledgers**
- "The first division of the Ledger is usually into three parts, viz.: (1) Sold Ledger, comprising the accounts of trade debtors; (2) Bought Ledger, comprising the accounts of trade creditors; and (3) General Ledger". Larger concerns split the Sold and Bought Ledgers alphabetically ("Sold Ledger A to E …") or by locality ("Bought Ledger, Town; … Country; … Foreign"). They split the General Ledger into a "Nominal Ledger" and a "Private Ledger (comprising Capital Account, Real Accounts, Balance Sheet, Trading and Profit and Loss Accounts, &c.)". The Sold/Bought/General (or "Private") split "is usually to be found even in comparatively small concerns", partly so that "The privacy of the General Ledger — which alone shows the result of the trading — may then be easily assured". — [Dicksee, Bookkeeping for Accountant Students, 6th ed. 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)
- The same text works through a Purchase Returns Book, Bills Receivable Book, Petty Cash Book ("a secondary Cash Book"), Wages Book, Stores Ledger and Cost Ledger. It also has a "Bought Ledger Payments Book" and self-balancing ledgers with control ("General Ledger Account") accounts in the Sold and Bought Ledgers, posted from monthly totals. Its Part III chapters are "Accounts of Manufacturers (Wages Accounts, Stores Accounts, Cost Accounts)" and "Transactions of a Manufacturing Company (Share Accounts, Allotments, Transfers, Debentures … General and Trade Ledgers … Cost Accounts, Closing the Books, Dividends)". — [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)
- Wages: time and piece summaries from the shops "are collected into the 'Wages Book'". — [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)

**Closing, final accounts, layout**
- Dicksee's specimen manufacturing company ("Bricks & Co., Limited") closes with a Trading Account, a Profit & Loss Account and a Profit & Loss Apportionment Account, then a Balance Sheet with **Assets** listed as freehold and leasehold land and buildings at cost, plant and machinery less depreciation, stores, goodwill, trade debtors, and cash (deposit at bank, current account, cash in hand). The total is £169,150. He comments that "it is not usual to publish the Trading Account" and that preparing quarterly accounts is "quite unusual in practice". — [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)
- Dividends in the books: a journal entry debits Profit & Loss Apportionment and credits "Dividend Account". Income tax "at the current rate must be deducted from all dividends paid to preference shareholders", credited to "Income Tax Account". In Dicksee's example the ordinary dividend is "free of income tax". One cheque for the net total goes into a **separate bank account** ("Bricks & Co., Lim., Dividends Account"), and individual dividend cheques are drawn on it from a list of Share Ledger balances. Debenture interest is paid net of tax in the same way. The reserve fund is created by journal entry from the apportionment account. — [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)

**Depreciation practice**
- Dicksee (3rd ed., 1912): "In the case of a normal factory, if all Machinery (save Special Machines) be classed together, a 7½ per cent. rate on the reducing balance will, as a rule, be sufficient … if the Machinery be substantially constructed and carefully worked … and kept constantly in thorough repair. Small Renewals, such as bearings … should … be treated as Repairs … A provision of 10 per cent. on the reducing balance may … be regarded as an outside limit … unless plant be of light construction, or the conditions of work unusually trying." Special machinery is to have separate accounts per machine. — [Dicksee, Depreciation, Reserves and Reserve Funds, 3rd ed. 1912](https://archive.org/details/depreciationrese00dickrich)
- Patterns and moulds "should be written off at a rate of certainly not less than 25 per cent., and more probably 33⅓ per cent., of the reducing annual balance". Patterns for a single season are written off entirely in that year. — [Dicksee 1912](https://archive.org/details/depreciationrese00dickrich)
- Price and life of a machine tool, as Dicksee illustrates: "a really first-class Screw-Cutting Lathe could not be purchased for much under £100, and … would turn out good work for 50 years", against a lighter lathe "for about £30" lasting "(say) ten years". — [Dicksee 1912](https://archive.org/details/depreciationrese00dickrich)
- Garcke & Fells (1893 edition): loose plant and tools are usually booked to a "loose tools and plant account". At year end the firm either writes it all off or values the tools and writes off "25 to 35 per cent. of the total of the book value of the loose tools and plant in use" through shop expenses. Their lease amortisation example uses 5% interest. — [Garcke & Fells, Factory Accounts, 1893](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620) (pre-1908 evidence)

**Audit practice (Dicksee, *Auditing*, 9th ed., 1912)**
- Wages: frauds in wages "are among the most frequent". The recommended control is that time or piecework is "certified by the foremen; the calculations of wages worked by one office clerk, and checked by another; the cash for the wages made up by the cashier, and the wages paid by him or his deputy in the presence of a works manager. Where possible, a cheque should always be drawn for the exact amount of wages required." The auditor might "unexpectedly put in an appearance when the wages are being paid". The Wages Book should be added, and "a week or two's wages should be taken at random, and checked". The book cites a 1902 fraud by a timekeeper and a ticket clerk using "bogus Wages Tickets", in a department whose weekly wages exceeded £10,000. — [Dicksee, Auditing, 9th ed. 1912](https://archive.org/details/practica00dickauditingrich)
- Petty cash: the auditor checks that every payment was authorised and acknowledged, the additions are correct and the balance is in hand. "Some clients have a very bad habit of making comparatively large payments through petty cash." "All receipts should be banked, no matter how trifling in amount, and a clerk in charge of cash receipts should never be in charge of cash payments." — [Dicksee, Auditing 1912](https://archive.org/details/practica00dickauditingrich)
- Bought Ledger: in a continuous audit the auditor may pass Bought Ledger statements for payment and compare the discount taken with the terms recorded in the ledger, for a special fee. Bank and petty cash balances are examined "at least once a month" in large continuous audits. — [Dicksee, Auditing 1912](https://archive.org/details/practica00dickauditingrich)

**Stock-taking and valuation**
- Fire-insurance practice treats stock destroyed by fire on the basis of "the cost of production, not including any percentage for profit", less obsolescence. — [Garcke & Fells 1893, App. D](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620) (pre-1908)
- Dicksee's audit text repeatedly ties debtors, departmental accounts and "estimated stock" to "the periodical stocktaking", with a monthly sales/purchases/estimated-stock account reconciled to the actual stocktaking in larger concerns. — [Dicksee, Auditing 1912](https://archive.org/details/practica00dickauditingrich)

### Inferences
- Simulated office books: Cash Book (cash and bank columns, plus discount columns), Petty Cash Book (imprest), Purchases Day Book, Sales Day Book, Returns Inwards and Outwards Books, Bills Receivable and Payable Books, Journal, Sold Ledgers (Town, Country, Export), Bought Ledger, Nominal Ledger, Private Ledger (kept by the secretary or a director), Wages Book, Stores Ledger, Cost Ledger, Plant Register. Statutory books as in section 1.
- Rhythm: daily postings to the day books and cash book; weekly wages; monthly ledger balancing with control accounts and a monthly statement to customers; half-yearly or yearly closing with a full trial balance; an annual stock-taking at the financial year end. For a cycle maker that would probably be late summer (August–September), after the selling season and before the November show. Harrison notes that cycle companies' financial years usually ended "August or September" (see section 6).
- Depreciation in the model: machinery 7½% reducing balance; loose tools 25–35% (or revaluation); patterns, jigs and dies 25–33⅓%; buildings 1–2½% or none on freehold land (estimate, not sourced). Repairs and small renewals go to revenue.
- Stock valuation "at cost or market price, whichever is lower" is plausible as period practice but **not verified** in what I read. Use cost, with obsolete models written down.

### Gaps
- No Garcke & Fells edition after 1893 was read; the 1911 edition exists. No Pixley text was read.
- No surviving balance sheet of a Coventry cycle company was found.
- Typical audit fees for a company of this size were not found. The texts mention fees in guineas (for example 30–60 guineas for books in one legal case) but not a norm.

## 3. Banking: banks in Coventry 1913, current account, cheques and crossings, clearing, overdrafts, wages cash, bills of exchange, stamp duties

### Takeaway
In 1913 a Coventry manufacturer would bank with **Lloyds Bank Ltd** or the **London City & Midland Bank Ltd**, the two big joint-stock banks that had absorbed Coventry's own banks. Lloyds took over the Coventry & Warwickshire Banking Co. in 1879; Midland took over the Coventry Union Banking Co. in 1889. Rudge-Whitworth banked with Lloyds. **Barclays had no meaningful Midlands presence until it bought the United Counties Bank in 1916.** Cheques carried a **1d stamp** in 1913 (raised to 2d only from 1 September 1918). Receipts for **£2 or more** needed a **1d stamp**. Bills of exchange other than on demand paid an ad valorem scale from 1d up to 1s per £100. Coventry had no clearing house of its own (Birmingham had one). Out-of-town cheques were collected through London.

### Cited Findings
**Which banks**
- Coventry & Warwickshire Banking Co. (formed 1835): "In 1879 an offer of amalgamation with Lloyds was accepted". — [Archives Hub: Coventry & Warwickshire Banking Company records](https://archiveshub.jisc.ac.uk/search/archives/a6ce81cb-b8a2-30a3-a458-0120ae67c596)
- Midland (Birmingham & Midland Bank): "Amalgamation with Coventry Union Banking Company and Leamington Priors & Warwickshire Banking Company followed in 1889". — [Encyclopedia.com: Midland Bank plc](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/midland-bank-plc). Midland later marked the 150th anniversary of the Coventry Union Banking Company as "Midland Bank's predecessor in Coventry". — [Coventry Collections](https://www.coventrycollections.org/search/details/archive/110249328)
- Barclays acquired the United Counties Bank in **1916**, "thereby securing a substantial presence in the Midlands". United Counties was the 1907 name of the Birmingham District & Counties Banking Co. — [Barclays: West Midlands history](https://www.home.barclays/who-we-are/our-strategy/backing-the-uk/west-midlands/history/); [Archives Hub: United Counties Bank](https://archiveshub.jisc.ac.uk/search/archives/353d83b5-7d74-319e-9f39-c1df93bbfa4f)
- Bankers named by Coventry firms in the 1914 *Who's Who in Business*:
  - Rudge-Whitworth Ltd (Crow Lane, Coventry; cycles, motorcycles, wheels): "Bankers: Lloyds Bank Limited" — [Grace's Guide 1914 Who's Who: Company R](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_R)
  - Rotherham & Sons (Spon Street; watches, cycle and motor accessories): Lloyds Bank Limited — [same](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_R)
  - Hollick & Pratt Ltd (Mile Lane, Cheylesmore; motor bodies; staff 150–160): "London City and Midland Bank, Ltd." — [Company H](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_H)
  - Thomas Stevens (Coventry) Ltd (Stevengraph Works): London City and Midland Bank — [Company S](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_S)
- Members of the London Bankers' Clearing House in 1911: Bank of England, Barclay & Co., Capital & Counties, Glyn Mills Currie, **Lloyds Bank**, London & South Western, **London City & Midland**, London County & Westminster, London Joint Stock, Martin's, Metropolitan, National, National Provincial, Parr's, Robarts Lubbock, Union of London & Smiths, Williams Deacon's. — [Thomson, Dictionary of Banking, 1911](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofbank00thomrich)

**Clearing system and timing**
- "A country banker 'clears' the cheques which he holds upon another banker in the same town in the daily exchange or daily clearing … The difference … is settled by cash, draft, or London Payment." "There are Clearing Houses at Birmingham, Bristol, Leeds, Leicester, Liverpool, Manchester, Newcastle and Sheffield." Coventry is not on the list. "Cheques upon other banks in England and Wales are sent to London to be collected through the Bankers' Clearing House, in Post Office Court, Lombard Street." — [Dictionary of Banking 1911](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofbank00thomrich)
- London had "four clearings each day, viz.: Metropolitan, Town (morning), Country Cheque, and Town (afternoon)". The Country Clearing opened at 10.30, with last deliveries including returns at 12.30 (10.00 and 11.30 on Saturdays). Under the rules for country cheques, each London bank sends cheques to its country correspondents for collection. The country bank must "write by return of post" crediting its London agent, and returns any unpaid cheque "direct to the country or branch bank … whose name and address is across it". The fourth of the month, when many bills fell due, was among the busiest days. — [Dictionary of Banking 1911](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofbank00thomrich)
- "Effects not cleared": banks could refuse to pay against uncleared cheques only if the customer had been told so. Some paying-in slips carried the notice "Cheques, etc., for collection, though credited to the account when paid in, are not available for drawing against until the proceeds have been received at the branch." — [Dictionary of Banking 1911](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofbank00thomrich)

**Current account terms, interest, commission**
- "London bankers do not, as a rule, allow interest upon a current account but country bankers usually do so, the rate being regulated according to the custom of the district". Commission is charged for collecting and paying cheques, or "in lieu of commission, the customer has to maintain a certain credit balance upon which no interest is allowed". Deposit-receipt interest was often scaled to Bank Rate. Interest on loans was charged with "half-yearly rests", that is, compounded. — [Dictionary of Banking 1911](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofbank00thomrich)
- Bank Rate: 5% on 1 January 1913 and on 1 January 1914; cut to 4½% on 8 January 1914 and to 3% by 29 January 1914 — [FRED, Bank of England policy rate table](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/BOERUKA.txt); [Bank of England, 1914–21 history, introduction](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/archive/ww/boe-1914-1921-vol1-introduction.pdf). **Caveat:** I saw these figures only as search-result extracts and could not open the source tables. One extract says a 5% rate was "imposed on October 2" 1913, which implies a lower rate earlier in 1913. Treat Bank Rate in 1913 as 4½–5%.

**Overdrafts, loans, security**
- "An advance is granted either by way of overdraft upon a current account, or by a loan upon a separate account, or … upon a promissory note. The discounting of bills is practically the same as making an advance upon the security of the bills." Managers judge by personal knowledge, "the certified balance sheet of the customer", and the history of the account. Preferred securities are "first-class stocks and shares, deeds of readily realisable properties, good bearer bonds, guarantees by reliable sureties, and life policies". Bankers avoided "unmarketable securities, such as mills, ironworks". An agreed overdraft limit cannot be withdrawn against cheques already drawn within it (Rouse v Bradford Banking Co., 1894). — [Dictionary of Banking 1911](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofbank00thomrich)
- A cycle-industry case: F. Hopper & Co. (Barton-on-Humber, 800 workpeople in 1912) issued "£12,000 First Debentures plus £5,000 Second Debenture Stock in July 1907 to secure bank overdraft of £15,000". — [Harrison, PhD thesis, York 1977, table from Elswick-Hopper business records](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)

**Wages cash**
- Dicksee advises: "Where possible, a cheque should always be drawn for the exact amount of wages required", with the cash made up by the cashier and paid out in the presence of a works manager. — [Dicksee, Auditing 1912](https://archive.org/details/practica00dickauditingrich)

**Bills of exchange: drawing, acceptance, discounting, dishonour**
- "Bills payable thirty or more days after date are not due for repayment until three days after the expiration of their term: these days are called Days of Grace." "A Bill payable in this country requires an ad valorem stamp." A cheque is "a bill of exchange, drawn on a banker and payable on demand … A cheque requires a penny stamp." — [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)
- Discounting: "The usual practice of bankers is to give credit for the face value of the bill, and to debit the customer's account with the amount of the discount; … some bankers deduct the discount from the face value … and give credit for the net proceeds." Dishonour of a discounted bill: "a cheque is drawn for the amount of the bill and charges and paid over to the holder", and the whole is debited to the customer it came from. Undiscounted dishonoured bills are debited to the acceptor "plus noting charges". Bills could also be endorsed over to creditors in payment. — [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)

**Stamp duties in force in 1913 (Stamp Act 1891, First Schedule)**
- "BILL OF EXCHANGE — Payable on demand or at sight or on presentation: **1d**." This covers cheques. — [Stamp Act 1891, Sch. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/54-55/39/schedule/FIRST/enacted)
- Other bills of exchange and promissory notes: not over £5: 1d; £5–10: 2d; £10–25: 3d; £25–50: 6d; £50–75: 9d; £75–100: 1s; "Exceeds 100l. for every 100l., and also for any fractional part of 100l.: 1s". — [Stamp Act 1891, Sch. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/54-55/39/schedule/FIRST/enacted)
- "RECEIPT given for, or upon the payment of, money amounting to 2l. or upwards: **1d**." Exemptions include receipts for money deposited with a banker. — [Stamp Act 1891, Sch. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/54-55/39/schedule/FIRST/enacted)
- Protest of a bill: the same duty as the bill where that does not exceed 1s, otherwise 1s. Voting paper or proxy for a company meeting: 1d. Policy of insurance against accident: 1d. — [Stamp Act 1891, Sch. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/54-55/39/schedule/FIRST/enacted)
- Conveyance or transfer on sale: 2s 6d for £20–25 of consideration, 5s for £25–50, 10s for £75–100, then 5s per £50, i.e. about 10s per £100 (½%) under the 1891 scale. The Finance (1909–10) Act 1910 s.73 **doubled** these duties but excluded "any stock or marketable security", and excluded transactions up to £500 certified as not part of a larger one. — [Stamp Act 1891, Sch. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/54-55/39/schedule/FIRST/enacted); [Finance (1909–10) Act 1910 s.73](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/1910/act/8/enacted/en/print)
- Letters of allotment: Dicksee (1909) says they "bear a sixpenny stamp if they represent shares of a face value of £5 or upwards, and a penny stamp if below". — [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)
- The cheque and bill duty became 2d only from 1918. The Finance Act 1918 s.36 substituted "Twopence … for one penny as the stamp duty on all bills of exchange and promissory notes chargeable … with duty at the rate of one penny and drawn on or after the first day of September, nineteen hundred and eighteen". **So in 1913 a cheque cost 1d, not 2d.** — [Finance Act 1918 s.36](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/1918/en/act/pub/0015/sec0036.html). The receipt stamp went to 2d under the Finance Act 1920 (search-result summary) — [Irish Statute Book, FA 1920 s.34](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/1920/en/act/pub/0018/sec0034.html)

### Inferences
- A local Coventry cheque (Lloyds to Midland) was probably exchanged between the two branches the same or next day by clerks, since Coventry had no clearing house. A customer's cheque drawn on a bank in Manchester or Glasgow went via the bank's London office into the Country Clearing. Its fate would be known by return of post, about 3–4 working days after paying in **(estimate)**.
- Overdraft cost: plausibly Bank Rate plus about 1%, with a floor of 4–5%, compounded half-yearly. This is a common period convention I could **not** source. Discount of good three-month trade bills would be near or a little above Bank Rate **(estimate)**.
- Objects for the office: pass book (the bank's handwritten copy of the account, sent for writing up), paying-in book with counterfoils, cheque book (pre-printed cheques with impressed 1d stamps), bills receivable and payable books, a box of adhesive penny stamps for receipts, a rubber stamp or printed "& Co." crossing on outgoing cheques.

### Gaps
- No primary source found for overdraft interest rates in 1913, the actual 1913 Bank Rate dates, or the clearing time for a Coventry–London cheque.
- No source found for whether the Coventry branches of Lloyds or Midland belonged to the **Birmingham** clearing.
- No National Provincial or other bank branch in Coventry was checked.
- The 1891 schedule text was read for crossings indirectly only. The rules on crossed cheques ("& Co.", "not negotiable", the banker's name), under the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 ss.76–81, were not re-read in a primary source.

## 4. Taxation and local rates

### Takeaway
For the income-tax year 1913–14 the standard rate was **1s 2d in the £ (5.83%)**, set by the Finance Act 1913. A company's trading profit was assessed under **Schedule D on the average of the three preceding years' profits**, with a deduction for wear and tear of machinery. Tax was deducted at source from dividends and debenture interest. Super-tax (6d in the £ on the excess over £3,000 for individuals with total income above £5,000) fell on wealthy shareholders, not on the company. Local rates were charged on the factory's rateable value, and how far machinery counted towards that value varied between assessment committees.

### Cited Findings
- "Income tax for the year beginning on the sixth day of April nineteen hundred and thirteen shall be charged at the rate of one shilling and twopence, and the same super-tax shall be charged … as was charged for the year beginning on the sixth day of April nineteen hundred and twelve." — [Finance Act 1913 s.2](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/1913/en/act/pub/0030/sec0002.html)
- The 1s 2d rate dates from 1909–10, when super-tax was introduced: "an additional duty of income tax (in this Act referred to as a super-tax) at the rate of sixpence for every pound of the amount by which the total income exceeds three thousand pounds", for "any individual, the total of which from all sources exceeds five thousand pounds". It was assessed by the Special Commissioners. — [Finance (1909–10) Act 1910 ss.65–66, 72](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/1910/act/8/enacted/en/print)
- Earned-income relief: individuals with incomes of £2,000–£3,000 got the reduced rate (Finance Act 1907 s.19, as extended by FA 1910 s.67). Child allowance applied for incomes between £160 and £500. — [Finance (1909–10) Act 1910 ss.67–68](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/1910/act/8/enacted/en/print)
- Schedule D rules, quoted in Garcke & Fells (1893): profits of a trade or manufacture are computed "upon a fair and just average of three years, ending on such day of the year immediately preceding the year of assessment on which the accounts … shall have been usually made up". A business started within the three years is averaged from commencement. There is no deduction for repairs beyond the three-year average, for capital withdrawn or employed, or for improvements to premises. Doubtful debts may be estimated. — [Garcke & Fells 1893, App. B](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620) (pre-1908, but the rules were unchanged until 1926–27, see next point)
- Averaging continued after 1913: "Until the year 1927-28, it was necessary to take sufficient earlier accounts to make up in all three years. The liability was the average for one year of the adjusted profits of the whole period." I saw this only as a web-search extract. I could not confirm which page it came from: I checked [BAILII TC 19 195](https://mansfield.bailii.org/uk/cases/UKHL/1935/TC_19_195.html) and [HMRC INTM165120](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/international-manual/intm165120) and neither contains it. It is consistent with the averaging rule quoted from Garcke & Fells above.
- Wear and tear: the Commissioners "shall … allow such deduction as they may think just and reasonable, as representing the diminished value, by reason of wear and tear during the year, of any machinery or plant used for the purposes of the concern" (Customs and Inland Revenue Act 1878, 41 Vict. c.15, s.12). Garcke & Fells note that "some difficulty may be experienced in inducing the local commissioners to permit a sufficient deduction". — [Garcke & Fells 1893, App. B](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620)
- Relief when profits fall: Garcke & Fells' worked example is a manufacturer who made £10,000 a year for three years and is assessed on £10,000. If the year's profit is only £5,000 he "will be entitled to recover back the tax … on £1,666" and have his next assessment fixed at £8,333 (28 Vict. c.30 s.6). — [Garcke & Fells 1893](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620)
- Assessment machinery: local commissioners together with Government "Surveyors of Taxes". In cases of overcharge, "communicate or obtain an interview with the Surveyor of Taxes as early as possible". — [Garcke & Fells 1893](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620)
- Deduction at source: preference dividends and debenture interest were paid net of tax, with the tax credited to "Income Tax Account" in the company's books. Dicksee's example uses 1s in the £ (the 1909 exercise); the 1913 rate was 1s 2d. — [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)
- Local rates on factories: assessments "originate with local surveyors and assessment committees, and in practice the greatest diversity prevails". Where factories are few, machinery is often ignored and the assessment rests on net rental value. In industrial districts, committees use "capacity of production as the guide … the spindle, horse-power, or other common factor". The High Court's Bishopwearmouth case held machinery could be taken into account. — [Garcke & Fells 1893, App. C](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620) (pre-1908)

### Inferences
- Company tax burden in the model: with an adjusted profit averaging £8,000 over three years, tax at 1s 2d is about **£467** a year. The company pays it through the Schedule D assessment and recovers it from shareholders by deducting it from dividends, effectively acting as a withholding agent **(estimate, based on the cited mechanism)**.
- The tax year runs 6 April to 5 April. A cycle firm's year ending August or September means the 1913–14 assessment rests on the accounts for, say, the years to August 1910, 1911 and 1912.
- Other duties: inhabited house duty applied to dwelling-houses, not factories (not verified). Customs duties on imported components (for example German or American parts) did not exist for cycle parts before the 1915 McKenna duties (general knowledge, not verified here).

### Gaps
- The **Coventry poundage** (rate in the £) for 1913–14, and the rateable value of a typical Coventry cycle works, were **not found**.
- Exact Schedule D practice for companies in 1913 (the claim procedure for wear-and-tear allowances, Form 11) was not retrieved from a 1913 source.

## 5. Insurance: fire, employers' liability and workmen's compensation, boilers, goods in transit; premiums

### Takeaway
Fire insurance was written by "tariff offices" under the Fire Offices' Committee (formed 1868), which fixed minimum rates by risk class. Policies carried average clauses, reinstatement options and stock indemnity at cost. Insurers granted substantial discounts for automatic sprinklers; one source gives 25% if inspected twice a year. Consequential-loss (loss of profits) cover was tariffed from 1909. The Workmen's Compensation Act 1906 (in force 1 July 1907) made the employer liable for injury to virtually all workmen. Insurance was **not** compulsory, but most employers insured. Premiums were quoted in shillings **per £100 of wages**. Boiler explosions were excluded from fire policies and insured separately by specialist companies, which also did the periodic inspections that the factory acts required.

### Cited Findings
**Fire**
- The Fire Offices' Committee "first appeared in May 1868" and "continued to supervise the rating of fire risks insured by the tariff offices for over a century". From 1853 the cooperating companies were called the "Tariff Offices". — [AIM25: Fire Offices' Committee](https://atom.aim25.com/index.php/fire-offices-committee)
- A Consequential Loss Committee followed an FOC resolution of 11 December 1908, approved 14 May 1909, to "rate the premiums for consequential loss fire insurance and administer the Consequential Loss Tariff". — [AIM25: FOC Consequential Loss Committee](https://atom.aim25.com/index.php/fire-offices-committee-consequential-loss-committee-2)
- Risks were classed "ordinary, hazardous, extra hazardous, and special". Premiums ran "from 1s. 6d. per cent. for ordinary dwelling-houses" up to several pounds per cent for dangerous stores and special factory risks, so terms were agreed case by case. The "Tariff Association" fixed minimum rates for hazardous risks, which encouraged mutual insurance among manufacturers and the use of American and Continental offices. Under the "conditions of average" an underinsured owner bears a proportional share of the loss. Insurers have the option to reinstate. Salvage is usually auctioned. Stock is indemnified at production cost without profit. Rent cover is usually limited to one year's rent. Boiler explosions are **not** covered by fire policies. — [Garcke & Fells 1893, App. D](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620) (pre-1908)
- Sprinklers: "Initially, a discount of 25% was granted if the sprinkler system was inspected twice a year, and without regular inspections, this was reduced to 20%." — [VdS, 150 years of sprinklers](https://vds.de/en/certification/inspection-service/information-innovation/information-news/150-year-sprinkler-2). This is a German insurers' body writing a general history, so it may describe German rather than British FOC practice.

**Workmen's compensation and employers' liability**
- The Workmen's Compensation Act 1906 commenced on 1 July 1907 and extended compensation to almost all employments. — [Wikipedia: Workmen's Compensation Act 1906](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workmen%27s_Compensation_Act_1906)
- Insurance remained voluntary. The post-war Holman Gregory committee recommended compulsion and a 70% loss ratio, but "Compulsory insurance was never implemented (except for mines)". Before its recommendations, of every £100 employers paid to insurers only about £51 went back to workers. — [Wikipedia: Holman Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holman_Gregory) (post-1914 context)
- Premium levels in the 1906 Commons debate: in low-risk farming "the premium ranged from 7s. 6d. to 10s. per cent.", meaning per £100 of wages, which "represented ten or a dozen times the risk" because of minimum premiums. Insurers expected the 1906 extension to raise premiums: "the General Accident Company thought that the additional premium required would be 35 per cent. and the General Insurance thought it would be 50 per cent." Evidence before the Digby committee put the added cost at 25–50%. — [Hansard, Workmen's Compensation Bill, 26 March 1906](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1906/mar/26/workmens-compensation-bill-1)
- Railways' compensation actually paid in 1906 "works out at about 10s. 7d. per 100 pounds of wages" (Cd. 3622). An English insurer's December 1907 tariff for builders ran from 15s to 40s per £100 of wages, depending on building height and machinery ($0.75–2.00 per $100). — search-result extract of a US report quoting British data, [UW Digital Collections, "Industrial Accidents and Employers' Liability"](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/AUIUBBEBOIFVPJ8K/text/AMHRKZCPBPETNQ8F). The page text itself would not load, so treat as indicative.
- Stamp duty on an accident or indemnity policy: 1d. — [Stamp Act 1891, Sch. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/54-55/39/schedule/FIRST/enacted)

**Boilers**
- "Losses due to the explosion of boilers or collapse of flues are not covered by ordinary fire policies. Such losses can … be provided against by insurance with the companies carrying on this branch". These companies give "responsible inspection of the boilers by an officer of the Insurance Company, with a written report of the result, at periodical intervals". They indemnify damage to the boiler and surrounding property, and injury to persons, except where it results from overloaded safety valves or a wilful act. They also undertake repairs, maintenance and engine indicating under special contracts. — [Garcke & Fells 1893, App. D](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620)
- Statutory examination: "Every steam boiler used for generating steam in a factory or workshop must be examined thoroughly by a competent person at least once in every fourteen months", with the report entered in the general register within 14 days. This matches the UK Factory and Workshop Act 1901 s.11, but the wording I saw is from a **New South Wales 1916 bill** modelled on it. — [NSW Factories and Shops (Amendment) Bill 1916](https://legacy.legislation.nsw.gov.au/archive/BillsStanding/Factories%20and%20Shops%20(Amendment)%20Bill%201916.pdf). The UK text did not download.

### Inferences
- In practice the "competent person" for boiler examinations was usually the boiler insurer's engineer-surveyor, so boiler insurance and statutory inspection were bought together **(inference, consistent with Garcke & Fells)**.
- Model premiums **(estimates; no 1913 cycle-trade tariff found)**:
  - **Workmen's compensation and employers' liability**: light engineering with power presses, brazing hearths and enamelling stoves probably 10s–20s per £100 of wages. On a wage bill of about £30,000 that is roughly £150–£300 a year, adjusted at year end on a declaration of actual wages.
  - **Fire**: a brick multi-storey works with enamelling stoves, japanning and brazing hearths, petrol, celluloid and oils would be rated well above ordinary (perhaps 2s 6d–7s 6d per cent). Cover of £40,000–£60,000 on buildings, plant and stock gives about £60–£200 a year, less any sprinkler discount. Loss-of-profits cover was optional from 1909.
  - **Boiler insurance and inspection** for one or two Lancashire boilers: a few pounds per boiler per year.
- Goods-in-transit: cycles sent by rail travelled at the railway's "owner's risk" or "company's risk" rates. Separate goods-in-transit policies existed but I found nothing specific. Transport is covered by another researcher.

### Gaps
- No 1913 British FOC tariff rate for cycle or engineering works was found, nor any UK-specific sprinkler discount rule (FOC sprinkler rules date from the 1880s onward but were not retrieved).
- No pre-1914 workmen's compensation premium rate specific to engineering or cycle making was found.
- No boiler-insurance premium figures were found.
- No goods-in-transit premium information was found.

## 6. Calibration figures: capital, turnover, profit, dividends, employment, cost structure of British cycle companies c. 1897–1914

### Takeaway
Hard published figures for Coventry cycle firms in 1910–14 proved hard to retrieve online. Newspaper archives (BNA, Papers Past, Welsh Newspapers, HathiTrust) were blocked or paywalled. The best documented anchors are:
- gross output per head in the UK cycle and motor trades of **£229** (1907 Census of Production) and **£198** at F. Hopper & Co. (1912, 800 workpeople);
- average cycle prices around **£4–5** ex-works or export by 1912–13;
- 1906 cycle-trade weekly earnings: men **34s 8d**, women **13s 8d**, boys **10s 9d**, girls **9s 6d**;
- dividend norms, with 10% regarded as "a big dividend";
- profit and dividend records for 1897–1900, the heavy capital write-downs of 1900–05, and examples of capital and overdraft structures.

From these, a 400-worker Coventry firm in 1913 plausibly turned over **£80,000–£100,000** a year, with net profit of **£5,000–£10,000** in a fair year **(estimate)**.

### Cited Findings
**Output per worker, prices, wages**
- "Gross output per man in the American motor-cycle and cycle trade probably stood higher than the British: it was £310 in 1904 and £482 in 1909, compared to **£229 per capita in the U.K. motor and cycle trades of 1907**, and to **£198 for F. Hopper and Company Limited of Barton-on-Humber** … in 1912". "Hopper's employed 800 workpeople in that year and exported approximately half of its total output". The sources are the UK Census of Production 1907 and the Elswick-Hopper business records. — [Harrison, "Growth, Entrepreneurship and Capital Formation in the UK's Cycle and Related Industries, 1870–1914", PhD, York 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)
- "In the last week of September 1906 British cycle workers earned: men aged 20 years and over, 34/8d; women 18 years and over, 13/8d; boys, 10/9d; and girls, 9/6d" (Board of Trade, *Earnings and Hours of Labour*, vol. VI, Cd. 5814, 1911). — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)
- Prices: "Between 1905 and 1914 … the average price of the American cycle fell by approximately ten shillings, while that of the British machine by over £2. Between 1907 and 1913 the average export price of British cycles fell by £0.84 while that of the German by £0.49." Harrison's Table 16 gives UK average export prices; the values consistent with that £0.84 fall are **£4.97 in 1907 and £4.13 in 1913** (my reading of an OCR-garbled table). There is also a UK ex-factory (Census) average of about £5.52 in 1907 and £4.54 in 1912 (same caveat). — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)
- UK exports of pedal cycles, parts and accessories: £1,957,287 (1910), £2,024,199 (1911), £2,058,817 (1912), £2,087,198 (1913). — [Harrison 1977, Table 13](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)
- Home demand signal: Cyclists' Touring Club membership fell from 56,147 (1900) to 15,474 (1913), as utility cycling replaced the club and leisure market. — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)

**Employment of named firms**
- Rudge Cycle Co. (Coventry) employed 600 hands in 1887 and 764 in 1893. Premier (Coventry) employed over 500 in 1891. The Coventry Cycle Co. employed 160 in 1887. — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)
- Humber's new Folly Lane works in Coventry (1908) covered 13.5 acres, could produce "75,000 cycles and 7,500 automobiles per annum and employ 5,000 persons". — [Grace's Guide: Humber](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Humber)
- Coventry Chain Co. (Spon End Works; chains for cycles and engineering) in 1914: "now employ 1500 hands … Staff: 100. Works, 1400", on premises of 135,000 sq ft. That is roughly 1 office or staff person per 14 works hands. — [Grace's Guide 1914 Who's Who: Company C](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_C)
- Raleigh (Nottingham) was by 1913 "the biggest bicycle manufacturing company in the world" — [Wikipedia: Raleigh Bicycle Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raleigh_Bicycle_Company). It "Expected to make 65,000 machines" in 1914 — [Grace's Guide: Raleigh Cycle Co](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Raleigh_Cycle_Co). Secondary sites say about 1,700 workers made over 60,000 cycles a year by 1913. I saw this only in search summaries and did not verify it.

Most financial years ended "usually August or September". — [Harrison 1977, Table 25](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)
- Contemporary benchmark (1897): G. H. Cartland of New Enfield said "Some might say 10 per cent was not a big dividend. For his part he thought it was". A shareholder called "a 10 per cent dividend … somewhat exceptional in commercial undertakings. In the iron trade 10 per cent would make their mouths water". — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)

**Capital structures and restructurings**
- After the boom, many firms wrote capital down. Swift Cycle's 200,000 £1 ordinary shares took "a four-fifths reduction in 1901". New Centaur cut its £99,875 ordinary capital to £49,937 10s (1903). Singer Cycle's preference and ordinary holders accepted large reductions (1903). Raglan's £120,000 ordinary was cut by two-thirds (1905). Premier, Raleigh and Dunlop were reconstructed (1902, 1899, 1906). Firms that avoided write-downs include James, Star, J. B. Brooks, Enfield, Lucas, New Hudson, Riley and Triumph. New Premier's net profit was £78,133 in 1896/7, followed by losses in 1899–1902. Singer Cycle's £75,396 profit (1896/7) fell to £3,067 by 1901/2. — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)
- Rover had half its ordinary capital written off in 1902–3. J. K. Starley kept his own flotation capital down "to enable him to live with it". — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)

### Inferences (calibration model for a fictional 400-worker Coventry cycle works, year to 31 August 1913; all **estimates**)
- **Workforce**: about 400 works hands plus about 25–30 office and staff (the Coventry Chain ratio of roughly 1:14). The works might be 270 men, 60 youths and boys, and 50 women and girls in enamelling, lining, polishing, packing and the saddlery or finishing shops, plus about 20 foremen and chargehands.
- **Wage bill**: take the 1906 rates up about 5–8% for 1913 (men about 36s, boys about 11s 6d, women about 14s 6d, foremen about 50–60s). Weekly works wages come to about £570–£620, roughly **£29,000–£31,000 a year** over 50 working weeks. Salaries (managers, secretary, clerks, travellers) add about £4,000–£5,000.
- **Turnover**: £200–£230 gross output per head × about 430 heads gives **about £85,000–£100,000**. As a cross-check, 12,000–15,000 machines at an ex-works or trade price of about £5–6 (a Coventry "quality" maker above the national average of about £4.1–4.5), plus spares and repairs, gives about £70,000–£95,000.
- **Cost structure (share of sales)**:
  - materials and bought-in components (tube, stampings, lugs, rims, spokes, tyres, saddles, chains, free-wheels or 3-speed hubs, enamel, packing) about **45–50%**;
  - works wages about **32–35%**;
  - works overhead (power, coal and gas, repairs, loose tools, depreciation, rates, insurance) about **6–8%**;
  - selling and administration (salaries, advertising, catalogues, the Olympia show, travellers, carriage, bad debts, bank charges) about **6–8%**;
  - **net profit about 5–10%**, i.e. about £5,000–£9,000.
- **Balance sheet**:
  - issued capital about £60,000–£80,000 (for example £40,000–£50,000 ordinary in £1 shares and £20,000 in 5–6% cumulative preference shares);
  - possibly £10,000–£15,000 of 5% debentures with a floating charge registered under s.93;
  - general reserve about £5,000–£15,000;
  - fixed assets: freehold land and buildings about £25,000–£35,000; plant and machinery about £20,000–£30,000 after depreciation at 7½%; loose tools, jigs and patterns about £2,000–£4,000;
  - stock and work in progress about £15,000–£25,000 (peaking in spring);
  - trade debtors about £10,000–£20,000;
  - bank overdraft in the winter and spring build-up, perhaps £5,000–£15,000 against debentures or deeds.
- **Appropriation in a fair year**: preference dividend paid in full; ordinary dividend **5–10%**, sometimes with an interim in spring; £1,000–£3,000 to reserve; directors' fees £100–£300 each.
- **Seasonality** for cash flow: production builds stock from autumn to spring, sales peak March to July, the overdraft peaks around February to April, and the stock-take and closing fall in late August.

These almost certainly exist in *The Times*, *The Economist*, *The Statist*, *Cycle Manufacturer* and the *Stock Exchange Year-Book 1914*, but they were paywalled or blocked here. ## 7. Petty cash and small payments: postage, carriage, telegrams, sundries; stamp accounting

### Takeaway
Small outgoings went through a Petty Cash Book on the **imprest system**: a fixed float topped up by a cheque for the exact amount spent, with the spending analysed into nominal-account columns. Postage stamps were bought from the post office and recorded, often in a separate postage or stamp book. Inland postal rates in January 1913: letters **1d** for up to 4 oz plus ½d for each further 2 oz; postcards **½d**; parcels **3d** for 1 lb rising to 11d for 11 lb; registration 2d; inland telegrams **6d for 12 words** plus ½d for each extra word.

### Cited Findings
- Imprest system (Dicksee, 1912): one variant gives the petty cashier a fixed monthly float. In the other, "there will be a small initial balance, and when it becomes nearly exhausted a cheque will be given for the exact amount spent up to date". Cheques are posted to a Petty Cash Account in the Ledger. The monthly summary is credited to Petty Cash and debited to the various nominal accounts, so the Petty Cash Account balance always equals the initial float. "Both the above are varieties of the 'Imprest System,' so called from the demand (or imprest) presented to the Chief Cashier by the Petty Cashier." The Chief Cashier "should thoroughly examine the Petty Cash Book each time he draws a cheque". — [Dicksee, Auditing, 9th ed. 1912](https://archive.org/details/practica00dickauditingrich)
- A petty cash book is "a secondary Cash Book". Dicksee's exercise opens with "received from cashier for Petty Cash, £10". — [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)
- A "Postage Book" appears as a standard office record in a fraud case reported in Dicksee's *Auditing* ("They could take the Postage Book, for instance"), alongside the examination of "the payments of postage stamps" and a "Stamp Distribution" record. — [Dicksee, Auditing 1912](https://archive.org/details/practica00dickauditingrich)
- Post Office Guide, 1 January 1913:
  - Letters: "Not exceeding 4 oz. in weight … 1d. For every additional 2 oz. … ½d." Unpaid letters were charged double postage on delivery.
  - Postcards: "a halfpenny, and on every reply post-card a penny".
  - Inland parcels: 1 lb 3d, 2 lb 4d, 3 lb 5d, 5 lb 6d, 7 lb 7d, 8 lb 8d, 9 lb 9d, 10 lb 10d, 11 lb 11d (maximum 11 lb; 3 ft 6 in long; length plus girth up to 6 ft).
  - "The ordinary registration fee is 2d."
  - "INLAND TELEGRAMS. The charge is 6d. for the first 12 words, including the address, and ½d. for every additional word."
  - Source: [Post Office Guide, 1 January 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- Receipts given for petty payments of £2 or more needed a 1d receipt stamp, which made an adhesive "postage and revenue" stamp a routine office item. — [Stamp Act 1891, Sch. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/54-55/39/schedule/FIRST/enacted)

### Inferences
- Model a petty-cash float of about £10–£20, topped up weekly or monthly by an imprest cheque. Typical columns: Postage, Telegrams and Telephone, Carriage (small parcels and railway carriage paid on delivery), Travelling (tram and railway fares), Stationery, Sundries (cleaning, tea, gratuities), Wages Advances.
- A stamp book records stamps bought (from the petty cash or by cheque) and stamps used, by letters posted per day. A 1913 Coventry works office might post 50–150 letters and circulars a day, more in the catalogue season, costing about £1–£3 a week in postage **(estimate)**.
- Telephone: Coventry firms listed telephone numbers in 1914 (for example Rudge-Whitworth "Telephone: No. 2 (G.P.O.) and 404 (National), Coventry") — [Grace's Guide 1914 Who's Who: Company R](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_R). Calls and rentals would be paid by cheque against Post Office accounts, with trunk calls perhaps through petty cash **(inference)**.

### Gaps
- No 1913 telephone tariff (rental or trunk call charges) was retrieved. The National Telephone Co. was transferred to the Post Office on 1 January 1912; that is general knowledge, not verified here.
- No primary specimen ruling of a "stamp book" or "postage book" was found, only references to its existence.
