# Manufacturing processes in a British (Coventry) cycle works, c. 1910–1914

Scope note for the report writer. The best single primary source is W. F. Grew, *The Cycle Industry* (Pitman, 1921), chapter V, "From the Stores to the Railway Dray". Grew takes the reader on a "personally conducted tour round a large cycle factory" in process order. He wrote in 1921, but he describes the hearth and dip brazing, stoving and hand finishing that were in use before the war. The best source for c. 1910–12 Coventry is G. R. Carter's chapter "The Cycle Industry" in *Seasonal Trades* (ed. Webb and Freeman, 1912). Carter based it on interviews with managers, foremen and trade union officials, and it lists every shop and job title. Workshop handbooks (1897–1916) supply the temperatures and times. A. E. Harrison's 1977 PhD thesis (York) supplies works-level facts from the trade press of 1895–1914. Practice is marked **[American]** wherever the source is American.

## 1. What was the sequence of departments/shops in a cycle works c. 1913, and what happened in each?

### Takeaway
A large works ran in this order: rough stores, then machine shop and press shop (with hardening), then frame building (jigging and pegging), brazing, pickling or sand-blasting, filing, polishing, enamelling and stoving, lining and transfers, and the finished stores. Plating, wheel building and the brakework and mudguard benches ran alongside. Then came finishing (assembly) and the viewer, then wrapping and packing, then despatch. Batches were issued from stores, for example "100 machines of a certain model". Parts passed back through a stores, where they were viewed and counted, between the making stages and the finishing stage. Frames were stove-enamelled in 2–3 thin coats at roughly 300–400 °F, for about 1–1.5 hours per coat, with rubbing down between coats.

### Cited Findings

**Overall flow and batch logic**
- Grew's tour covers a large factory "where everything except saddles, handles, tyres, and toolbags, is produced on the premises", "commencing at the stores and finishing at the loading bay of the packing department" (Grew 1921, ch. V) — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- **Rough stores:** "bins on the floor and racks lining the walls right up to the roof". They held "steel tubing in multiple lengths of several feet", bar steel "for making into cups, cones, and spindles for hubs", "castings and stampings for frame lugs, spokes in bundles, steel and wood rims", and millwrights' stores. Each stores had a storekeeper. Deliveries were counted or weighed, and a record card hung over each bin. Stock was kept above a "high-water mark" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- **Finished stores:** bins, wood or iron "fixtures" (pigeon-hole shelving) and racks of finished or part-finished parts: "Thousands of axles, cups, cones, balls, nipples, nuts, screws… the racks hold finished frames, forks, saddles, mudguards, brakework" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- **Batch issue:** a requisition is made to "put through, we will say, 100 machines of a certain model (the requisitions in some factories are much larger)". The rough stores then issues to the machine shop and frame builders "100 sets of frame lugs, 100 sets of tubes cut to length and mitred, enough tube to make up 100 seat pillars, fork-sides, and crowns for 100 forks, 200 rims, the necessary spokes". These "are made up into complete frames, wheels, forks, etc., and go when completed to the finished stores; here they are viewed, and re-issued to the finishing department with the necessary tyres, saddles, handles, brakework" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Each time parts enter the stores, the operatives are credited with the work for piecework. "In very large factories there may be a separate stores for tyres, another for brakework, frames and forks, and another for wheels, each floor or department having its own stores" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Carter (1912) gives the same order of shops: raw materials (tubes, bars, wire) are issued from storerooms to the "turnery departments" (automatic machines making axles, hubs, cones, cups). Next come the press shops; "hardeners"; the frame department (building and brazing); "filers, or sand blasters"; the "polishing shop"; the "plating shop"; the "enamelling shop"; the "liners"; and a "wheel shop". "All the completed parts are gathered in a storeroom, whence they are issued to skilled mechanics, termed 'finishers,' or 'assemblers'". Wrapping and packing follow — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- "Messengers, usually youths, transport material from one shop to another" (Carter 1912) — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- In 1881 a Coventry directory described George Singer's works as having "forging, stamping, turning, milling, drilling, wheel-making, tool making, fitting, examining, brazing, nickel-plating, polishing, painting and packing departments" — quoted in [Harrison 1977, p. 141](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- In 1886 Starley Brothers left their St John's Works, Coventry, which had become "inconvenient" "owing to the separation of the workshops from each other". The new factory was "built so as to have everything under cover… designed with a view to the continuity of progress of the parts from the forgings to the finished machines with as little moving about from one place to another as possible" (The Cyclist, 4 Jan. 1888) — [Harrison 1977, p. 138](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)

**Lug preparation and the machine and press shops**
- Lugs (castings or stampings) are machined on a jig "which is bolted to the lathe or drilling machine". Iron castings are hollow, with little metal to remove. Stampings are solid and need considerable machining. A finished lug "weighs only a few ounces". "Raleigh and Rudge-Whitworth use pressed steel lugs", folded from sheet "between dies in a very powerful screw, or other type press" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Lug quality grades (1897): "malleable castings for cheap, low quality machines, forged stampings for those of a somewhat superior make, and finally, pieces stamped from sheet metal… in a powerful press for machines of the higher grades". Lugs were also "drawn cold from ordinary weldless steel tube by means of hydraulic pressure" — [Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog)
- Pressings need "more than one pair of dies" because cold sheet must be "coaxed" into shape. The dies were expensive, and paid only once designs stopped changing every year (Grew 1921, ch. VI) — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Machine shop work: "turret lathes, forming hubs from bar steel, milling machines, forming the teeth on sprocket wheels… milling or profiling cranks, turning and boring frame lugs and fork crowns, profiling the internal parts of free wheels". Hubs were made "from stampings and castings as well as from bar steel" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Press shops: "handle-bar hoops, crank brackets, and other parts are shaped by powerful presses… requires trained mechanics for the heavier and more intricate portions. Skilled workmen termed 'hardeners' next heat these parts with a special chemical mixture to harden the steel" (i.e. case-hardening) — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- **[American]** Some large American works took case-hardened cups and cones straight to the polishing room without grinding. Lozier and Gormully & Jeffery preferred to grind. High-grade British makers ground them (American Machinist, 21 May 1896, via Harrison) — [Harrison 1977, pp. 324–5](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)

**Frame building (jigging and pinning)**
- "The frame builder is provided with a sort of master frame, called a jig, on which he assembles the lugs and tubes roughly, and when they are positioned by stops on the jig he turns screws which lock the parts while he drills holes at each joint for conical metal pegs which keep the tubes in place while the joints are brazed. The frame builder is responsible for the correct alignment of the frame." He coats the joints with flux before passing the frame on, "or it is taken for him, to the brazing shop" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Frames "should be, put together on suitable erecting beds, fitted up as jiggers". After brazing, the frame is "placed upon a similar jigger table" and allowed to cool while held firmly, so that the bracket and rear forks stay parallel and the head stays true (1897) — [Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog)
- Internal "bird-mouthed" ferrules were driven into tube ends at the joints for strength (1897). Butted tube (Patent Butted Tube Co., Dec. 1898) "obviated the use of 'liners'" — [Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog); [Harrison 1977, p. 158](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)

**Brazing**
- Hearth brazing: "an open fire of 'breeze' (small coke) is kept at a high temperature by allowing a pressure coal gas flame to impinge on the glowing coke. The joint… is pushed into the fire". The brazer feeds "a 'stick' of brass or a spoon filled with brass dust called 'spelter'". Parts the brass must not touch are coated to stop it adhering — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Liquid (dip) brazing: "placing the joint of the frame or other part into molten brass spelter, heated in a special kind of gas oven" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- 1897: "The usual method of brazing is by means of Bunsen flames on a fine coke bed… supplied with air under pressure from a compressor". Ordinary town gas was used, or gas "from a special Dowson gas plant" — [Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog)
- **Rudge-Whitworth (Coventry) "maintained the use of 50 open brazing hearths in 1911 and had no liquid brazing plant"**, because its flush-jointed frames needed the spelter placed inside the tube and lug (Cycle Trader and Review, 28 July 1911, pp. 214–22). Raleigh made liquid brazing commercially viable in 1900 but "continued to use the old open-hearth method… as well" — [Harrison 1977, p. 336](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- "Several of the larger cycle firms" laid down their own gas-generating plants "in view of the large consumption of gas used in cycle frame brazing" (mid-1890s) — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- At Rudge-Whitworth the works laboratory (from 1901) found weak joints "associated with the use of a brazing brass containing a percentage of tin". This led to a specification and regular testing of all brazing brass — [Harrison 1977, p. 334](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- **[American, 1898–99] productivity of liquid brazing.** One maker: "one man and a boy can turn out seventy-five machines in a run of seven hours", against "five to seven men" before with gas-furnace brazing — [The Wheel 1899](https://archive.org/details/wheelcyc2218189891899newy). Fay Mfg. Co.: "One man and a boy helper can braze 100 complete frames in ten hours… A crucible of correct pattern will last from two to three weeks" — [Cycle Age 1899](https://archive.org/details/cycleagetra2118981899nati)
- **[American, 1899] cost structure.** Columbus Bicycle Co. costs per 100 men's frames: all dipping and grinding $10.06; flame-brazing the rear forks and braces to the bracket $3.00; "filing braces and hanger" $28.00 (total $41.06). Filing was by far the largest brazing-related cost. Columbus dipped the front frame first and flame-brazed the rear triangle on afterwards — [Cycle Age 1899](https://archive.org/details/cycleagetra2118981899nati)

**Pickling, sand-blasting and filing**
- After brazing, "the frames and forks go into vats and are pickled… a bath of corrosive liquid that attacks the rough spelter and softens it previous to the filing up of the joint". Alternatively, in a sand-blasting shop spelter "is blown off by a strong current of air, in which sand or shot is carried… from a flexible pipe held in the operator's hand, the frame… being supported on a bench. The sand blasters wear masks and cloaks" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- "The rough edges of the joints are cleaned by 'filers,' or sand blasters" (1912) — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)

**Polishing (iron polishing before finish, and plate polishing)**
- Frames go "to the iron polishers… [who] hold the frames on grinding wheels or in some cases endless belts coated with emery" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Polishing wheels have leather edges coated with glued emery. Final "plate polishing" uses calico "mops" with grease and tripoli or rouge, plus lime or whiting ("mopping"). The shop "resound[s] with the whir of the polishing lathes" and the air is full of steel, emery, glue and leather dust. Respirators and goggles were supposed to be worn. "Practically all parts come to the polishing shop" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- 1912 Coventry: "In all the best factories only men and youths are employed as 'polishers,' since the work is sometimes heavy, and always dirty" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)

**Cleaning and nickel plating**
- Parts from polishing are "not chemically clean". They are "thoroughly scrubbed with chemicals to remove the grease" before plating or enamelling. Small articles "are strung on wires… and hung from metal rods which are charged with an electrical current of low voltage". The nickel slab is the anode. Parts come out "dull plated… resembl[ing] unpolished aluminium" and are re-mopped — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- 1912: parts "scrubbed clean in a soda solution and immersed in electric plating-vats. Afterwards they are polished with felt 'mops'". Women and girls (mostly in Birmingham) clean parts, string small parts on wires and mop — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- 1897 plating line: hot potash bath, then rinse, then cyanide dip (brass or copper) or hydrochloric acid dip (steel), then scour with pumice on a water trough, then rinse, then the nickel vat. An optional copper strike first. Vat: wood lined with lead, then match-boarding. Solution: double sulphate of nickel and ammonia, 1 lb per gallon. Anodes 3 in apart. Plate by dynamo rather than battery. Dynamos deposit "from 20 ozs. up to 50 lbs." of nickel per hour and need 1–4 hp; "most useful… about 50 ozs. of metal per hour". Time in the bath "depends on the thickness of plate wanted" — [Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog)
- Rudge-Whitworth's lab (early 1900s) "led to a patented [plating] process giving much improved coatings in less time than usual" — [Harrison 1977, p. 334](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)

**Enamelling (dipping or pouring) and stoving**
- Work is degreased ("baths of heated patent liquid", or turpentine). Then "the frame, fork, mudguards, etc., are either dipped in liquid black enamel and hung up to drain or liquid enamel is poured over them". When the excess has drained back to the pan's sump, parts are lifted on hooks ("the enamelled surface must not be touched with the hand"). They are hung "in gas heated stoves, where they are baked at a high temperature for a few hours. The very best bicycles receive at least three coats of thin enamel and are stoved between each application". The finish "should resist blows with a wood broom handle without cracking" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- 1912 Coventry: frames, forks and mudguards are "enamelled black, green, red, etc., as required. The 'enamellers' are usually men, assisted by youths" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Temperatures: "The usual temperatures at which to stove enamels are from 300° to 380° Fahr." (1897). Gas-heated double-cased stoves "from 6 ft. in height, 4 ft. in width, and 3 ft. in depth, to 6 ft. in height, 9 ft. in width, and 6 feet in depth". For light colours, combustion gases must not touch the work — [Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog)
- Times and coats (1916): first coat "Stove for one hour at 380° F., leaving the ventilator open for the first five minutes". Later coats "stove at about the same heat for one and a quarter to one and a half hours". "Two coats are generally employed in finishing, though on first-class work… three coats are sometimes given" (one first-coating enamel and two finishing coats). Rub down between coats with pumice powder and water. Best work is hand-polished after the last coat with rotten-stone and water, "worked off with the bare hand". "A cycle frame should be hung head downwards with the first coat" and no tube should hang quite horizontal — [Cassell 1916 p.86](https://tekniskkulturarv.dk/book/dec6aa56-2a37-4558-8ab8-f70b760598dd?page=86); [Cassell 1916 p.87](https://tekniskkulturarv.dk/book/dec6aa56-2a37-4558-8ab8-f70b760598dd?page=87)
- c. 1900: "All good cycle work is stove-enamelled". A 6 ft × 6 ft × 3 ft 6 in stove needs six rows of atmospheric gas burners and must reach "400° Fahr.". It has rows of iron rods 3–4 in below the roof to hang work, and a thermometer read through a mica window. Enamel is ladled over work hung above a pan; "a single cycle may be completed with as little as one gallon of enamel". The first coat is rubbed with tripoli and pumice before the next. Forks are hung crown uppermost for the last stoving. "Wheels require an enamel to stove at a lower heat" — [Hasluck c.1900](https://archive.org/details/cyclebuildingan00unkngoog)
- 1901: bicycle work needs "at least two, and sometimes four or five, successive coats of good but thin enamel". Japanning metals takes 3–5 coats, each oven-dried at 250–300 °F ("about 270 degrees being the average"). Frames can be dipped in a trough, but for bicycles (unlike bedsteads) rubbing down between coats is expected — [Brown 1901](https://archive.org/details/handbookonjapann00browiala)
- Rudge-Whitworth's laboratory work "led to more enlightened choice of materials employed for producing the black coating on bicycle frames" (early 1900s) — [Harrison 1977, p. 334](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)

**Lining and transfers**
- "The enamelled parts are afterwards ornamented with lines of green, red, or gilt, with monograms and other devices by skilled workmen called 'liners.' This calls for highly skilled hand work" (1912) — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Lining used small flat "lining tools" or a long sable pencil. Transfers were laid on with varnish or gold size, then hung in the stove "for about ten or fifteen minutes… at a temperature from about 150° to 200°". They were then varnished and re-stoved about 20 minutes. Too much heat blisters the gold and turns it brown (c. 1900) — [Hasluck c.1900](https://archive.org/details/cyclebuildingan00unkngoog)

**Wheel building**
- Steel rims "are drilled or punched in special machines which space out the holes evenly, for 32, 36, 40, or 44 spokes". Spokes are "supplied headed, bent, and screwed" (often with rolled threads). Nipples are brass or gunmetal, with steel washers. The builder laces spokes through hub flange and rim. Truing "is effected by screwing the nipples down the spoke with a special nipple key turned by hand or power on the end of a flexible shaft. The final truing process is done entirely by hand". "The wheel building is often done by women and girls" (1921) — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- 1912 Coventry wheel shop: "Semi-skilled machine minders fashion steel wire into spokes, pass them on to youths (and sometimes women and girls) who fit them loosely into the hubs and rims, and in turn pass them on to the expert wheel builders… who finish the wheels and 'true' them" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Premier Cycle Co. (Coventry) built its own automatic rim-drilling machine (cam-indexed, several drill spindles), hub-drilling machines and a self-acting hub-turning lathe (1897) — [Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog)
- **[American]** Some American works trued wheels on Pratt & Whitney or Schrader-Garvin truing tables rather than by hand and eye. Uneven spoke tension often left wheels out of true (1896) — [Harrison 1977, p. 325](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)

**Brakework, mudguards, handlebars and seat pillars**
- "Brakework and mudguards are usually made up in a separate shop or on a separate bench". Mudguards arrive from specialists "rolled… from flat strips of thin steel… bright". Stays and bridges are riveted, electrically welded or brazed on. Roller-lever brakes are "usually made up by the cycle maker". Bowden mechanisms are bought in. "The handle-bar brakework is usually assembled on the bar ready to slip into the machine in the finishing shops" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Handlebars are "largely bent by specialists, but some firms make their own". Touring bars are bent cold over a spring mandrel. Racing bars are loaded with sand or resin, then heated and bent. Seat pillars are "nearly always made by the 'lap' joint method" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)

**Finishing (assembly)**
- An "iron pillar standing up at the edge of the bench". The head tube of the frame (without fork) is dropped over it so the frame can swing. The finisher scrapes surplus enamel out of the bottom-bracket threads, "runs a dummy, or easy fitting tap, through the threads", oils them, and screws in the cups. He fits the axle, cotters on cranks and chainwheel, then fits the seat bolt, head cups, rear mudguard, brakework and rear wheel. "To ease the threads the finishers use a hand tapping machine". Then chain and pedals. The front fork and wheel have been "gradually growing to separate front units complete with their mudguards". The frame is dropped over the fork stem and the machine turned upside down. The head balls are "poured in from a little tool that scoops up and counts the required number of balls". Then the handlebar and saddle pillar. "The accessories are always fitted last, and then the machine goes to the viewer" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- 1912: finishers are "usually trained workmen, assisted by learners. The lacing of dress guards on ladies' cycles is usually done by females. Youths, and sometimes females, affix the smaller parts and accessories, and wrap the bright and enamelled parts with cloth ready for the workmen who pack" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)

### Inferences
- A 13-stage flow for the simulation, supported by the citations above: (1) rough stores; (2) tube cutting and mitring plus lug and fork-crown machining, with machine shop and automatics (hubs, cups, cones, axles, cranks, chainwheels) and press shop, then hardening; (3) frame and fork building on jigs, with pegging and fluxing; (4) brazing on hearths (and/or a dip bath); (5) pickling or sand-blasting, then filing; (6) iron polishing; (7) finished stores for viewing and count; (8a) frames, forks and mudguards to enamelling (dip or pour, 2–3 coats, stoved and rubbed between coats), then lining and transfers (stoved again at low heat); (8b) bright parts to plate polishing, cleaning, nickel vats and re-mopping; (8c) wheel shop (rims drilled, spokes laced, trued) and brakework/mudguard bench; (9) finished stores again; (10) finishing (assembly) at pillar benches; (11) viewer; (12) greasing and wrapping; (13) crating or packing for despatch.
- Stoving dominates enamelling time. Three coats at 1–1.5 h each, plus loading, cooling and rubbing down, mean frames spend at least one, more likely two, working days in the enamelling shop. Stoves were sized about 6 ft high by 4–9 ft wide by 3–6 ft deep. Assuming roughly 15–30 frames hang per stove load (my estimate; not sourced), a works making 200 bicycles a week needs a few stoves running more than one load a day. This fits Rudge-Whitworth's reported "three large stoves" in an earlier works (unverified; see Gaps).
- Rough brazing throughput: the American figure of 75 machines in 7 hours by 5–7 hearth brazers works out to about 1.5–2 frames per hearth-brazer-hour, including forks. That suggests a British hearth brazer did perhaps 10–20 frames a day. A 200-a-week works would then need about 3–6 brazing hearths. This is an estimate scaled from American evidence.
- Piece-rate cross-check from Carter (1912). Wheel truers averaged 37s a week for a 54-hour week, and one rate quoted is "10½d. per pair of wheels finished". That implies about 42 pairs a week, or roughly 75–80 minutes per pair at the finishing and truing stage, after lacing by youths. This is an inference and assumes the rate applies to truers.

### Gaps
- No pre-1914 British source found that gives minute-by-minute operation times (for example minutes per frame to build, braze or file). The Cycle Trader and Review article on Rudge-Whitworth's works (28 July 1911, pp. 214–22) and the articles on Raleigh (30 March 1911; 6 Oct. 1911) cited by Harrison probably hold such detail but are not online.
- No source found for stove batch sizes (frames per stove load), the exact composition and temperature of the spelter bath, or pickling-bath chemistry in British cycle works.
- Tube cutting and mitring (saws, mitring cutters) is mentioned only as "tubes cut to length and mitred". No description of the machines was found.
- Frame truing on surface plates after brazing: only the 1897 "jigger table" is documented. No 1910s British description of setting or alignment checks was found.
- A search summary attributed to Grace's Guide describes an earlier Rudge works: an Otto gas engine; an enamelling shop "with all requisite dipping tanks and three large stoves"; bobbing and mopping shops; a plating shop with vats and dynamo; and a brazing shop on the upper floor with open fires and concrete floors. I could not find this text on the current Grace's Guide page, and its date (probably 1880s–90s) is unverified.

## 2. Which components did a Coventry assembler make in-house versus buy in, and how did this vary by firm size?

### Takeaway
By 1910–12 even the leading Coventry makers bought tyres, rims (often), chains, steel tube, bar, wire, balls, saddles, lamps, bells and most small screws. They made frames, forks, lugs (machined, or pressed in the largest firms), hubs, bottom brackets, cups, cones, axles, cranks and chainwheels, brakework and wheels. They did all their own polishing, plating and enamelling. Medium firms bought more finished components. Small assemblers bought nearly everything and only "finished" (polished, enamelled, plated, assembled), or sent the plating and enamelling out.

### Cited Findings
- 1912: "The 'Cycle Manufacturers' do not make their own supplies of tyres, wheel rims, chains, accessories — bells, lamps, etc." These come from specialists, "e.g., Dunlop Tyre Company for tyres, wheel rims, pumps, etc.; Lucas Ltd. for lamps, bells, etc.; Perry and Company for cycle chains". The manufacturers "purchase their supplies of steel tubes, bars, wire, accessories, etc., and thence produce the finished cycle. This branch of the industry is centred at Coventry". Parts and accessories are centred in Birmingham — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- 1921: the large factory makes everything "except saddles, handles, tyres, and toolbags". Even so, it buys "Small screws, nuts, steps, bolts… from specialists who can make these parts from steel bars in automatic lathes at very much lower prices". Spokes "are supplied headed, bent, and screwed". Mudguards are "rolled by specialists". Handlebars are "largely bent by specialists" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft). This conflicts in part with Carter (1912), who has spokes made in the wheel shop by "semi-skilled machine minders" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Firm-size classes (1921). The **small maker** "buys a frame from one place, or the tubes and lugs ready to build the frame, the wheels complete, minus tyres, come from another specialist; handlebars and seat pillars from another". His work "consists largely of polishing, enamelling, plating, and assembling, or… finishing". The **medium-sized factory owner** "makes most of the parts himself but purchases a number of finished or partly finished components". The **very large** firm does "all the various preliminary processes on their own premises" — [Grew 1921, ch. VI](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- An assembler's plant "consists mostly of enamelling, polishing, and plating conveniences and sundry bench tools and jigs" (Grew 1921, ch. XVI) — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Sources of materials (1921): tubing mainly from Birmingham (Tubes Ltd group). Bar steel for bearings from Sheffield. Brake and saddle springs from Redditch. Saddles from Coventry, Birmingham and Walsall. Tyres "almost wholly… Birmingham". Castings and stampings from Coventry, Birmingham, Walsall, Dronfield, Oakengates. Rims from Birmingham and Coventry. Enamels from Wolverhampton, Birmingham, Bristol, London. Nickel-plating materials from Birmingham. Swedish steel blocks for tube. Balls from Sweden. South Wales tinplate for chain cases. German celluloid. Machine tools from Yorkshire, Coventry, Birmingham, America and (pre-war) Germany. Present-day mechanics "have largely to fit together what is made for them by other producers" — [Grew 1921, ch. IV](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Smith's Stamping Works built a factory at Red Lane, Coventry (1896) "just so as to be near the Coventry cycle manufactories". It made forged cycle cranks, chain and sprocket wheels, fork ends, brackets, hubs and tube joints — [Harrison 1977, p. 141](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- Small entrants relied on "buying cycle parts from the component and accessory producers, and the sending of work out — especially nickel plating and enamelling — to be performed by specialist operatives" — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- Perry & Co. and BSA supplied "sets of fittings for small makers, who were thus enabled to make bicycles with the engineering part that required first-class machinery largely eliminated" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Coventry-Eagle (Hotchkiss, Mayo & Meek, Coventry) built bicycles "assembled from bought-in parts" — [Grace's Guide: Coventry-Eagle](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Coventry-Eagle)
- Supplier scale c. 1913–14:
  - Sturmey-Archer was producing 100,000 three-speed hubs a year by 1913, manufactured by Raleigh — [Grace's Guide: Sturmey-Archer](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Sturmey-Archer)
  - Perry & Co. (1914) made cycle chains, free wheels, hubs, bells and accessories, with 2,000 employees — [Grace's Guide: Perry](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Perry_and_Co)
  - Brampton Bros made chains and fittings (about 1,000 employees in 1897) — [Grace's Guide: Brampton](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Brampton_Brothers)
  - Accles & Pollock (1914) made "steel tubular parts for cycles", with 700 employees — [Grace's Guide: Accles & Pollock](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Accles_and_Pollock)
  - The Patent Butted Tube Co. (Reynolds) was founded in 1898 and published its first cycle tubing catalogue in 1902 — [Grace's Guide: Reynolds](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Reynolds_Tube_Co)
  - Hoffmann made steel balls at Chelmsford from 1898 — [Grace's Guide: Hoffmann](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Hoffmann_Manufacturing_Co)
- Chain making was fully automated by 1914 (Renold): rivets at 1,100 per hour and "five feet of cycle chain was produced per minute". Perry's Tyseley chain works (1907) had "300 machines… in the automatic manufacture of chain rollers" and stamps running at 80 strokes a minute — [Harrison 1977, pp. 328–30](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- Raleigh (Nottingham, 1911–14) made its own cups, cones, free-wheel parts and ball races on automatics, and had a three-speed gear shop. This is an example of the most integrated end — [Harrison 1977, pp. 328–9](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- A 1913 amateur guide shows bought-in "sets of parts": frame (obtainable "enamelled in any shade"), nickel-plated wheels, Hyde free wheel, handlebars with Phillips "Vox Populi" roller-lever brakes, chainwheel, cranks, pedals, Hans Renold chain, mudguards, seat pillar and Brooks saddle — [Henry 1913](https://archive.org/details/cyclebuildingrep00henr)

### Inferences
- For a fictional mid-sized Coventry works (300–500 workers, about 200 bicycles a week) the sources support this split. **Made in-house:** frames and forks (bought tube and lugs, lugs machined or pressed in-house), hubs, bottom brackets, cups, cones, axles, cranks, chainwheels, seat pillars, handlebar stems, brakework, all polishing, plating, enamelling and lining, wheel building, assembly, and probably case-hardening. **Bought in:** tube (Accles & Pollock, Reynolds, Tubes Ltd), forged or pressed lug blanks and stampings (e.g. Smith's Stamping Works), rims, spokes, nipples, steel balls, chains (Renold, Perry, Brampton, Coventry Chain Co.), free-wheels (Perry, BSA, Hyde), three-speed hubs (Sturmey-Archer, or BSA), saddles (Brooks, Lycett, Middlemore), tyres (Dunlop and others), Bowden brake parts, bells and lamps (Lucas), toolbags, small screws and nuts, enamels, and nickel salts and anodes.
- Grew's 1921 claim that a large works bought only saddles, handles, tyres and toolbags describes the most integrated firms (Raleigh, Rudge-Whitworth, BSA). Carter's 1912 statement fits typical Coventry practice better.

### Gaps
- No inventory or bill of materials for a specific Coventry firm c. 1913 was found that says which items it made and which it bought.
- No source quantifies the share of bought-in cost.

## 3. What machinery, tools, jigs, fixtures and gauges were used, and how was power delivered?

### Takeaway
Machine shops used capstan or turret lathes and automatics (many by Alfred Herbert of Coventry, others American), special hub-turning and hub-drilling lathes, rim drillers, sprocket-milling machines and presses. Frame shops used frame jigs and erecting beds, angular drilling vices and universal lug-boring jigs, brazing hearths (coke beds with gas and compressed-air blowpipes) or dip pots, pickling vats and sand-blast. Finishing used emery wheels and belts, calico mops, plating vats with dynamos, and gas stoves. Each part was checked with gauges. Power came from gas (or oil) engines through line shafting. From about 1907 leading firms moved to electric motors driving groups of machines (Rudge-Whitworth used 23 motors).

### Cited Findings
- Alfred Herbert (Coventry) built special cycle tools. They included a hub-from-bar tool (summer 1895) producing hubs "at the then 'rapid speed' of six to eight steel hubs, four inches long, per hour", and a tool for "boring the four tube holes in bottom brackets" simultaneously. A rim and mudguard rolling machine followed in 1896, a two-spindle hub driller, and an automatic bracket-boring machine in 1897. Buyers of Herbert's No. 4 capstan lathe (1895) included Raleigh, Rudge-Whitworth, Triumph, the Coventry Machinists' Co., J. K. Starley, Centaur and Bayliss-Thomas — [Harrison 1977, p. 145](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- Triumph's (Coventry) workshops in 1905 were "filled by automatic machine tools, the majority of which were of American origin". In 1904 Premier spent heavily "chiefly for purchases of automatic machinery". J. V. Pugh of Rudge-Whitworth invented an automatic machine for cycle wheel rims in 1904 — [Harrison 1977, p. 327](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- Raleigh in 1911 made cups, cones, free-wheel parts and ball races "on automatic machines chiefly of Alfred Herbert's manufacture". Its own semi-automatic milling machines formed chainwheel teeth "on 396 chainwheels each at one time" — [Harrison 1977, p. 328](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- 1897 tools: Roper's angular sliding vice for drilling lugs and brackets at set angles; "after being once set, a boy can accurately bore any quantity of brackets". Hatton's "universal cycle jig" bores all lugs "by a comparatively unskilled mechanic". Also emery band machines (Sterne & Co.), polishing and buffing lathes, a "wheel erector", a "factory inflator", erecting stands, tube clamps, flexible steel mandrels and roller blocks for bending tube, a double-cased gas enamelling stove, and Fletcher Russell gas blowpipes and gas furnaces. Premier designed and built its own automatic rim-drilling, hub-drilling, hub-turning and sprocket-milling machines — [Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog)
- "A high-class cycle factory possesses some of the best brains in light mechanical engineering". The drawing office designs "all the tools for the rapid production of the parts". Toolmakers produce "the jigs, templates, dies, etc." — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Each part is checked by viewers "provided with most accurate gauges which are tried on every part". By 1921 rims and some threads were standardised through the Engineering Standards Committee — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft). Cycle screw threads were standardised in 1901, and chains and chainwheels in 1897–8, by the Cycle Engineers' Institute group — [Harrison 1977, p. 335](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- Finishers' bench tools included the "dummy, or easy fitting tap", "a hand tapping machine, and a similar hand machine for screwing the outsides of bolts", the counting ball-scoop, and nipple keys "turned by hand or power on the end of a flexible shaft" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- **Power.** Rudge-Whitworth "found that electrical driving from 23 motors yielded a bigger and better output over that yielded by the gas engines formerly used", with cooler, cleaner shops and saved floor space (The Engineer, Jan. 1907). "This innovation, pioneered by Rudge-Whitworth in the cycle trade, was soon adopted by other cycle manufacturing concerns". Enfield in 1914 used 41 Westinghouse motors. Renold used 65 motors of 12–20 hp "via shafting and chains" — [Harrison 1977, p. 332](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- 1912: cycle works keep "a staff of mechanics and enginemen to look after the motive power, usually gas or oil" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Brazing gas came from town supply or works producer-gas plants (Dowson) (1897), and from works gas plants at "several of the larger cycle firms" (1890s) — [Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog); [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- Rudge-Whitworth set up a research and testing laboratory in 1901 under H. L. Heathcote, testing raw materials, brazing brass, plating and enamel. In 1902–03 it took "micro-photographs of every screw thread on the Rudge-Whitworth bicycle" — [Harrison 1977, pp. 333–4](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)

### Inferences
- A plausible 1913 mid-sized Coventry works would have run a gas engine (town gas or producer gas) with overhead line shafting and belts, possibly partly converted to group-drive electric motors. Both were documented in Coventry cycle works at the time. Brazing hearths, stoves and plating dynamos sat outside the shafting system or were belt-driven from it (dynamos).
- For 3D staging the sources give these visual props: rows of capstan lathes and automatics under belted shafting; drilling machines with angle jigs; the frame-jig table; coke-bed brazing hearths with gas and air pipes and a compressor; acid vats; a sand-blast bench with masked operators; emery wheels and belts; calico mops; lead-lined plating vats with anode rods and a dynamo; dip tanks and drain pans; gas stoves with hanging rods; lining benches; pillar-topped assembly benches; a viewer's bench with weighing machine and book; wrapping tables; and a crate-making carpentry shop.

### Gaps
- No machine-count inventory (lathes, automatics, presses) for any Coventry cycle works c. 1913 was found.
- No pre-war British source describes surface plates and setting gauges for frame truing.
- No horsepower figure was found for a mid-sized cycle works' engine.

## 4. How was inspection (viewing) done at each stage, and how were frames numbered?

### Takeaway
"Viewers" gauged parts as they came back into stores from each producing shop, and returned faulty parts for rectification or scrapped them. A final viewer weighed every finished machine, entered its number in a book and tested bearings, chain, steering, brakes and tyres before wrapping. Frame numbers were stamped into the frame. Rudge-Whitworth stamped under the bottom bracket before 1928; Raleigh stamped under the seat lug. The exact production step at which the number was stamped is not documented.

### Cited Findings
- "When production is proceeding, each part is inspected by viewers. This department is a most important one… The viewer may in some cases be quite a subordinate, but he or she is provided with most accurate gauges which are tried on every part; with lightning like quickness the gauge detects inaccurate workmanship, an error or wear of tools, and back go the faulty parts to the producer to be rectified or they may, on detection, have to be scrapped altogether" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Completed frames, wheels and forks "go when completed to the finished stores; here they are viewed" before re-issue to finishing — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Final viewer: "This man is, or should be, a practical cyclist as well as a practical mechanic. He seizes the machine and after weighing it, entering its number in a book, and feeling the steering, he proceeds to test all the bearings and the chain for correct adjustment, bangs the machine up and down and about, tries the brakes, pinches the tyres, runs an eye over all, and finally passes it along" to wrapping — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- 1912 Coventry job titles include "cycle testers", along with liners and wheel truers, who "require a considerable period as learners before they become helpers and then full rate workers" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- The 1881 Singer works had an "examining" department — [Harrison 1977, p. 141](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- English high-grade practice (American Machinist, 1899): "Each machine is a special product… No work is rushed wholesale through the shop. In each case, the bearings are tested" — [Harrison 1977, p. 326](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- The GPO "employs a special staff to control its bicycle contracts, to supervise and inspect their manufacture and repair… at the factories it favours with its contracts" (outside inspectors on government work) — [Grew 1921, ch. XV](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Frame-number location (collector sources). Rudge-Whitworth: frame number "stamped under the bottom bracket until 1927, and from 1928 at the top of the seat tube" (search summary of collector pages) — [Retrobike: Rudge-Whitworth frame numbers](https://www.retrobike.co.uk/threads/rudge-whitworth-frame-numbers.326293/latest). Raleigh: number "located underneath the seat lug" or on the back of the seat tube just below the seat lug. Raleigh dating list: 1912 = 375,693; 1913 = 427,829; 1914 = 482,851 — [Menotomy Vintage Bicycles: Raleigh serial numbers](https://menotomymaps.com/bikepix/ra_sn.php)
- Perry's chain works (1907): "Every operator works to a gauge so that the assembling of the various parts becomes practically automatic and no rejections are necessary" (a trade-press claim) — [Harrison 1977, p. 328](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)

### Inferences
- The Raleigh serial gap from 1913 to 1914 (about 55,000) matches Raleigh's reported output of about 60,000 a year in 1913 ([Lenton Listener](https://www.lentontimes.co.uk/images/gallery/raleigh/raleigh_listener_10_11.htm)). Serials therefore ran as one continuous sequence, one per frame.
- The number is struck into bare steel and stays legible under enamel. It was therefore almost certainly stamped after brazing and cleaning, and before enamelling: at frame building, at the post-filing view, or on entry to the finished stores. It could be stamped on the bracket shell before brazing. This is an inference; no source states the step.
- Viewing points for the simulation: (a) incoming materials (Rudge's lab tested brazing brass and raw stock); (b) machine-shop parts gauged on return to stores; (c) frames, forks and wheels viewed in finished stores before enamelling and plating; (d) final viewer for each complete machine (weigh, log number, test). The GPO or War Office inspector is an optional extra for contract machines.

### Gaps
- No scrap or reject rates were found for any British cycle works.
- No primary pre-1914 source was found stating where Coventry makers other than Rudge-Whitworth stamped frame numbers (Singer, Swift, Premier, Triumph, Humber), or at which step.
- Whether the final check included a test ride on the road or track is not documented in the sources found. Grew describes a bench and handling check only.

## 5. How were finished bicycles packed for despatch and stored as finished stock?

### Takeaway
After viewing, women and girls (or youths) greased each machine and wrapped bright and enamelled parts in paper strips or "butter cloth" tied with string. Machines going by passenger train went just wrapped. Those going by goods train went in narrow individual wooden crates made by the works carpenters, sometimes several crates inside an outer crate. Export machines were dismantled and packed several to a metal-lined case. Makers avoided large finished stocks, built to order late in the season, and pushed stock out to their own town depots in December to March.

### Cited Findings
- After the viewer the machine passes "to the women and girls who proceed to grease it, wrap strips of paper or butter cloth all over it and tie it up with string. If it is going overseas and the journey is long, the machine may have to be dissembled to enable it to be packed with others in metal lined cases. For passenger train in the British Isles it goes forward wrapped in paper or cloth and for goods train in a crate, either singly or with two or three others" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- "For transit by goods train in Great Britain each machine occupies a narrow wood crate; by removing pedals and handle-bar it is possible to squeeze a bicycle into a space of 77 in. x 23 in. x 48 in., or 48½ cu. ft. If two or more machines are to travel together the separate crates are sometimes enclosed in another wood crate that is strong enough to hold the lot and to allow it to be slung" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- A 1913 cycle crate was made "from material about 2 in. by 1 in. Approximately about 90 ft. of wood will be required for a full size machine", nailed with wire nails, with handlebars turned in line with the machine — [Henry 1913](https://archive.org/details/cyclebuildingrep00henr)
- New frames were supplied with "tubes… bound round with pieces of paper to protect the enamel" (1913) — [Henry 1913](https://archive.org/details/cyclebuildingrep00henr)
- 1912 Coventry: youths and sometimes females "wrap the bright and enamelled parts with cloth ready for the workmen who pack the cycles for delivery". "Some of the leading firms employ a minimum of female workers just for wrapping finished cycles". Works employ "warehousemen to receive and dispatch goods; carpenters to make crates" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Stock policy (1912): "makers and dealers do not care to produce or carry heavy stocks. They work as closely to actual orders as possible". "As the season draws on to a close, makers produce cycles, etc., only on orders, so as to avoid being left with stocks". All leading makers had depots "in half-a-dozen of the principal towns" carrying stocks. "From December until March the works are very busy stocking their 'depots'" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)

### Inferences
- Packed-goods area for the simulation: a viewer's bench, then greasing and wrapping tables (paper or butter-cloth rolls, string), then a carpenters' crate shop (2 × 1 in battens). Single crates measured about 77 × 23 × 48 in. Pedals and handlebar came off for crating. Export cases were metal-lined, with several dismantled machines each. The despatch bay was at the railway or cart dock (Grew's "railway dray").
- Finished machines were probably held only briefly in a warehouse before despatch. They were not stockpiled, except for the winter build-up of depot stock.

### Gaps
- No description found of cardboard cartons for pre-1914 bicycles. Pre-war sources mention only paper, cloth and wooden crates.
- No figure found for how many finished machines a Coventry works held in its warehouse at a time.

## 6. What were the main skilled trades and job titles, and roughly how many of each?

### Takeaway
Carter's 1912 list (based on Coventry interviews) is the most complete. Trades named: turners and machine minders, pressers, hardeners, frame builders, braziers, filers and sand-blasters, polishers, platers, enamellers, liners, spoke-machine minders, wheel builders and truers, finishers or assemblers, cycle testers, wrappers, packers, toolmakers and toolsetters, pattern makers, smiths, storemen, chargemen, foremen, messengers, enginemen and mechanics, warehousemen and carpenters. Coventry works were mostly male, with youths as learners. Women did little beyond wrapping and light work before the war. No departmental headcounts survive in the sources found. Rudge-Whitworth's 50 brazing hearths in 1911 is the only hard count of a skilled workforce.

### Cited Findings
- Job titles and skill levels (1912):
  - Turnery: automatic machines tended by "youths or women as machine minders, though most of the leading firms employ only male 'turners'".
  - Press shops: need "trained mechanics".
  - "Hardeners" are skilled.
  - Frames: "skilled mechanics, assisted by youths as learners, construct the frames, forks, bars". Brazing is "a task requiring all the skill of a trained 'brazier'".
  - "Filers, or sand blasters"; "polishers"; "platers"; "enamellers" (men with youths); "liners" ("highly skilled… very well paid and much in demand").
  - Wheel shop: spoke "machine minders", youths lacing, "expert wheel builders" who true.
  - "Finishers" or "assemblers" (trained, with learners).
  - "Toolmakers and toolsetters", "pattern makers", "storemen", "'Charge-men'… over a set of machines or workers, foremen [who] superintend the individual 'shops,' messengers, usually youths", "mechanics and enginemen", "warehousemen", "carpenters to make crates", "smiths", "packers".
  
  — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Training: toolmakers, pattern makers, mechanics and carpenters served years as learners and improvers. "Platers, braziers, enamellers, finishers" started as helpers. "Liners, wheel truers, cycle testers, require a considerable period as learners". "Braziers, polishers, mechanics" often trained in other hardware or engineering trades. Wrapping, dress-guard lacing, fitting tyres and accessories, scrubbing parts for plating and rough polishing were "'picked up' by youths and girls in a few days" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Coventry female labour: "In the Coventry and Redditch district relatively little female labour is employed in cycle manufacture". In 1907 "only 602 females were employed in the cycle and motor manufacture" in Coventry, against 1,409 of 4,736 employees in Birmingham cycle and motor factories. "The firms located in the Coventry district are confining their employees more and more to skilled male workers, with youths as learners" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Contrast (post-war, 1921): "there are few large factories where girls and women are not found in practically every department… female polishers, enamellers, wheel builders, press minders, platers" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft). Industry-wide, 25% of the cycle labour force was female by 1911 (9.3% in 1901), and 16.9% of cycle and motor wage-earners were under 18 in 1907 — [Harrison 1977, p. 331](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- Grew's staff categories: storekeepers (one per stores), frame builders, brazers, sand-blasters, iron polishers, machine-shop operatives, wheel builders, polishers, platers, enamellers, finishers, viewers, packers, plus drawing office, toolroom, progress staff ("looks after the progress of the work through the various shops and keeps account of the numbers of parts produced each week"), millwrights, managers, foremen and clerks — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- Normal week: "fifty-four hours" (1912). Most production sections were on piecework, while staff, toolmakers, enginemen and packers were on time rates. Average adult male earnings per 54-hour week were cited for braziers (~35s), turners (~36s), filers and sand-blasters (~30s), enamellers (30s), liners (38–40s), finishers (36–38s) and wheel truers (37s). Example piece rates: "10½d. per pair of wheels finished, 6d. for polishing a handle-bar, 3d. each for forks, 7d. for gear sets, 6d. for brake sets and parts" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft) (included here only as a basis for estimating output rates)
- Hard counts: Rudge-Whitworth had 50 open brazing hearths (1911) — [Harrison 1977, p. 336](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/). In 1891 the Quinton Cycle Co. (Coventry) "employed 380 men in fitting shop" with "over 5,000 machines yearly output" (as summarised from VCH) — [VCH Warwick vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)
- Firm sizes and output (for calibration):
  - Rudge-Whitworth (1906): 1,800 workers, about 75,000 machines a year.
  - Swift (1896): "nearly 1,000 workmen", "weekly production of 700 machines".
  - Premier (1896): 600 hands. Coventry made 310,000 cycles in 1906. About 5,800 Coventry workers were in cycles in 1911.
  
  — [VCH Warwick vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189). Other firm sizes: Rudge employed 764 hands in Coventry in 1893. Firm workforces in the 1890s included Premier 1,100 (1896), Humber 1,000 (1896), Swift 1,000 (1896) and Rudge-Whitworth 1,200 (1895) — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- Raleigh (Nottingham) output was 60,000 a year by 1913 — [Lenton Listener 1981](https://www.lentontimes.co.uk/images/gallery/raleigh/raleigh_listener_10_11.htm)
- Coventry firms' German branches (labelled German): Premier, Nuremberg, 1906: 47,800 bicycles with 510 workers. Triumph, Nuremberg, 1906: 21,000 bicycles with 350 workers — [Grace's Guide: Premier](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Premier_Cycle_Co); [Grace's Guide: Triumph](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Triumph_Cycle_Co)

### Inferences
- Output per worker. Rudge-Whitworth 1906 made about 42 machines per worker per year (about 0.8 a week). Swift 1896 made about 0.7 per worker per week. A works of 300–500 making about 200 a week (about 10,000 a year) runs at 0.4–0.67 per worker per week. That fits a firm doing much in-house, making high-grade machines, or also making motorcycles or cars, as most Coventry firms were by 1913.
- **Estimated** headcount for a 400-person works making 200 bicycles a week. This is my allocation, not a source. Stores and messengers 20. Machine shop and automatics 70. Press and hardening 15. Toolroom and pattern shop 20. Frame building 15. Brazing 10, i.e. about 5–8 hearths. Filing and sand-blast 20. Iron and plate polishing 35. Plating 15. Enamelling 20. Lining and transfers 8. Wheel shop 30. Brakework, mudguard and handlebar benches 15. Finishing and assembly 35. Viewers and testers 10. Wrapping and packing 15. Carpenters and crates 5. Warehouse and despatch 10. ## 7. What was the typical lead time from raw materials to finished bicycle, and how much work in progress existed?

### Takeaway
No source gives a measured throughput time. Works ran to a yearly cycle. New models were fixed in October and tools made in October and November. The Show came in late November. The works were in motion by the end of December. December to March went to stock orders, and March to May ran at full pressure with overtime. August to early November was slack. Machined and pressed parts were made to stock well ahead (they were in the stores before assembly was ordered). Frames, wheels and finish were made in batches (e.g. 100) once enough stock had collected. The finishing trades varied most with demand. Work in progress therefore sat mainly as stocks of parts in stores, which were deliberately buffered, not on the shop floor.

### Cited Findings
- Planning in a very large works (1921): directors "arrange for a production of, say, 1,000 machines a week for six months. The requisitions for material go through the buying department… and in a reasonable time… the parts commence to collect in the various stores throughout the factory. When the stock is sufficiently forward to ensure a regular supply to the various shops; the stores begin to issue the orders to build and assemble, paint and finish". A progress staff "keeps account of the numbers of parts produced each week, so that an equal number of front forks and handle-bars… are available". Without it "there might be 1,000 bicycles ready for delivery in a given week and no handle-bars" — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- "1,000 machines per week can be made with the same staff, or approximately the same staff, as is required for 500 machines per week" (Grew on overheads and scale) — [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)
- The yearly cycle (1912): "In October cycle makers decide upon the models for the coming season. Pattern-makers and toolmakers are at once set to work… [models] are exhibited at the 'Show' late in November… most branches of the factories are set in motion by the end of December. From December until March the works are very busy stocking their 'depots' and supplying the large stock orders… from March until the end of May the works are running at fullest pressure… the busiest period at the works is from February until the end of May, when much overtime is worked. In June and July the works are busy according to the volume of orders… With the close of July pressure has fallen off… the close of August, September, October, and early November represents the slackest period… short time is being worked" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Which departments carry stock (1912): "Turners, pressers, and those making parts by means of automatic machinery can be kept on producing for stock for some time. For whatever the new models may be as regards design of frames, the turned and pressed parts are much the same and can be safely stocked". "The sections wherein hand labour is most important… plating, enamelling, wheel building, finishing, wrapping, packing, and also the filers, truers, and polishers" feel the variation "earliest and most of all" — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Automatic machinery let makers meet a season's demand "so rapidly, that the season's demand could be easily met by working the factories at full pressure during the season alone". "Formerly, the manufacture of a cycle took a much longer period" (1912) — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- Firms' financial years ended July to September, and materials contracts ran to 30 September (1912) — [Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)
- At the 1896 boom peak, makers quoted delivery of six weeks, and Premier (Coventry) "could not deliver for certain all of its orders-on-hand under three months" — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)
- BSA's cycle component department ran night shifts in 1897 until demand fell. Shift working is documented as an option — [Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)

### Inferences
- Rough throughput estimate for one batch, built up from the process timings above. The source timings are cited; the totals are mine. Frames for a 100-machine batch: building and pegging plus brazing take about 1–2 days at 5–8 hearths. Pickling and filing take 1–2 days. Polishing takes about 1 day. Viewing in stores, under 1 day. Enamelling takes 2–3 coats at 1–1.5 h stoving each, plus rubbing down: 1–2 days. Lining and transfers (short stoves) take about 1 day. Wheels and plating run in parallel over 2–4 days. Finishing, viewing and wrapping for 100 machines take 1–2 days. So **about 1–2 weeks from issue of tubes and lugs to packed machines**, with machined parts drawn from stock made weeks or months earlier. Raw-material lead times from Birmingham and Sheffield suppliers would come on top.
- WIP for the simulation is best shown as: full rough-stores bins; large finished-stores racks of turned parts (cups, cones, axles, hubs) built up over the winter; a few batches of 100 frames moving through brazing, filing, polishing and enamelling; and frames hanging in stoves. Seasonal staffing is possible: full in February to May, short time in August to November.

### Gaps
- No source states throughput time for a frame or bicycle, or the size of WIP in a British cycle works.
- No information found on whether Coventry cycle works ran night shifts in 1913 (Triumph motorcycles "have been working day and night", Carter 1912, but this refers to motorcycles).
