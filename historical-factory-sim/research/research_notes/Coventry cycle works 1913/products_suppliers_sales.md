# Products, suppliers, prices and sales channels of a mid-sized Coventry cycle works, c. 1913

Scope note: what a Coventry cycle maker of c.1913 sold (range, specs, retail prices), how and to whom it sold (agents, factors, stores, export), on what terms, and what it bought from whom. Manufacturing processes, office forms, wages law, transport and company finance are out of scope and covered by others.

Key source types used:
- **Trade-journal show report (primary).** *Cycle and Motor Trades Review* report of the cycle show at Olympia, November 1910, covering the 1911 season. Grace's Guide transcribes it in sub-pages for cycles, accessories and tyres. Some prices in it are OCR-garbled; those are flagged where they occur.
- **Contemporary book.** W. F. Grew, *The Cycle Industry* (Pitman, 1921), full text on archive.org.
- **Business history.** R. Lloyd-Jones and M. J. Lewis, *Raleigh and the British Bicycle Industry, 1870-1960* (Ashgate, 2000). It draws on Raleigh archives, Harrison (1969) and the Board of Trade trade returns. I read the full text via the dokumen.pub copy; its numeric tables were extracted from flattened text, so column alignment is flagged where it is uncertain.
- **Victoria County History.** *VCH Warwickshire*, vol. 8 (Coventry), section on cycle manufacture.
- **Directories and museum pages.** Grace's Guide company pages, which quote the *1914 Whitaker's Red Book* for employees and specialities, and Online Bicycle Museum pages that quote catalogue prices.

## 1. Typical catalogue range, specifications, options and retail prices (c.1910–1914); hire purchase / easy payments

### Takeaway
A Coventry maker of the period typically listed 8–20 models in three or four "grades". These ran from a cheap "Popular" or "Special" at about £5–£7, through a "Standard"/"Royal" grade at about £7–£9, to a "Tourist" or "Imperial/Grand/de Luxe" at 10–15 guineas, which carried a Sturmey-Archer (or Armstrong) 3-speed and an oil-bath gear case. Each grade came in gent's and lady's (dropped-frame) patterns, alongside road and path racers, an "all-weather" all-black model, juveniles, a tradesman's carrier (about 8–10 gns), and sometimes tricycles and tandems. Bells, lamps and carriers were sold as extras.

Easy payments were real but documented mostly for the big firms: Raleigh ran a "Gradual Payment" system through its depots from c.1906, and Mead sold by mail order. I found no Coventry-specific 1913 HP terms sheet.

**Option pricing and accessories**
- Raleigh's pre-war extra charge for a 3-speed gear attachment was **21s** ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- Older catalogue structure, 1898 Raleigh list (OCR-flattened table): models were priced in variants "a) Without Brakes and Mudguard / b) With Brakes and Mudguard / c) With Brakes Only / d) With Mudguards Only". For example, the Radford light roadster was about £20 / £22 / £21 5s / £20 5s ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis, Table B.5](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- Accessories: "saddles, pedals, and tool-bags ... are always included in the price of a bicycle, yet bells and lamps are seldom, if ever, thrown in" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).
- Exception: Royal Enfield's 1911 "Modele Riche" was "marketed with lamp, bell and rear carrier" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).
- Sturmey-Archer was producing **100,000 three-speed hubs a year by 1913** ([Grace's Guide – Sturmey-Archer Gears](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Sturmey-Archer_Gears)).

**Carriers, tricycles and juveniles**
- Tradesman's carriers were a major show theme. Rover's was 10 gns, Kynoch's 8 gns. Day Mfg's "Dayton Armstrong" was "guaranteed to sustain a load of no less than fifteen hundredweight". F. & H. Melen of Birmingham supplied boxes and baskets for "fishmongers, dairymen, grocers, butchers, chemists, electricians". The trade press urged that carriers "ought to be considerably more pushed by the average agent" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).
- A 1913 "Tradesman's Humber No 19" brochure exists, as images only ([tradesmansbike – Humber brochures](https://tradesmansbike.wordpress.com/brochures-humber/)).
- The G.P.O. used bicycles and "in some towns carrier tricycles", enamelled G.P.O. red, bought on contract with factory inspection ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).
- Juvenile makers: "At one time the firm of Townend Bros., Ltd. [Coventry] held almost a monopoly"; by c.1920 Humber, Mascot and others specialised in them ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).

**Easy payments / hire purchase**
- Raleigh introduced "a gradual payment system, administered via the company's sales depots" around 1906. By November 1907 Bowden complained of "the large sums locked up in the gradual payment system".
- A "Gradual Payment Department" appears as a separate line in Raleigh's 1910–14 trading accounts.
- After 1901 the trade's revival was "accompanied by improved selling techniques such as better trade catalogues and easy payment schemes".
- Earlier precedents: in 1876 a Chelsea dealer offered £5 cash plus £2 instalments, or 5s off for cash; in 1892 Raleigh ran 12–18-month instalment credit through its agents; in 1898 it announced "a new system of hire purchase" to agents.
- Source for all of the above: [Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html).
- Mead Cycle Co. ("of Coventry and Birmingham", offices at 35–39 Wapping, Liverpool) was a large mail-order/direct seller whose "catalogue includes everything that a cyclist could possibly need". It sold the "Coventry Flyer" at 15 gns in 1905 ([Grace's Guide – Mead Cycles](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Mead_Cycles)). A 1912 Mead advert offered a "10 day free trial" ([PeriodPaper listing, search snippet](https://www.periodpaper.com/products/1912-ad-mead-cycles-10-day-trial-ranger-bicycle-model-original-advertising-067677-tw1-155)).

### Inferences
- **A plausible 1913 catalogue for a mid-sized Coventry firm** (modelled on Calcott, Premier and Swift; prices 1911-season adjusted only slightly, since Grew's "£8 8s" in 1914 implies little change):

  | Model | Grade | Price | Notes |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | No. 1 de Luxe | Top | 12–13 gns | Sturmey-Archer 3-speed, oil-bath case, Brooks saddle, Renold chain, 22-ct gold lining |
  | Tourist | | 10 gns | 3-speed, detachable oil-bath case |
  | Royal | | £7 10s–£8 15s | 3-speed and metal gear case optional (+21s) |
  | Popular / Special | | £5 10s–£6 15s | Single free-wheel, two rim brakes, second-grade tyres |
  | Road racer / path racer | | £5–£7 | |
  | All-weather (all-black) | | — | Variant of Royal or Imperial |
  | Lady's patterns | | +5s to +£1 | Of each grade |
  | Boy's / girl's juveniles | | £5 5s–£5 15s | |
  | Tradesman's carrier | | 8–10 gns | Name plate, basket, coaster hub |
  | Tricycle / tandem | | 18–20 gns | Only if the firm keeps a tricycle line; 1908 Singer level |
- **Frame and wheel options.** The usual pattern was frame sizes 20"–26" in 1" steps, 28" wheels standard and 26" on some racers and ladies' machines. This rests on the surviving 24"/25"/26" frames and Rudge's five sizes, not on a single catalogue.
- **Colours.** Black with gold lining (gold leaf at the top grade) was the norm. Green ("Standard Green", "Green" models), chocolate (Singer, Minstrel), khaki (BSA "Territorial") and G.P.O. red are attested. "All-weather" models were dull or bright black with celluloid-covered bars and no plating.

### Gaps
- No complete 1913 Coventry catalogue was transcribed online. Online Bicycle Museum shows 1913 Centaur and 1909/1913 Humber pages as images only. A 1913 Armstrong 3-speed catalogue exists ([Sturmey-Archer Heritage](https://www.sturmey-archerheritage.com/index.php?page=history-detail&id=350)) but its prices were not seen.
- I found no 1913 tandem price and no exact 1913 tricycle price; the 1908 Singer 18–20 gns is the latest found.
- The 1914 Gamages catalogue, *Yesterday's Shopping* (about 50 pages of bicycles), is on archive.org but access-restricted ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/yesterdaysshoppi0000gama)). It is a good lead for department-store retail prices of bikes, tyres, saddles, lamps and bells.
- I found no 1913 HP terms (deposit, number of monthly payments, surcharge) for any Coventry firm.

## 2. Trade structure, discounts, credit terms, sole agencies, travellers

### Takeaway
The channel was: maker → (sometimes factor/wholesaler) → local "agent" (cycle dealer, ironmonger, garage); plus direct contracts with stores and co-operatives, mail-order houses, government (G.P.O., War Office), and export houses.

Makers sold to agents at a "trade price" well below catalogue list. 1890s–1901 Raleigh evidence shows list less one-third (or deep cash discounts) and 2½% for cash, but I found no 1913 trade-terms sheet. Travellers sold to agents. The Cycle and Motor Cycle Manufacturers' and Traders' Union (C.M.T.U., Coventry) ran the show and framed rules for trading with agents. Formal retail price maintenance enforcement (the Cycle Trade Union) dates from 1914.

### Cited Findings

**Channel types**
- Grew's production hierarchy ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)):
  - "the small local assembler who makes up an odd machine or two from finished parts purchased from the big factoring houses";
  - "the small maker";
  - "the medium-sized factory owner, who makes most of the parts himself but purchases a number of finished or partly finished components from outside sources";
  - the "very large and complete factory owners".
- Grew on trade suppliers: "Several large factories are now devoted almost exclusively to the production of bicycles for the trade ... and undertake contracts to supply the large stores, co-operative societies, and others with bicycles that are sold under the trader's own name" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).
- **Timerick, 7 Little Park Street, Coventry**: "He makes for the trade, supplying machines with the agent's transfer ... Whilst building for the trade, Mr. Timerick can arrange for a few sole agencies" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).
- F. & H. Melen (Birmingham): "a special list is provided for the use of agents, the name of the makers of the machines not appearing, and agents can have their own transfers attached".
- Robin Hood Cycle Co. (Nottingham): "caters for the trade ... 'The trade for the trader'".
- Factors such as Brown Bros. (Great Eastern St, London) offered complete machines ("Brown", "Hobo", "Marco"), "built-up frames, wheels, and the firm's usual enormous selection of component parts". Other London wholesale houses included Day Mfg, the East London Rubber Co. (whose "Saxon" was built "entirely from B.S.A. fittings") and the Wincycle Trading Co. ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).
- Humphries & Dawes (Birmingham) did "a large trade ... with the best wholesale houses and shippers" in frames and lugs "to customers' own patterns" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).
- The trade press repeatedly addressed agents: "it is the agent who is the sell[er]"; Hobart Bird pushed high grades because they "give the agents better profits ... and ... better goodwill" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).

**Discounts and credit**

All from [Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html) unless noted.
- Raleigh's 1901 "Book of the Raleigh" prices each model at a "List price" and a "Net cash price". The net figure is exactly **two-thirds of list**: £18 18s → £12 12s; £30 → £20; £15 15s → £10 10s; £37 10s → £25.
  - **Ambiguity:** the source does not say whether "net cash" was the agent's price or a cash price to the public.
- 1897 Kestrel proposal: dealers' price "£9 10s., less 2.5 per cent discount for cash". Raleigh would buy Kestrels at "a special 10 per cent discount off 'recognised trade prices'" and market them "on a 5 per cent commission".
- 1898–99: "discounts of 10 per cent had been given for guaranteed orders of 12 machines". Agents were coaxed "off high frames" with bonuses of 5s. Gazelle machines carried "discounts of 20 per cent ... to retailers for cash sales".
- 1890s critique: "large discounts were given on list prices, irrespective of the amount ordered". In contrast "American and continental makers adopted ... a 'net cash pricing system ... not subject to discount'".
- American practice, flagged as such: Pope cut list prices 25% in 1897.
- 1901: Raleigh supplied a "no name machine ... to agents, but not fixing a retail price".
- 1892: "dealers ... had delayed payments on goods dispatched until they could themselves ensure a sale". Raleigh also "alienated small retailers 'who are solely aggrieved at our strict terms of credit'" in Canada.
- Grew on the 1890s boom: "every dealer ordered three times as many bicycles as he expected to sell ... When the off-season for sales arrived those fictitious orders were mostly cancelled" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).

**Travellers**
- Raleigh's success depended on "every official ... including the Managing Director, Secretary, London Manager, Travellers, Staff ... and Foremen" (1900).
- Harold Bowden served as "Raleigh's traveller for Eastern England" (c.1899–1903).
- In 1903–4, missing the show meant "travellers and agents were consequently confused over Raleigh's future pricing policy".
- Source for the three points above: [Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html).
- BSA: "In November, 1893, Mr. Ilston joined the company as traveller, selling B.S.A. hubs and other components" ([Grace's Guide – BSA: Bicycles](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/BSA:_Bicycles)).

**The Union and price maintenance**
- Grew: "The cycle trade has a well organized Union to conduct its annual exhibition, frame regulations for the conduct of its membership in trading with the agents, to watch its interests in Parliament ... The Cycle and Motor Cycle Manufacturers' and Traders' Union ... headquarters are The Towers, Coventry; the manager being Major Watling and the secretary Mr. Timerick" (c.1921) ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).
- C.M.T.U. presidents: 1911 Sir Charles Marston (Sunbeam); 1912–13 Arthur Brampton (Brampton Bros, fittings); 1914–16 Harry Smith ([Grace's Guide – BCMCMTU](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/British_Cycle_and_Motor_Cycle_Manufacturers_and_Traders_Union)). Marston, as "President of the C.M.T.U.", chaired the November 1910 Olympia show dinner ([Grace's Guide – 1910 Exhibition](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition)).
- The Modern Records Centre catalogue says the Cycle Trade Union was "set up on the initiative of the [C.M.T.U.] in 1914 to maintain the 'security of selling value' (i.e. to enforce retail price maintenance), to control auction sales and advertising, and generally to regulate the cycle trade". It was wound up in 1962 ([MRC catalogue MCA/X/1](https://mrc-catalogue.warwick.ac.uk/records/MCA/X/1), via the search-result text; the page itself would not load).
- The Archives Hub description of the Motor Cycle Association, via search snippet: "from approximately 1910 ... the Association was dominated by manufacturers who regulated the wholesale and retail trades in order to maintain manufacturers' pricing policies and terms of trade" ([Archives Hub](https://archiveshub.jisc.ac.uk/search/archives/ec839b9c-2428-3480-9608-5eb5aa635f5f)).
- Later evidence: a motor-cycle price-maintenance agreement among BCMMTU members collapsed in 1923 ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).

### Inferences
- **Trade price.** Agents most plausibly bought at list less 25–33⅓%, with 2½% for prompt cash (monthly account). This is consistent with the Raleigh two-thirds net figures and the 2½% Kestrel term, but not documented for 1913. Model it as the default "trade price list" with a "terms" line, e.g. "Less 25%; 2½% monthly account; carriage paid on orders of 3 machines".
- **Quantity bonus.** A bonus of an extra 5–10% for a guaranteed season order (e.g. 12 machines) is attested in 1898–99 and is a reasonable game mechanic.
- **Sole agency.** The usual pattern was exclusive rights for a town or district in exchange for a minimum season order and carrying the maker's transfers and showcards. Timerick's offer of "a few sole agencies" shows a small Coventry maker did offer them.
- **"Trade" machines.** A mid-sized Coventry firm would likely sell three channels at once:
  - branded machines through appointed agents;
  - "trade" machines with the agent's own transfer (Timerick, Melen);
  - occasional contracts for stores, co-ops or factors.

### Gaps
- I found no 1912–1914 document giving trade discounts or credit terms (monthly account, 2½% settlement, prompt-payment dates) for any British cycle maker. No source on travellers' commission rates or salaries, or on the shape of a traveller's "journey" (route, frequency). The *Cycle and Motor Trades Review* and *Cycling* for 1912–14 would be the places to look.
- I could not verify whether the C.M.T.U. enforced resale prices or stop-lists before the 1914 Cycle Trade Union.

## 3. Ordering, spares and guarantees

### Takeaway
Sources show agents ordered for the season after seeing the November show and the new catalogue, placing orders (some "guaranteed" for a quantity) through travellers or by post. Makers set production targets from those orders. Spares came from makers, factors and specialist component houses, and Grew thought spares unprofitable for big makers. Frame numbers were sequential at major makers and recorded against sales in shop ledgers. I found no detailed guarantee-registration procedure.

### Cited Findings

**Order flow and production planning**
- Raleigh's 1899 season: "in March, although they had 1 500 orders guaranteed, there was an 'unexpected' switch in demand to 'tall frames'". Agents were "fighting shy of placing orders for immediate delivery" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- Production planning from targets (same source):
  - Raleigh "purchased materials well in advance" to meet a target of 12,000 machines in 1900, with weekly output "not ... below 400 machines".
  - "For 1901 10,000 sets of parts were sanctioned to meet an expected output of 16,000 cycles."
- Grew's description: "a requisition comes from the office management, sales or other department, to put through, we will say, 100 machines of a certain model"; the storekeeper then issues "100 sets of frame lugs ... 200 rims" and so on. Large works "arrange for a production of, say, 1,000 machines a week for six months" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).

**Spares**
- Grew: "the supply of repair parts and replacements is not a lucrative part of a big cycle factory's equipment and may be most unremunerative". Makers resisted standardising "cups, cones" and similar parts, fearing "any tinkerer in a country town" would fit inferior standard parts ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).
- Component spares lists existed:
  - a Sturmey-Archer 1913–14 3-speed fitting and adjusting booklet with "Spares list" ([National Motorcycle Museum](https://www.nationalmotorcyclemuseum.co.uk/product/sturmey-archer-1913-to-1914-3-speed-epicyclic-hub-gear-fitting-adjusting-using-plus-illust-spares-list-nessa451/));
  - a BSA fittings catalogue dated February 1913, Parts 1 and 2 (images, [Grace's Guide – BSA: Bicycles](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/BSA:_Bicycles)).
- Factors such as Brown Bros held "enormous selection[s] of component parts" for the trade ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).

**Frame numbers and guarantees**
- Rudge-Whitworth frame numbers by year: 1910 585,010; 1911 626,400; 1912 663,066; **1913 697,524**; 1914 726,731; 1915 740,862. "Marque enthusiasts use records of shop ledgers that recorded dates sold and frame numbers" ([Online Bicycle Museum – frame number dating](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/frame-number-bicycle-dating-guide/)).
- "Fully guaranteed" was a standard selling claim, e.g. Hobart Bird's low-priced "Sensation" "considering that it is fully guaranteed". Raleigh in 1901 could "guarantee every cycle to be built dead true and perfectly brazed" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles); [Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).

### Inferences
- Rudge-Whitworth's frame numbers advanced by about 34,500 between the 1912 and 1913 reference points and about 29,200 in 1913–14. That is a usable proxy for its annual output, roughly 30,000–35,000.
- **Paperwork for the simulation.**
  - A season order from an agent goes on a traveller's order book or a posted order form, by model number, frame size, gear and colour.
  - It is confirmed by a maker's acknowledgement.
  - It is then delivered in batches through the spring, with the frame number recorded on the invoice/advice note and in a frame-number register (number → model → agent → date).
  - Telegrams for urgent repeat orders are plausible; telegraphic addresses were universal (e.g. Rudge-Whitworth's listing with telephone number) but no source describes telegram ordering explicitly.
- A spares price list ("parts list") was standard for component makers (BSA, Sturmey-Archer). For a mid-sized Coventry assembler, agents probably got most spares from factors or the component maker directly; the maker kept frame and fork spares and its own transfers.

### Gaps
- I found no surviving 1910s cycle guarantee card or registration form text, and no stated guarantee period (12 months vs "lifetime" frame).
- I found no direct evidence of telegram ordering, nor of the format of travellers' order books in the cycle trade.

## 4. Annual events and seasonality

### Takeaway
New season models were announced at the November show. The Stanley show ran each November to 1910; from November 1910 the C.M.T.U. held an international cycle and motor-cycle show at Olympia. Agents placed season orders through the winter, and deliveries and retail sales peaked in spring and early summer. Factories had a slack season with a smaller workforce, and juveniles were pushed for Christmas.

### Cited Findings
- **Stanley Show.** Organised by the Stanley Cycling Club from 1878, at the Royal Agricultural Hall, Islington, from 1892 to 1910. "The 34th and final Stanley Cycle Show occurred in November 1910"; it was succeeded by the Olympia motor-cycle show (November 1911) ([Wikipedia – Stanley Cycle Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Cycle_Show)).
- **Olympia, November 1910.** Grace's Guide records the 1910 cycle and motorcycle exhibition as "Held at Olympia in November 1910 ... the first international show of cycles, motor-cycles, and their accessories ... at Olympia", with C.M.T.U. president Marston presiding ([Grace's Guide – 1910 Exhibition](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition)).
  - The two sources may be describing parallel 1910 events; the discrepancy is noted.
- Show reports present "models for the coming season" and "for 1911" in November 1910. They mention agents "having a sharp eye on the Christmas trade" (juveniles and pedal cars), and Swift's practice of a private "special exhibition ... during show week at their Holborn Viaduct depot" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).
- Coventry firms changed patterns each season: "The competitive nature of the cycle trade, stimulated by the practice of introducing revised models for the new season, ensured that changes ... occurred with great frequency". There were "inadequate provision for stocks during the off-season" and "the changing of the patterns at the end of each season" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- **Seasonal labour.** "In 1901 the Raleigh factory employed 550 workers in the slack season and 850 in the busy season" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- **Seasonal ordering.** Agents' guaranteed orders were in by March; agents cleared old stock before buying new; the "off-season for sales" brought cancellations ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html); [Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).
- The C.M.T.U. "conduct[ed] its annual exhibition" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).

### Inferences
- **Calendar for the simulation:**
  - Sept–Oct: design and price-setting for the next season; catalogue printing.
  - November: show week; catalogue and trade price list issued; travellers start their winter journeys.
  - Dec–Feb: season orders and stock-building; juvenile sales for Christmas.
  - Mar–June: peak deliveries and repeat orders.
  - July–Aug: tailing off, clearance and slack season with workforce cuts.
  - Throughout: carrier-cycle and G.P.O./contract business is less seasonal.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the exact dates or venue of the November 1913 Olympia cycle and motor-cycle show. Grace's Guide's "1913 Motor Show" (7–15 Nov 1913) is the car show.

## 5. Export markets, volumes and export practice

### Takeaway
Britain was the world's leading cycle exporter from 1909 to 1913, ahead of Germany, which had led 1901–08. In 1913 Britain exported **147,633 complete cycles worth £609,482** (about £4 2s 7d each), plus a larger value of parts. Markets included India, the Empire (Australia, South Africa), Japan, Scandinavia, Russia, Spain and South America; France was largely closed by tariff.

Exports went through confirming houses and merchants, overseas sole agents, makers' own foreign depots, shippers and wholesale houses, and occasionally foreign branch factories.

### Cited Findings

**Volumes and values**
- UK cycle exports (Table C.1, [Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html), from the trade returns):
  - 1907: 102,399 machines, £508,822 (£4.97 each).
  - 1910: 129,106, £542,511 (£4.20).
  - **1913: 147,633, £609,482 (£4.13 each).**
  - 1916: 58,878.
  - 1918: about 28,000.
  - The text: "the volume of British cycle exports fell from 147 633 machines in 1913 to 58 878 in 1916".
- Gross exports of cycles *and parts* in Table A.3 appear to be about **£2.09m in 1913** (sequence …1,957; 2,014; 2,059; 2,087 for 1910–13, in £000). Values "rose ... by a factor of approximately 3.5 times between 1901 and 1913" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). The table extraction is flattened, so treat the £2.09m with caution.
- World export shares: in 1901 the UK and US each had just over 26% of world exports (then "nearly £2 million"); Germany had nearly 31% and "remained dominant through 1908"; "after that British exports expanded back to dominance which lasted through 1913" ([Petty, ICHC 11](https://www.ichc.biz/proceedings/ICHC-011-24.pdf), citing [Harrison 1969](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1468-0289.1969.tb00174.x)).

**Markets**
- Grew on Europe: "Russia, Spain, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, etc., were all large buyers of British bicycles up to August, 1914". France imposed "an almost prohibitive import tariff on bicycles of about 1s. per pound weight"; "At one time the leading British cycle makers all had big sales depots in Paris, but the Government ... gradually squeezed them out" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).
- Japan:
  - Imported 34,523 bicycles in 1907, falling to 16,000 by 1912.
  - In 1910 "a branch factory of the 'Premier' bicycle company from Coventry England was established on the Dunlop factory grounds" in Kobe. It imported coaster brakes and 3-speeds from England and distributed through Maruishi Shokai.
  - By 1913 Japan still made no ball bearings, spokes or chains.
  - Source: [Petty, ICHC 11](https://www.ichc.biz/proceedings/ICHC-011-24.pdf).
  - Premier's Japanese operation lost money in 1913–15 ([Grace's Guide – Premier Cycle Co](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Premier_Cycle_Co)).
- Humphries & Dawes (Birmingham) were "catering largely for the foreign trade, including especially Japan and Australia". Minstrel & Rea "make a speciality of Colonial and foreign trade". Palmer made a 650×65 mm tyre "for rims of foreign make" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles); [Tyres](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Tyres)).
- Hopper (Barton-on-Humber), per the Wikipedia article summarised in search results: "by 1912 ... exporting bicycles all over the British Empire, with particularly strong export markets in Australia, India, Japan and South Africa" ([Wikipedia – Elswick Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elswick_Hopper); search snippet, page not opened).

**Export mechanics**

All from [Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html) unless noted.
- Raleigh used "confirming houses to cover sales in the rest of the world, especially in colonial markets. One of the most successful ... was the confirming house of Levetus & Co., who handled Raleigh's business in the Punjab, Burma, Nepal and their most important overseas market before 1914, India".
- The London merchants Agar Cross organised distribution "in Argentina and the Far Eastern markets".
- "In Australia, sole agency rights were granted to the Astral Cycle Co. of Melbourne in 1894."
- Raleigh's Canadian agency was the Braverman Hardware Co., Hamilton; it also had a New York depot (1890s).
- 1898: "95 per cent of their foreign business, with the exception of Paris, was conducted on a cash basis", while "American houses offer practically unlimited credit". Raleigh made "special export models at £9 16s. and £9 5s.", and export orders were "about two-thirds of all orders" in April 1898.
- Coventry precedents ([VCH Warwickshire vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)):
  - Coventry Machinists had "an extensive French connection".
  - Singer had a Paris branch (1896).
  - "Starley Brothers had depots in Europe as well as in the U.S.A., Africa, and Japan".
  - Hillman, Herbert & Cooper (Premier) had a German factory from 1888.
  - The Austrian Small Arms Factory made Swift cycles under licence by 1891.
- Premier's Nuremberg works made 44,500 bicycles with 485 workers in 1908. The Triumph Cycle Co.'s German company (Nuremberg) made 20,500 with 305 workers in 1908 and had sales rights for Germany, Austria-Hungary, Switzerland and the Balkans ([Grace's Guide – Premier](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Premier_Cycle_Co); [Grace's Guide – Triumph](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Triumph_Cycle_Co)).
- The UK home market was unprotected before the war. Grew's 33⅓% ad valorem duty on foreign bicycles was a wartime/post-war measure "at the time of writing" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich); [Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html), on the "unprotected home market").

### Inferences
- **Export price level.** Average export value of about £4 2s per machine against home retail of £5–£15 implies exports were mostly lower-grade machines, or were valued f.o.b. at trade prices, often without tyres or saddles.
- **Export trade in the simulation:**
  - indents from confirming houses (Levetus-type) and London merchants, for cash against documents or on the house's confirmation;
  - occasional direct orders from overseas sole agents (Australia, South Africa, Scandinavia);
  - packing in crates "knocked down" for shipment through a forwarding agent or shipper.
  The packing and forwarding mechanics are not covered in these sources.

### Gaps
- I found no total UK cycle output for 1913; Census of Production 1907 cycle figures were not reached. For calibration: Coventry alone made 310,000 cycles in 1906 ([VCH](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)).
- I found no country-by-country 1913 export breakdown, and no source on indent-house commission rates or shipping terms (f.o.b./c.i.f.) for cycles.

## 6. Suppliers to Coventry assemblers: who, where, prices, terms

### Takeaway
Coventry makers bought from a dense Midlands supply network, with a few specialists elsewhere:
- **Tubes:** Accles & Pollock (Oldbury); Patent Butted Tube Co (Reynolds, Birmingham).
- **Fittings and lugs:** BSA (Small Heath); Brampton Bros (Birmingham); Components Ltd (Bournbrook); Humphries & Dawes.
- **Chains:** Renold (Manchester); Coventry Chain Co. (Spon End, Coventry); Perry & Co. (Tyseley, Birmingham); BSA/Eadie.
- **Hubs and free-wheels:** BSA/Eadie (Redditch); Perry; Micrometer Free Wheel Co. (Spon St, Coventry); Sturmey-Archer (Nottingham); Armstrong.
- **Saddles:** Brooks (Birmingham); Lycett (Birmingham); Middlemore & Lamplugh (Coventry).
- **Tyres:** Dunlop; Palmer (London); Clipper; Bates (Leicester); Leicester Rubber "John Bull".
- **Rims:** Westwood; Roman Rims (Birmingham).
- **Brakes:** Bowden Brake Co. (Tyseley).
- **Mudguards:** Bluemel (Wolston near Coventry).
- **Gear cases:** Richmond Gear Case Co. (Coventry).
- **Lamps:** Lucas; Powell & Hanmer; Miller (all Birmingham).
- **Balls and bearings:** Hoffmann (Chelmsford).
- **Plating and presswork:** Coventry Plating & Presswork Co.

Period component prices are sparse: a 21s 3-speed extra, tyres at 2s 6d–10s 6d, and 1890s pedal and handlebar prices. I found no supplier credit terms dated 1912–14.

### Cited Findings

**Context**
- Coventry firms "assembled components and accessories manufactured by the innumerable light-metal works of Birmingham and the Black Country ... saddles came from Walsall, and springs from Sheffield and Redditch" ([VCH](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)).
- "Coventry's reliance on importing cycle components from outside the city" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- Grew: the small maker "buys a frame from one place, or the tubes and lugs ready to build the frame, the wheels complete, minus tyres, come from another specialist; handlebars and seat pillars from another". Even a large works typically bought in "saddles, handles, tyres, and toolbags". "Small screws, nuts, steps, bolts, etc., are usually bought from specialists"; "mudguards are rolled by specialists ... delivered to the makers bright"; spokes come "headed, bent, and screwed" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).

**Tubes**
- Accles & Pollock, Oldbury. 1914: "Specialities: steel tubular parts for cycles, motors, aeroplanes etc. ... Employees 700" ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Accles_and_Pollock)). At the 1910 show it was making Bowden handlebars under arrangement with the Bowden Wire Co. ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Patent Butted Tube Co (later Reynolds Tube Co), Birmingham. Butted tubing was patented in 1897; the company was formed in 1898; its first bicycle-tubing catalogue (1902) boasted "a 4½-pound tube set" ([Grace's Guide – Reynolds Tube Co](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Reynolds_Tube_Co)).

**Fittings and lugs**
- BSA, Small Heath, Birmingham: complete sets of fittings from 1894. "Until 1910, BSA sold only components and bicycles in 'knock-down' form" ([Online Bicycle Museum – BSA fittings](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/tour/bsa-fittings-machines/)). It began building complete cycles in 1908 and acquired Eadie (Redditch) in 1907; it issued a February 1913 fittings catalogue ([Grace's Guide – BSA: Bicycles](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/BSA:_Bicycles)). At the 1910 show BSA/Eadie offered "B.S.A. brakes, handlebars, chains, seat pillars, B.S.A. free wheels and three-speed hub, the Hyde free wheel ... Eadie ... chains and variable gears" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Brampton Brothers, Oliver Street, Birmingham, cycle fittings ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Brampton_Brothers)): "their specialities are met with practically wherever the wide awake maker, factor and dealer is to be found ... absolutely reliable, at right prices" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Components Ltd, Bournbrook, Birmingham: "complete frames and sets of stampings for any type of machine", the Crabbe brake and "Speedily" fork ends. Owner of Ariel; bought Rover's cycle business in 1912 ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles); [Grace's Guide – Components](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Components); [Online Bicycle Museum – Rover](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/19131914-chainless-rover-roadster/)).
- Humphries & Dawes, 4 Lancaster St, Birmingham: "O.K. frames, lug sets, shell fittings", "Swan" fittings, lugs "to any pattern" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).

**Chains**
- Hans Renold Ltd, Progress Works, Brook Street, Manchester: "There is scarcely a machine in the cycle trade which can lay claim to high-class finish which does not possess a Renold chain ... Renold chains are not of the cheapest class" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Coventry Chain Co., Spon End, Coventry: 1914, "Employees 1,500" ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Coventry_Chain_Co)).
- Perry & Co., Tyseley, Birmingham: 1914 specialities "cycle chains, free wheels, hubs, bells and cycle accessories ... Employees 2,000" ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Perry_and_Co)); "Perry Victor chain" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).

**Hubs, free-wheels and gears**
- Sturmey-Archer Gears Ltd, Nottingham (Raleigh-controlled): 100,000 3-speed hubs a year by 1913 ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Sturmey-Archer_Gears)); "tri-coaster" with phosphor-bronze drum (1910 show). Of gear sales in 1906, "Raleigh tak[es] its quota of 25 per cent and the rest being distributed to the trade" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- Armstrong "Triplex" 3-speed: used by Singer, Rover, New Hudson and Star in 1911.
- Micrometer Free Wheel Co., 41 Spon Street, Coventry ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).

**Saddles**
- J. B. Brooks & Co., Criterion Works, Great Charles St, Birmingham: 1914, "Employees 600 to 700" ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/J._B._Brooks_and_Co)). Models: B.10 ("the ever popular road saddle"), B.90 ("for heavyweights"), "Champion", "Champion Flyer" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Lycett Saddle & Motor Accessories Co., Bromley Street, Deritend, Birmingham (1910): saddles and leather goods, "prices ... distinctly moderate" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Middlemore & Lamplugh Ltd, Coventry: saddles ("Rideasy"), tool bags, "Service and Express carriers" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)); formed in 1896 from William Middlemore and Lamplugh & Co ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/William_Middlemore)). Grew notes that tool bags and luggage are "generally made by the saddle firms" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).

**Tyres**
- Dunlop: the trade standard. Rover, Premier, Singer and BSA specified Dunlop, and "Scott" tyres were "manufactured and guaranteed by the Dunlop Company" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).
- Lower-grade tyres: Raleigh's cheapest c.1899 had "lower grade Warwick tyres, rather than Dunlop" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). "Cambridge" tyres appear on cheap models (Singer 1908, Star 1911).
- Palmer Tyre Ltd, Shaftesbury Avenue, London (1914): Palmer cord tyres for cycles ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Palmer_Tyre_Co)).
- Clipper Tyre Co. ("Reflex Clipper"); W. & A. Bates, St Mary's Mills, Leicester, with "Cycle tyres of exceptionally low price—indeed as low as **2s. 6d.** ... up to **10s. 6d.**" ([1910 Tyres](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Tyres)).
- Leicester Rubber Co. ("John Bull"): from 1906 the Burton brothers were "initially selling imported cycle [tyres]" as factors; they began manufacturing at Evington Valley Road only in 1915 ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Leicester_Rubber_Co)).
- Grew lists Dunlop, Palmer, North British (Clincher), Bates, Avon and Midland Rubber, all with "large works and a very complete sales organization" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).

**Rims, brakes, mudguards, gear cases, plating**
- Rims: Westwood Manufacturing Co., Birmingham and Coventry (Westwood rim; sold to Dunlop 1896) ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Westwood_Manufacturing_Co)). Roman Rims Ltd, Upper Priory, Birmingham: jointless aluminium rims, "the quality one" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Brakes: Bowden Brake Co., Kings Road, Tyseley, Birmingham, "makers of cycle brakes" ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Bowden_Brake_Co)); "Bowdenbar", "Bowden" brakes "in the manufacturers' patterns" (1910). Grew: on high-class bicycles brakes "are usually operated by Bowden cables"; the "roller lever brake ... is usually made up by the cycle maker" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).
- Mudguards: C. W. Bluemel & Bros, Wolston near Coventry, "Noweight" celluloid guards and free-wheel covers ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Gear cases: Richmond Gear Case Co., Croft Road, Coventry, celluloid chain covers and a 3-lb tradesman's carrier ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Plating: Coventry Plating & Presswork Co., Read Street, Coventry ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).

**Lamps, bells and steel balls**
- Joseph Lucas; Powell & Hanmer (Chester St, Aston, Birmingham, with depots in London and Glasgow); Miller & Co. (Miller St, Birmingham); R. Broadhurst (Smithford St, Coventry) ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories); [Grace's Guide – Powell and Hanmer](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Powell_and_Hanmer)).
- Bells: Perry & Co. (above).
- Steel balls: Hoffmann Manufacturing Co., Chelmsford, ball maker since 1898 ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Hoffmann_Manufacturing_Co)).

**Component prices (all dated; little is 1913-specific)**
- 3-speed attachment: 21s extra pre-war on Raleigh. BSA's three-speed hub price rose "from 28 to 33 shillings" in 1920 ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- 1898: Raleigh made pedals at 10s 6d a pair "but Humber were paying only 7s. 6d. to purchase them from BSA"; Campion Cycle Co. quoted handlebars at 4s 6d; Raleigh owed Perry & Co. £1,600 for pedals; in October 1897 Raleigh "owed creditors £23 000 for materials and components" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- 1893: "The trade price of B.S.A. hubs was 15/- per pair in 1 dozen lots; Brown Brothers, of London, contracted for 2,000 pairs" ([Grace's Guide – BSA: Bicycles](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/BSA:_Bicycles)). The 1893 date was checked on the page; a search summary misdated it to 1913.
- Tyres in 1910: 2s 6d–10s 6d per tyre (Bates, above).
- Reduced 1911 prices: of one accessory firm, "the prices in several instances have been reduced for 1911" ([1910 Accessories](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Accessories)).
- Kestrel 1897 costing: production cost "£7 for machines without brake or mudguards and £7 12s. 6d. with accessories", using BSA components and "second grade tyres" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).

### Inferences
- **Buying sets for the simulation.** A mid-sized Coventry assembler would buy:
  - tubes (A&P or Butted Tube) by the foot or set;
  - lug sets and bracket and fork fittings (BSA or Brampton) by the gross or dozen sets;
  - chains (Coventry Chain locally for standard grades, Renold for the top grade);
  - free-wheels and hubs (BSA/Eadie, Perry);
  - 3-speed hubs (Sturmey-Archer or Armstrong, priced so the retail extra is about 21s);
  - saddles (Brooks top grade, Lycett or Middlemore lower grades);
  - tyres (Dunlop top, Clipper/Bates/"Warwick"/"Cambridge" lower);
  - rims (Westwood steel, Roman for lightweights);
  - brakes (Bowden), mudguards (Bluemel or bought-in rolled strip), gear cases (Richmond), and plating if not done in-house.
- **Supplier terms (default assumption, not documented for 1913).** Monthly account with 2½% for settlement by a set day of the following month; carriage paid over a minimum value; lead times of 1–4 weeks for stock items and longer for special lug patterns (Humphries & Dawes made "to customers' own patterns"). The 1897–98 Raleigh trade-creditor figures show that supplier credit was substantial and routine.
- **Local versus distant suppliers.** Coventry Chain, Middlemore & Lamplugh, Bluemel, Richmond, Micrometer and Coventry Plating were within a cart's ride. Birmingham suppliers were about 18 miles away by rail; Renold (Manchester), Hoffmann (Chelmsford), Palmer (London) and Sturmey-Archer (Nottingham) were further.

### Gaps
- I found no 1912–14 trade price lists for tubes, lugs, chains, saddles, tyres, rims, spokes, brakes, enamel, nickel salts or anodes, gas, coal or packing materials. Likely sources are the Feb 1913 BSA fittings catalogue (images on Grace's Guide), the Brooks catalogue archive at ebykr (1913 book with prices, per search result), and factors' catalogues (Brown Bros).
- I found no source on supplier delivery lead times or credit terms in the 1910s.

## 7. Size of real Coventry cycle firms (calibration for "mid-sized")

### Takeaway
Coventry had roughly 50 cycle makers in 1912–13 and about 5,800 cycle-trade workers in 1911. The big firms (Rudge-Whitworth, Humber, Swift, Premier, Singer, Triumph, Rover) produced 20,000–75,000 machines a year with 600–2,700 workers; the rest were small. A "mid-sized" Coventry maker would plausibly employ 100–400 hands and make about 3,000–15,000 machines a year (inference). Productivity benchmarks run about 50–90 machines per worker per year.

### Cited Findings
- **Firm counts.** Cycle makers numbered **75 in Coventry in 1897 and 49 in 1913**, against 309 → 160 in Birmingham and 80 → 66 in Nottingham ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html), Table 2.8, from Harrison 1969). They write of "275 firms in Birmingham, Coventry and Nottingham in 1913".
- VCH: about 50 Coventry producers in 1896, 1905 and 1912; "only about 30 per cent. of the firms listed in 1896 were still in existence in 1912", which suggests "a two-tiered structure ... a fairly rapid turnover of small firms ... and ... a number of larger more permanent units" ([VCH](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)).
- **Employment and output.** "By 1911 some 5,800 Coventry workers were employed in the cycle trade, compared with about 9,000 in Birmingham and Aston"; "In that year [1906] 310,000 cycles were made in the city" ([VCH](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)).
- **Rudge-Whitworth.**
  - 1906: "employed 1,800 workpeople and produced some 75,000 machines" ([VCH](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)).
  - Lloyd-Jones & Lewis give 75,000 machines "out of the 310 000 bicycles made in the city" and "some **2 700** workers" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). The two sources conflict on workforce.
  - Their Table C.3 lists Rudge-Whitworth "cycle sales" as 44,531 (1910), 41,949 (1911), 34,374 (1912), **30,013 (1913)** and 24,771 (1914). The flattened table may label these as £ value, but they track the frame-number increments closely, so they are probably units; uncertain.
- **Other Coventry firms (1890s data).** Premier: 600 hands and 20,000+ machines (1893–95), claiming 40,000 in 1897. Swift: "weekly production of 700 machines and ... nearly 1,000 workmen" (1896). Quinton: over 5,000 a year. Triumph: about 6,000 (c.1896). Smaller firms: "a hundred cycle workshops ... employing any number of workers from a dozen to 500" (1891); Coventry & Midland Cycle Co. about 50 hands; Clarke, Cluley & Co. over 40 (1891) ([VCH](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)).
- **Raleigh (Nottingham) benchmarks.** Output 46,075 (1911), 51,805 (1912), **57,675 (1913)**, 59,219 (1914). Workforce 550–850 (1901, at about 12,000–16,000 machines) and 2,000 (1915) ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).
- **British-owned German works.** Premier's Nuremberg works made 44,500 bicycles with 485 workers (1908). Triumph's Nuremberg company made 20,500 with 305 workers (1908) ([Grace's Guide – Premier](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Premier_Cycle_Co); [Grace's Guide – Triumph](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Triumph_Cycle_Co)).
- **Grew's scale remarks.** "1,000 machines per week can be made with the same staff, or approximately the same staff, as is required for 500 machines per week". Medium-sized firms make "most of the parts" but buy in some components ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).
- **Supplier workforces, 1914, for scale.** Coventry Chain 1,500; Perry 2,000; Accles & Pollock 700; Brooks 600–700 (Grace's Guide pages above).

### Inferences
- **Productivity benchmarks.**

  | Firm | Year | Machines/worker/year | Basis |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Premier Nuremberg | 1908 | ≈92 | 44,500 machines / 485 workers |
  | Triumph Nuremberg | 1908 | ≈67 | 20,500 / 305 |
  | Rudge-Whitworth | 1906 | 28–42 | 75,000 / 1,800–2,700 (more vertically integrated) |
  | Raleigh | 1913 | ≈30–40 | Integrated, also making Sturmey-Archer gears |

  An assembler buying most components would sit at the high end, about 60–90 per worker.
- **Fictional "mid-sized" Coventry firm (estimate).**
  - 150–250 workers.
  - Output 6,000–10,000 machines a year: in-season about 200–300 a week, slack season about half.
  - A 10–15-model catalogue across three grades, sold through a few hundred agents and two or three travellers.
  - Calcott Bros (5–11 gns range, carriers, juveniles, 3-speed roadsters) and Timerick (trade and agent's-transfer work) are good real-world templates.
- Rudge-Whitworth's 1913 volume (about 30,000–35,000 from frame numbers and Table C.3) suggests even a top Coventry maker sold well under Raleigh's 57,675 in 1913. That leaves a mid-sized firm at roughly one-third to one-fifth of Rudge's volume.

### Gaps
- I found no 1913 workforce or output figures for specific mid-tier Coventry firms (Calcott, Bayliss-Thomas, Coventry-Eagle, Centaur, Lea & Francis, Sparkbrook, Smith & Molesworth). Spennell's 1912–13 Coventry directory lists "Cycle, Motor and Allied Trades" (pp. 823–830) but is not transcribed on Grace's Guide ([Grace's Guide – Spennell's 1912-13](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Spennell%27s_Annual_Directory_of_Coventry_and_District,_1912-13)).
