# Goods transport, export shipping and communications for a Coventry cycle works, c. 1913

Scope note: these notes cover how goods and messages moved in and out of a Coventry cycle works around 1913. They give the paperwork, charges, timings and physical equipment of each channel. Internal factory paperwork, wages law and product or sales terms are left to other researchers. The strongest primary sources are listed below. All are scanned on archive.org and were read in full text.
- **Post Office Guide, 1 January 1913.** The official rate book for post, telegraphs and telephones.
- **Fred. W. West, *The Railway Goods Station: a Guide to its Control and Operation*.** Published by E. & F. N. Spon, London. The title page says 1913 and the archive.org record says 1912. Written by a railway goods-station manager.
- **Henry Spencer, *The Commercial Organisation of Engineering Factories*.** Spon, 1914. It covers a motor-car works' forwarding, receiving and shipping routine.
- **Hooper & Graham, *The Import and Export Trade; or, Modern Commercial Practice*.** Macmillan editions of 1907 and 1916. Both reuse specimen documents dated 1898.
- **H. R. Darlington, *The Railway Rates and the Carriage of Merchandise by Railway*.** Published 1893. It prints the statutory 1891–92 rate orders and classification.
- **Grace's Guide transcription of the *1914 Who's Who in Business*.** Gives real Coventry telephone numbers, telegraphic addresses and codes.
- **Coventry-specific sources.** The Victoria County History (VCH) of Warwickshire, vol. 8, "Communications" (1969), and the photo captions and plans on warwickshirerailways.com.

## 1. Railways at Coventry in 1913: companies, the goods yard, sidings, yard operation, wagons and train frequency

### Takeaway
In 1913 Coventry's railway goods traffic was in practice an LNWR operation. The Midland Railway ran goods trains in over the LNWR's Nuneaton line under running powers and shared a goods shed. Most goods went through the cramped Warwick Road goods yard beside the main line station. That yard had LNWR and joint LNWR/MR goods sheds, a 5-ton hand crane, cattle pens, a weighbridge office and horse stables built into its brick buildings, and cartage was done by horse. Works to the north were served by sidings on the Nuneaton branch at Foleshill and elsewhere. The goods-only Coventry Loop Line, with depots at Gosford Green and Bell Green, opened on 16 August 1914, so it was just after 1913.

### Cited Findings
**Companies**
- The LNWR "continued in sole control of all railway services to and from Coventry, and successfully defended its position of monopoly against the attempts of other companies… to build a competing line" (e.g. a Great Western scheme of 1865 for a line to Priory Street). — [VCH Warks vol. 8, Communications](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)
- The Coventry–Nuneaton line opened in September 1850, "over which the Midland railway had freight running rights". — [warwickshirerailways.com, Coventry station](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry.htm)
- The Midland Railway's goods shed at Coventry was a "Joint LNWR/MR goods shed" built for the MR under running powers granted on 1 September 1865. A photo caption shows "the Midland Railway's sign between the two chimneys advertising their Parcels and Goods Office". — [warwickshirerailways.com lnwrcov554c](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrcov554c.htm); [Coventry station page](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry.htm)
- In 1900 "railway carriage was conducted by both the Midland Railway Company and the L.N.W.R., and by two private firms". The firms are not named; they were presumably carrying agents. — [VCH Warks vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)
- The VCH and the LNWR Society article reproduced on warwickshirerailways.com both say Coventry industry "developed to the north of the City, away from the railway". Its vehicle and cycle manufacturing "did not at any rate result in a large volume of railway goods traffic". — [warwickshirerailways.com, Coventry station](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry.htm); [VCH Warks vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)

**The goods station (Warwick Road)**
- Goods were at first routed via Birmingham, "but by 1863 there was a goods station west of Warwick Road Bridge". — [VCH Warks vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)
- In about 1890 the yard was entered off Warwick Road. A weighbridge office stood at the entrance on the right, a gated entrance led to the cattle pens, and the Midland shed stood behind. There were "three sidings with trap points" onto the Up Main, and an elevated disc signal controlled the exit. The Railway Inn faced the entrance across the road. — [warwickshirerailways.com lnwrcov3728](https://www.warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrcov3728.htm)
- 1890s improvements added a boundary wall, a levelled and fenced roadway to the cattle pens, and another siding. The MR goods shed was extended towards the road. The LNWR built "its two storey structure which ran parallel to Warwick Road and housed stables beneath". Goods "were collected or delivered to local customers via horse drawn vehicles", and the horses were stabled on the ground floor of the two multi-storey buildings along Warwick Road. A fixed hand crane of 3 or 5 tons and two cattle pens had sufficed earlier. — [warwickshirerailways.com (search summary of lnwrcov3728/3728a pages)](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrcov3728a.htm)
- A 1920 aerial photo is captioned with the following features:
  - the "Joint LNWR/MR goods shed" and a "No 2 Goods shed"
  - main offices at the Warwick Road end, "housing claims and telephone exchange"
  - a weighbridge with office
  - a "5 ton crane" for open wagons, particularly timber
  - stables in the brick footbridge rampart and at the lower left of the yard
  - the foreman's house beside a short loading ramp for road vehicles

  Coal avoided this yard and went instead to "Coundon Wharf, Coventry's main depot for coal, just one stop down the Nuneaton branch". The same 1920 set shows "the 25 ton electrically operated gantry, 5 ton hand-operated crane and stores area". These features are dated 1920 and may post-date 1913. — [warwickshirerailways.com lnwrcov554c](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrcov554c.htm); [Coventry station page](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry.htm)
- A 1910 LNWR plan at 2 chains to the inch shows the goods sheds, cranes, "landing" and sidings. It also shows sidings to Coventry Cattle Market and the Coal Wharf, wagon turntables, stables and office blocks from the 1890s, the weighbridge, cattle docks and signal cabins 1–4. The goods yard had a major expansion c. 1892–1903. — [warwickshirerailways.com, Coventry plans & maps](https://www.warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry_plans_&_maps.htm)
- Railway cottages by Warwick Road bridge housed "the station and goods yard staff". — [warwickshirerailways.com lnwrcov554](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrcov554.htm)
- Staff photos include "a photograph dated 31st August 1912 of Coventry's L&NWR and MR Goods & Cartage Foreman Staff". There are also captions for "Mr John James King, Chief Cartage Foreman"; for King and colleagues "together showing three different hatbands"; and for King "standing in front of one of the many horses stabled at Coventry station". — [warwickshirerailways.com, Coventry station (Members of Staff)](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry.htm)
- Cycles at the yard appear in Railway Gazette photos. Their date is not stated and they are probably 1920s–30s. One shows "Horse-drawn vehicles bringing a delivery of bicycles to Warwick Road goods yard for dispatching to all over the UK". Another shows "A rake of open wagons being loaded with crates of Triumph cycles inside Coventry No 2 goods shed". A third shows "A Clerestory LNWR Parcels Van being loaded with a range of bicycles for dispatching via an express service". — [warwickshirerailways.com, Coventry station](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry.htm)

**Private sidings and other goods points**
- The Nuneaton-line stations within Coventry were Coundon Road, Foleshill, Longford & Exhall and Hawkesbury Lane. Hawkesbury Lane was open for goods from 1889. A branch to Wyken Colliery dates from 1848. — [VCH Warks vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)
- At Foleshill there was a seven-siding goods yard on the up (Coventry) side. Industrial sidings toward the city included Brett's, Webster's and Courtaulds sidings, with Daimler's sidings opposite on the down side. These are named in captions c. 1958, and the dates of the siding agreements are not given. — [warwickshirerailways.com Foleshill](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrf136.htm)
- The Coventry Loop Line was built to "divert goods traffic from Nuneaton away from the congested lines running through Coventry station". It opened on 16 August 1914 and was 3.44 miles long, from Three Spires Junction to Humber Road Junction (c. 1916). Gosford Green had "six sidings arranged in three pairs" and handled "machined products outward and materials inward". Bell Green had four sidings plus a goods shed (1926 OS). Both were goods-only and were never used for passengers. — [warwickshirerailways.com, Coventry Loop Line](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventryloopline.htm); [VCH Warks vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)

**How a goods station worked (general, West 1912/13)**
- The equipment and staff grades named include turntables, capstans, lifts, cranes, weighing machines and hydraulic or electric capstans. Where no mechanical power was provided, "horses may be employed to do the intermediate shunting… in the majority of such cases it would be more economical… to substitute capstan power". The non-productive staff include "a shunter, capstan-worker, shunt horse driver, number-taker, craner, hoistman, warehouseman, timekeeper". Inspectors keep "points, turntables, capstans, weighing machines, etc, clear during falling snow". — [West, *The Railway Goods Station*](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)
- Gangs: the best unloading gang is "a checker, a caller-off, a turner-out, and four truckers", with not more than about half of the men appointed staff and the rest "extras" or "casuals". A loading gang is "checker, caller-off, and loader". In an example, a gang of five handles 240 tons in a 60-hour week. — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)
- Loading rules:
  - rope every load "if in doubt"
  - sheet the load with tarpaulins "rick-wise", with the overlap "towards the rear of the train"
  - "Leave the load over rather than use a bad sheet"
  - fasten the doors on both sides of covered and drop-side wagons
  - remove old labels and chalk marks
  - put a label on each side of the wagon with "the destination and route very distinctly", in ink, visible when sheeted
  - label "fragile goods" as a warning to shunters

  — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)
- Daily rhythm at a large station: "nearly all his outwards goods tumbling in upon him between 5 and 7 o'clock in the evening, for trains that must depart within the next two or three hours in order to reach their destinations early the next morning". Traders expect inward goods "first thing in the morning". — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)
- At one large station, arriving wagons carried their invoices on the wagon sides. "We have two bicycle messengers who are entirely occupied, day and night alternately, in riding to the passenger station to fetch any invoices which arrive there." — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)

**Wagons**
- On LNWR wagon insignia: "Until 1908, company ownership was shown only by two white diamonds per side". "After 1908 company letters LNWR introduced (size to suit vehicle)". "The painting of diamonds ceased about 1915". Tonnages were shown, e.g. "6 TONS" on covered and open goods wagons and "8 TONS COAL WAGON" along the top plank. — [HMRS, LNWR/WCJS insignia](https://hmrs.org.uk/lnwr-wcjs-loco-coach-and-wagon-insignia.html)
- LNWR goods wagon and van bodies were "medium lead grey" from 1850, "mixed from equal parts of black and white pigments… slightly darker than LMS grey". This is quoted from *LNWR Wagons* Vol. 1 in a modellers' forum. — [RMweb forum, LNWR van livery](https://www.rmweb.co.uk/forums/topic/114259-lnwr-van-livery/page/2/)
- The LNWR had about 18,000 wagons in 1862 and nearly 80,000 by 1923, mostly built at Earlestown. Its categories included open goods, coal, cattle and glass wagons. — [LNWR History Group, wagons intro](https://www.lnwrhg.com/ref/wagons/wagon_intro.php)
- Private-owner wagons were built to the Railway Clearing House 1907 standard: 10-ton open wagons of five or seven planks, with a 9 ft wheelbase, side and end doors and grease axleboxes. This comes from a model retailer's descriptions and is only a secondary source. — [The Model Centre, RCH 1907 wagons](https://www.themodelcentre.com/article/new-rapido-rch-1907-wagons-announced---pre-order-yours-now)

**Train frequency and transit**
- The LNWR offered next-day delivery "back in the 19th century". Express parcels (stamped) were advertised at 2 hours from Euston to Birmingham. From 1883 the company collected free from commercial premises, and it ran "a big fleet of road vehicles, from bicycles up to large horse-drawn vans". — [LNWR Society, Parcels Service](https://lnwrs.org.uk/parcels-traffic)

### Inferences
- A fictional Coventry cycle works of 1913 would use LNWR goods services and send by Midland over running powers only for Midland-line destinations. Its rail point is the Warwick Road goods yard (sundries and crates) unless it sits on the Nuneaton branch with a private siding (Foleshill area). Coal comes in through Coundon Road wharf, by canal, or by private-owner wagons into a siding.
- For a limited 3D yard view:
  - one or two brick goods sheds with a through siding and an internal platform, and a 30-cwt to 5-ton hand crane
  - an open-yard 5-ton hand crane
  - wagon turntables and possibly a capstan
  - a weighbridge and small weighbridge office at the gate
  - a two-storey brick range along the street with stables below
  - cattle pens
  - a boundary wall and gates
  - grey LNWR wagons with white diamonds and/or "L N W R" lettering
  - tarpaulin-sheeted open wagons
  - horse-drawn railway lorries and vans
- West's 5–7 pm rush to the goods yard, with departures 2–3 hours later and arrival "early the next morning", supports overnight transit from Coventry to London (about 94 miles) and to Liverpool. This is an inference. No Coventry working timetable was found.
- The 25-ton electric gantry seen in the 1920 aerial view should not be modelled for 1913 unless it is confirmed as earlier.

### Gaps
- No 1913 LNWR working timetable was found, so the number of goods trains per day from Coventry to Camden/Broad Street, Birmingham and Liverpool is unknown.
- I found no list of which Coventry cycle works had private-siding agreements in 1913. The named sidings are from c. 1958 captions.
- I found no staff or horse counts for Coventry goods station in 1913. The only staff evidence is a group photo of 31 August 1912.
- The undated Railway Gazette photos of cycles at Coventry are probably from the LMS era.

## 2. Railway paperwork and charges: consignment notes, risk rates, classification, cartage, invoices, accounts, claims, demurrage, minimum charges, transit times

### Takeaway
The trader filled in and signed a consignment note at despatch, choosing between the company's risk rate and a cheaper owner's risk rate. The railway's invoicing clerks then priced the goods from the Classification and the station rate books and sent a waybill (an "invoice") ahead with the train. At the far end the goods were entered on delivery sheets, and the carman got a signature and collected any "to pay" charges. Regular traders were debited on ledger or credit accounts and chased by collectors. In the statutory 1891–92 classification bicycles are Class 5, the highest merchandise class. The 1913 Railway Clearing House (RCH) General Railway Classification of Goods exists, but I could not read its cycle entry.

### Cited Findings
**Liability, owner's risk and company's risk**
- Under Railway and Canal Traffic Act 1854 s.7, railways were liable for loss or injury caused by neglect, notwithstanding any notice limiting liability. Conditions were allowed only if "just and reasonable", and special contracts had to be signed. *Peek v North Staffordshire Railway* (1863) established the "fair alternative" doctrine. A carrier may offer to carry with no liability if it offers "as an inducement a reduction of the price below that which would be reasonable remuneration for carrying at owner's risk". — [Wikipedia, Railway and Canal Traffic Act 1854](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_and_Canal_Traffic_Act_1854); [Irish Statute Book, 1854 Act s.7](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/1854/en/act/pub/0031/sec0007.html)
- An LSWR consignment note of 1912 for milk states that goods are carried "at owners risk, and at reduced rate under terms of agreement". — [Science Museum Group, consignment note 1998-9976](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co445384/consignment-note)
- A newspaper item, undated in my search result, says cyclists' bodies long disputed with the Railway Companies Association over cycles carried as passengers' luggage. The companies eventually "bound themselves to accept all risks, on the payment of an insurance rate of one penny per machine". Parcels staff charged "either at owner's risk or Company's risk". The date and context were not verified. — [Welsh Newspapers Online (search result)](https://newspapers.library.wales/view/4247765/4247770)

**Classification and rate structure**
- In the statutory classification confirmed by the Railway Rates and Charges Order Confirmation Acts 1891–92, "Bicycles" are cl. 5 and "Tricycles and velocipedes" are cl. 5. "Bicycle stands, wrought iron" are cl. 3. Class 5 is the top merchandise class. — [Darlington, *Railway Rates and the Carriage of Merchandise* (1893)](https://archive.org/details/railwayratescarr00darlrich)
- The maximum charges in those orders were made up of the following elements. — [Darlington](https://archive.org/details/railwayratescarr00darlrich); [Acworth, *The Railways and the Traders*](https://archive.org/details/railwaystraderss00acwouoft)
  - a conveyance rate per ton per mile, tapering by bands: the first 20 miles, the next 30, the next 50, and the remainder
  - a station terminal at each end. The Board of Trade's draft order proposed 1s 6d for "ordinary merchandise traffic"
  - service terminals for loading, unloading, covering and uncovering, rising by class. The draft order put the top figure at 4d; the lower bound is illegible in the OCR
  - separate extra charges for collection and delivery
- Small parcels by merchandise train were those not exceeding 3 cwt. Each was charged an extra per-parcel sum on top of the tonnage charge. A parcel under 28 lb could be charged as 28 lb, and "a fraction of fourteen pounds" as 14 lb. If the consignor refused to declare the contents, the parcel could be charged "as if… wholly composed of articles comprised in Class 5". — [Darlington](https://archive.org/details/railwayratescarr00darlrich)
- A volume titled "R[ailway] C[learing] H[ouse] Classification 1913" survives in the Canal & River Trust collection, containing the "General railway classification of goods by merchandise trains". — [Canal & River Trust BW91/7/4/2](https://collections.canalrivertrust.org.uk/bw91.7.4.2)
- The Railway Clearing House was set up in 1842 to apportion receipts for through traffic among companies and to set common standards. Its archive includes the classification of goods, rate tables and station handbooks (TNA RAIL 1091). — [National Museums NI, Railway Clearing House](https://collections.nationalmuseumsni.org/tra.rach); [TNA RAIL 1091](https://beta.nationalarchives.gov.uk/catalogue/id/C13247/)

**Document flow inside the railway (West 1912/13)**
- "The consignment notes for the outwards goods, where they are checked at the wagon side, are not available for the invoicers until the goods have been loaded." Then "invoices have to be written out and the charges calculated at racehorse speed, so that the documents may go by the last train out to meet the goods". Charges come "from the complicated Classification and Rate Books". — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)
- On arrival: invoices are removed "from the wagon sides" and entered on delivery sheets. West advocates a "single-entry delivery sheet" per consignment and typewritten invoicing. Delivery sheets and "bills" are issued to carmen, and each issue is logged on slips "showing… the amounts of charges to be collected". — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)
- "Paid" and "To pay": outward invoices are marked "Paid" (carriage paid) or "To pay" (carriage forward), and errors are corrected by "urgent train telegrams". Consignees refusing "to pay" charges cause a "recharge" request to the sending station "unless this already appears on the consignment note". — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)
- Accounts and collection:
  - outwards "paid" goods collected by company carmen and payable by consignors are debited to "collectors"
  - "a well-known trader's own carman comes to the station for the goods without the money", or the trader is too busy to pay the company carman, so goods are delivered and the account debited
  - other items include "Miscellaneous Debits, such as Rents, Wharfages, Demurrage, and Special Charges" and "Ledger and Credit Account Deductions… only brought to light after the rendering of the accounts"

  — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)
- Claims: damage is noted "in a book, as the packages come out of the wagon, and… afterwards endorsed on the invoices for reporting and claims purposes". Firms that do not record returned empties "require the railways to prove delivery… and if this cannot be done, claims frequently follow". — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)

**The manufacturer's side of the paperwork (Spencer 1914, a motor works)**
- Every outgoing consignment had "a delivery note, of which a duplicate is kept". It "gives the date of despatch and name of railway company or firm of carriers… description of the consignment and respective marks, the address… and stating who pays the carriage. An acknowledgment of receipt must… be embodied on the face of this note". — [Spencer, *Commercial Organisation of Engineering Factories*](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- The customer's "Delivery Advice Note" was carbon-copied in duplicate books. Its fields were:
  - telegraphic address, telephone number and reference
  - "We have to-day despatched the undermentioned goods per … Carriage … to …"
  - "Kindly advise the carriers as to any damage sustained in transit, and, pending an examination, accept delivery from carriers with due reserve"
  - columns for "Packages and marks" and "Description of goods"
  - "Be good enough to return empties, carriage paid"

  — [Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Inbound goods: a "Goods Received Book" records consignor, address, "per" (the carrier), weight, carriage, description, our order, carrier's invoice, supplier's invoice and stores-ledger entry. It notes "charges for carriage and freight as appearing on carrier's way-bill". A packages register tracks suppliers' returnable cases. — [Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)

### Inferences
- Simulation flow for an outbound rail consignment:
  1. The works' despatch clerk writes the delivery note and the railway consignment note, tickets "carriage paid" or "carriage forward", and chooses owner's risk or company's risk.
  2. The goods go by works van, or by an LNWR lorry on collection, to Warwick Road between 5 and 7 pm.
  3. The checker and caller-off check the goods at the wagon side.
  4. A railway invoice is written that night and sent by the last train.
  5. The goods are delivered next morning at the destination against a delivery sheet signature.
  6. The charge goes to the works' monthly railway account if carriage paid, or is collected from the consignee if "to pay".
- Since 1891 bicycles had been rated Class 5, which made cycle traffic expensive by weight. Cycle makers sent machines crated or in wicker crates and often by passenger train or parcels services, as the Railway Gazette photo of a parcels van loaded with bicycles shows. Owner's risk rates were the cheaper option that shifted liability to the maker.

### Gaps
- I could not read the 1913 RCH Classification's entry for cycles: whether packed or unpacked, any rate-and-a-half for loose machines, or any owner's-risk condition.
- I found no numbers for the actual 1913 Coventry–London or Coventry–Liverpool rates per ton or per cycle, or for owner's risk discounts. One search hit gave a 10% owner's/company's risk difference, but it comes from a colonial or Indian goods-tariff case and is not British evidence.
- Demurrage rates (per wagon per day, free days) were not found for 1913 Britain.
- The standard claim time limits on consignment notes (e.g. days to claim for damage or loss) were not found in a primary source.
- I could not confirm the size of the 1913 general rate increase under the Railway and Canal Traffic Act 1913.

## 3. Road transport: horse carts, carriers, motor lorries and vans, capacities and speeds; did works keep their own horses?

### Takeaway
In 1913 Coventry was still served by hundreds of weekly carrier's-cart services. The railways collected and delivered by horse lorry and van from yard stables. Motor lorries were legally capped at 5–12 mph depending on tyres and axle weight, and light vans (under 2 tons unladen) at the general 20 mph car limit. A single railway horse was expected to pull about 36 cwt at 6 mph for 20 miles a day. Traders' "own carmen" collecting from the railway are documented in general. I found no direct evidence for a particular Coventry cycle works' stables.

### Cited Findings
- Over 100 carrier's carts ran weekly in 1830 and over 360 by 1850. "In 1900 there were 330 weekly cart services", and carriers "still ran six regular weekly services in 1936". The 1830 cart to London took 30 hours. Carriers set out from inns such as the Red Lion in Hertford Street. — [VCH Warks vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)
- Horse performance on the GWR: "A single horse was expected to haul up to 36 cwt at a speed of 6 m.p.h. and cover 20 miles in their day's work". "A standard two horse van designed in 1912 could carry 4 tons 10 cwt". Single-horse vans had wooden sides about 2 ft high with iron hoops and a canvas sheet roof. This is GWR evidence, captioned for a photo of about 1927. — [warwickshirerailways.com GWR horse vans](https://warwickshirerailways.com/gwr/gwrhd705.htm)
- LMS figures for 1923: 9,370 road horses and 19,432 road wagons and carts. Types were flat drays or trolleys, covered vans and floats, with trolley capacities of 35–65 cwt. "70 hour week with a daily maximum of 14 hours was routine". These are post-1923 figures. — [LMS Society, horse transport](https://www.lmssociety.co.uk/topics/horseTransport.shtml)
- Railway carmen (West):
  - carmen and van-guards are best put on a bonus by weight carted
  - fixed "rounds" are common
  - load times "when the horse is in the shafts" are recorded
  - "horses with their harness and vehicles should be turned out of the station clean and smart"
  - the stables are inspected daily
  - vans are cleaned by "Youths of sixteen or seventeen" or old men
  - at small stations "two or three carmen are employed"
  - "a well-known trader's own carman comes to the station for the goods"

  — [West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)
- Motor law:
  - The Motor Car Act 1903 raised the speed limit to 20 mph and required registration, number plates and a 5s driving licence (minimum age 17), from 1 January 1904. — [Wikipedia, Motor Car Act 1903](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_Car_Act_1903)
  - Under the Heavy Motor Car Order of 1904, a heavy motor car was over 2 and under 5 tons unladen, with at most one trailer. On rubber tyres the limit was 12 mph if the loaded rear axle was 6 tons or less and 8 mph if 8 tons or less. On steel tyres it was 8 mph and 5 mph respectively. — [Phillimore, *Motor Road Transport for Commercial Purposes* (1920)](https://tekniskkulturarv.dk/book/11ab5513-b799-4fa9-a59d-b031fa2b12cf?page=147)
- The War Office subsidy scheme for 3-ton lorries began c. 1912; the Leyland "Subsidy A" type was built 1912–26. *Commercial Motor* reported the "subsidy trials results" on 27 February 1913. — [Wikipedia, Leyland RAF Type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leyland_RAF_Type); [Commercial Motor archive, 27 Feb 1913](https://archive.commercialmotor.com/article/27th-february-1913/9/the-subsidy-trials-results)
- Wicker cycle crates were patented in 1899 by John J. Plater & Sons of Birmingham, in eight designs. One example measures 96 × 26 × 50 in and has castors "for pushing along railway platforms". Some designs required the pedals or handlebar to be removed. — [Online Bicycle Museum, Plater wicker cycle crate](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/1899-john-j-plater-cycle-crate/)

### Inferences
- A 1913 Coventry cycle works of a few hundred workers plausibly had a small yard with two to four horses, one or two covered spring vans and a flat lorry for runs to Warwick Road and local suppliers. A large firm (Rudge-Whitworth, Triumph, Humber) may also have had a light motor van. This is inferred from West's references to traders' own carmen and from the general fleet sizes. No Coventry works stable record was found.
- For gameplay: horse van about 1½–4½ tons at walking or trotting pace (about 3–6 mph), about 20 miles a day; motor lorry 3 tons at 5–12 mph; light van under 2 tons unladen up to 20 mph.

### Gaps
- I found no documentary evidence of specific Coventry cycle works' own horses, stables or motor vans in 1913, and no road vehicle livery of the LNWR.
- The capacities and speeds of carrier's carts are not established.
- The identities of the "two private firms" doing railway carriage in Coventry in 1900 are unknown; Pickfords or Carter Paterson are possible but unconfirmed.

## 4. Canal: the Coventry Canal in 1913 and canal carriers

### Takeaway
By 1913 the Coventry Canal was a profitable but secondary route, mainly carrying coal to wharves around the city from the North Warwickshire pits. It used horse- or donkey-drawn narrow boats up to 71 ft 9 in × 7 ft. Fellows Morton & Clayton (FMC), the largest canal carrier, had a Coventry depot and ran non-stop "fly" boats on trunk routes. For a cycle works, the canal is a plausible coal and heavy-goods channel, not a despatch route for finished cycles.

### Cited Findings
- "The Coventry Canal was navigable only by small boats, and these were in the main horse- or donkey-drawn, even into the 20th century. Of the goods carried coal always remained the most important". It also served the Hartshill and Mancetter quarries, and in 1905 "was said to carry significant quantities of iron and nickel ores". The maximum boat size was 71 ft 9 in × 7 ft. — [VCH Warks vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)
- Wharves in Coventry in 1904, with canal distances from the Bishop Street terminal basin, were:
  - Hill's Wharf, Bishopgate Green (1¼ mile)
  - Stoke Basin (2 miles)
  - Navigation Wharf, Little Heath (2¾ miles)
  - New Inn Wharf, Little Heath (4 miles)
  - Longford Wharf and Hawkesbury Junction (5½ miles), with a lock into the Oxford Canal

  The Wyken New Colliery loop branch was still in use. In 1905 the Coventry Canal Company paid a 7% dividend. — [VCH Warks vol. 8 (citing Bradshaw's Canals, 1904; Royal Commission 1907)](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)
- The canal basin warehouses "were built in four phases between 1787 and 1914". — [Coventry Society, canal warehouses re-visited](https://www.coventrysociety.org.uk/2023/08/21/canal-warehouses-re-visited)
- FMC was "the largest and best-known canal transportation company in England" for much of the early 20th century. Its points included:
  - 34 depots, including Coventry
  - fly-boats working non-stop, e.g. London (City Road) to Birmingham (Fazeley Street) in "around 54 hours"
  - steamers built at Saltley 1898–1911
  - its first motor boat, *Linda*, with a Bolinder engine, c. 1911–12
  - its first livery of black and white with a red dividing line

  — [Wikipedia, Fellows Morton & Clayton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellows_Morton_%26_Clayton)
- The Griff collieries were served by the Griff arm (1787). In the 1930s they supplied the Birmingham and Coventry power stations "mostly in canal barges". This is later than 1913. — [warwickshirerailways.com, Griff collieries (search summary)](https://warwickshirerailways.com/misc/griff.htm)

### Inferences
- A works on or near the canal at Foleshill, Longford or Stoke could take boiler coal by the boatload from Bedworth or Griff, roughly a narrow-boat load of 20–30 tons. That tonnage is an inference from general narrow-boat practice and is not sourced here. Steel bar or castings could also come by FMC fly-boat from Birmingham. Finished cycles would not normally go by canal.

### Gaps
- No 1913 tonnage figures were found for the Coventry Canal; the Royal Commission returns of 1906–1911 were not read.
- No FMC Coventry timetable or rates were found, and the boat cargo capacity is not sourced.

## 5. Export: forwarding agents, bills of lading, shipping notes, consular invoices, certificates of origin, insurance, packing and marks, f.o.b./c.i.f., documentary bills

### Takeaway
An inland manufacturer such as a Coventry cycle maker sent export cases by rail to a shipping or forwarding agent at Liverpool, or London. It posted an Advice Note giving marks, numbers, measurements, weights, value and steamer. The agent shipped the cases, cleared Customs (the export "Specification"), took out bills of lading and sent back a Freight Note. Payment was commonly by a documentary draft at 60 or 90 days drawn through a bank, with the invoice, a full set of bills of lading and the insurance policy attached. The documents needed depended on the market: consular invoices for the United States, Chile, Brazil and Portugal, and certificates of origin for preferential colonial or treaty tariffs.

### Cited Findings
**Forwarding from an inland town**
- "The goods are usually sent to a shipping agent at the port where the vessel is loading, and this agent, for a small commission of so much per package, sees that the goods are put on board, attends to the Customs formalities, and takes out the Bill of Lading. In this case, all that the exporter has to do (after delivering the goods to the railway company, or carrier) is to fill up an Advice Note". The note gives "the name of the steamer… the dock or port… the measurement or weight of the packages, and the value". — [Hooper & Graham (1916)](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- The agent's "Freight Note" (specimen dated 1898) itemises freight per ton, carriage, cartage, dues, the bill of lading stamp and commission, and encloses "One set Bills of Lading". An alternative instruction asks the agent to "cover Marine Insurance… and charge to" the buyer, with "Shipping Expenses to be charged" forward. — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- Dock-delivered goods go with a Shipping Note or Boat Note. The ship's officer signs a Mate's Receipt, which is "clean" or, if "1 bale chafed", "foul". — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- At a 1914 motor works, the shipping clerk "will obtain tenders from the shipping agents, and ascertain the respective dates of closing and sailing". He states each case's overall dimensions and weight. Light bulky packages pay "by cubical measurement… calculated at 40 cubic feet" per ton, and others per ton of 20 cwt. — [Spencer (1914)](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)

**Packing and marking**
- Export cases are "of wood, lined with zinc or oilcloth, or waterproof paper… secured with iron bands (not strips of wood) nailed on". "In the absence of special instructions it is desirable to limit the weight to, say, 10 cwt". Mule-country loads are 220 lb, or 110 lb each side. — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- Each package bears "a distinctive mark with a running number", with the port of destination below in bold letters. Measurements, and sometimes gross, tare and net weights in kilogrammes, are also marked. Marks measure "15 to 18 inches each way", with letters "3 to 4 inches in height within triangles, double triangles, diamonds, hearts, or circles". "Stencils (sheets of tin…) are generally used for marking". Packages bear no consignee address so that they can be resold unopened. — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- A specimen bill of lading requires the "Port of Destination in letters not less than two inches long". — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- At the 1914 motor works, export goods were "packing in tin-lined cases, and all bright parts protected from liability to rust by… Russian tallow and white lead, supplemented by coarse canvas wrappings". An example mark is "Ceylon, per ss. 'Indiana'". — [Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Elswick-Hopper's cycle export packing in 1949 used four types. Complete machines with the front wheel out, or with both wheels out, went six to a case. Unassembled sets went 25 to a case. Export went "by road to ports, mainly to London and Liverpool"; "rail delivery is used for the home market". This is 1949 evidence, used only as an analogy. — [icenicam archive, Elswick-Hopper 1949](https://icenicam.org.uk/articlez/archive/arc0545.html)

**Bills of lading, Customs, freight**
- "Bills of Lading are usually made out in sets of two or three, each one of which… requires a sixpenny stamp… impressed before the document is signed", with a £50 penalty. One copy goes by post, one by the carrying steamer, and one is kept, plus an unstamped "Captain's Copy". A shipper gives a "Letter of Indemnity" to obtain a clean bill. — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- Freight was paid per ton measurement, where "a ton by steamer is generally 40 cubic feet", or per ton weight, plus "10% for primage". — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- "Within six days of the final 'clearance' of the vessel, the Customs 'Specification' must be made out". There are two forms, white for UK goods and pink for re-exports, using headings from the "Official Export List" (Eyre & Spottiswoode, price 2d). — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)

**Consular invoices and certificates of origin**
- United States: a "Consular Invoice" is declared before the US Consul for the district, usually in 3 copies, or 4 if the goods go in bond. It is not needed if the invoice is £20 or less. Packages are marked "Made in Great Britain". Consular invoices are "also required" for Chile, Brazil, other South American countries and Portugal, on forms supplied by their consuls. — [Hooper & Graham (1916)](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- Certificates of origin were "issued chiefly by the Chambers of Commerce", for double-tariff countries and for British colonial preference.
  - The 1907 edition says that, as of September 1901, they were required for Japan and, in certain cases, Turkey, with Russia mainly for iron and steel articles. Spain needed the Spanish consul's visa. Canada needed an exporter's declaration on the back of the invoice.
  - The 1916 edition lists, as of January 1916 (wartime), Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the British West Indies, Serbia, Nicaragua, Japan and Argentina, and in some cases Spain, France and Russia.

  — [Hooper & Graham 1907](https://archive.org/details/importandexport00coungoog); [Hooper & Graham 1916](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)

**Terms and payment**
- Price terms:
  - "F.o.b.: cost, plus packing, forwarding, and shipping charges"
  - "C. & f.: F.o.b., plus freight"
  - "C.i.f.: C. & f., plus insurance"
  - "Franco: C.i.f., plus foreign import duty and forwarding charges"
  - Manchester goods were "frequently bought 'f.o.b. Liverpool'"

  — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- Documentary bills: a draft "usually at 60 or 90" days. The "First" has the invoice, bill of lading, policy and letter of hypothecation attached; the "Second" and "Third" have the invoice and bill of lading. The bank advances "the full amount; in others only 90, 80, or 75 per cent". The bill may be "documents against payment" or "documents against acceptance". — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- Marine insurance: an "Insurance Slip" goes to the insurer or broker. The amount declared is the invoice plus freight plus "10 (or 15) per cent", rounded up to the next £5 or £10. Floating policies are used. Under Sale of Goods Act 1893 s.32(3), the goods are at the seller's risk in sea transit unless the buyer is given notice to insure. — [Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- At the motor works, marine insurance on cars was taken "W.P.A." ("with particular average"). — [Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)

**Cables and codes for export business**
- Sample cable rates per word in the 1916 edition (possibly wartime):
  - France, Holland, Belgium and Germany: 2d
  - New York: 1s
  - India: 1s 8d
  - Cape: 1s 3d
  - Australia: 3s
  - Japan: 3s 11d

  The minimum is 10d per foreign telegram. Printed codes include the "A1 and ABC Codes", and every large merchant also had a private code. Code words are limited to 10 letters for extra-European messages, and in practice only words of 7–10 letters are usable. — [Hooper & Graham (1916)](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)
- 1913 rules: code words must not exceed ten letters. Cypher and figure groups count at five characters to a word. Books of 25 foreign telegram forms bearing a 10d embossed stamp cost £1 1s. — [Post Office Guide, Jan 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)

### Inferences
- A Coventry cycle exporter would typically ship c.i.f. or f.o.b. Liverpool for the Empire and South America, through a Liverpool forwarding agent paid by commission per package. It would send crates by LNWR goods train to the Liverpool docks (overnight transit is an inference). Six machines per case, with wheels out, in zinc- or paper-lined iron-banded cases marked with a stencilled diamond or triangle mark, a running number and the port, is a reasonable 1913 model. It is extrapolated from Hooper & Graham, Spencer, and Elswick-Hopper's 1949 practice.
- Simulation documents per export order: advice note to agent, railway consignment note, invoice in triplicate, certificate of origin or consular invoice (by market), insurance slip and policy, set of three bills of lading (6d stamp each), draft at 60 or 90 days with documents to the bank, and the agent's freight note and debit note.

### Gaps
- No Coventry-specific forwarding agents or Liverpool shipping lines used by Coventry cycle firms in 1913 were identified.
- The certificate of origin list for exactly 1913 falls between the 1901 and 1916 lists cited and is not documented.
- Pre-war cable rates for 1913 come from the Guide's tables on pages 859–886, which were not read; the 1916 figures above may differ.

## 6. Post Office: letters, deliveries, parcels, registration, postal orders, express and railway letters, telegrams, telephone

### Takeaway
In 1913 the Post Office handled all inland mail at 1d for up to 4 oz, with several deliveries a day in towns. Parcels went at 3d–11d up to 11 lb, and registration cost 2d. Telegrams cost 6d for the first 12 words, address included, and were delivered free within the town delivery of a head office by boy messengers on bicycles. A registered telegraphic address cost £1 1s a year. Since 1 January 1912 the Post Office had also run the telephone system. Trunk calls were charged by distance: 3d for 25 miles or under, up to 1s for 100 miles, per 3 minutes. Coventry firms show telephone numbers such as "No. 860 Coventry (6 lines)", telegraphic addresses such as "Lathe, Coventry", and use of the ABC and Lieber's codes.

### Cited Findings
**Letters, cards, packets**
- "Not exceeding 4 oz. in weight… 1d. For every additional 2 oz. … ½d." Unpaid letters are charged double postage on delivery. The maximum size is 2 ft × 1 ft × 1 ft. Post-cards cost ½d and reply cards 1d. The "Halfpenny Packet Post" is for packets up to 2 oz. — [Post Office Guide, 1 Jan 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- Deliveries: an Edwardian memoir site states "there were four post deliveries a day, with the last one at half past nine at night", and that a reply could arrive the same day. The town is not specified, so this should not be taken as Coventry's schedule. — [1900s.org.uk, postal deliveries](https://1900s.org.uk/1900s-postal-deliveries.htm)

**Parcels**
- Rates:

  | Weight | Rate |
  |---|---|
  | Not over 1 lb | 3d |
  | 2 lb | 4d |
  | 3 lb | 5d |
  | 5 lb | 6d |
  | 7 lb | 7d |
  | 8 lb | 8d |
  | 9 lb | 9d |
  | 10 lb | 10d |
  | 11 lb | 11d |

  "No parcel may exceed 11 lb. in weight". The greatest length is 3 ft 6 in, and length plus girth may not exceed 6 ft (the OCR reads "8ft. 6in.", a misreading). Parcels must be prepaid and handed in at the counter, not put in a letter box. A certificate of posting is available. "Parcels are neither accepted for transmission nor delivered on Sundays". — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- Webster's 1913 dictionary describes the British parcel rates "from 1897" as 3d plus 1d per lb "up to the limit of 10 pounds". This conflicts with the Guide's 11 lb limit and with the January 1913 scale above; the official Guide should be preferred. — [Webster's 1913, "parcel post"](https://websters1913.timcieplowski.com/word/parcel-post/)

**Registration, express, railway letters, postal orders**
- Registration: "The ordinary registration fee is 2d." This gives compensation up to £5. Higher fees run 3d for £20, 4d for £40 and so on, up to £400 maximum. Registered items must be handed to an officer and a receipt obtained, and must be sealed "with wax, gum, or other adhesive matter". — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- Express delivery by special messenger: "For every mile or part of a mile from the Office of delivery… 3d.", plus a 3d weight fee over 1 lb. "Cycles are used, wherever practicable". A messenger takes at most ten packets. It runs on weekdays during telegraph hours, generally 8 am to 8 pm. — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- "Railway Letters": "Upon every Railway Letter a fee of 2d. in addition to postage" is charged. The letter must not exceed 4 oz and is handed in at the passenger or parcels booking office. It is forwarded "by the next available train" to the station of address, to be called for or delivered. The example given is "a letter handed in at Rugby Station for Barrow-in-Furness". — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- Postal orders were issued in values from 6d to 21s. Poundage is ½d up to 2s 6d, 1d from 3s to 15s and 1½d from 15s 6d to 21s; this reading of the Guide's table is from a poor OCR. Up to 5d in stamps, no more than three stamps, may be added to an order's value. — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)

**Telegrams**
- "The charge is 6d. for the first 12 words, including the address, and ½d. for every additional word." The charge is independent of distance. Figures count at five to a word. Offices are "usually open on week-days from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m.", and in many towns telegrams can be sent at any hour from the railway station. — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- "Telegrams are delivered free of charge within three miles of the Office nearest the address… and when that office is a Head Post Office no charge is made for delivery within the town postal delivery". Porterage beyond that is charged per mile; the OCR reads "8d.", and 3d is the likely figure but should be verified. A reply of up to 48 words may be prepaid. Repetition, for code or figure telegrams, costs half the tariff. — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- "Any person may register an abbreviated or arbitrary address on payment of a registration fee of £1 1s. a year". It consists of two words, a dictionary word of 10 letters or fewer plus the town, e.g. "Hercules, Liverpool". Telegrams can be delivered by telephone to subscribers, in large towns only with a registered address. Written copies follow "by post in daily batches without extra charge". — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- Telegraph messenger boys were aged 13–17, wore Post Office uniform and were drilled. They worked about 50 hours a week, usually 8 am–8 pm, and "In 1897 the Post Office began to provide bicycles for Boy Messengers". — [The Postal Museum, Working for the Post Office (search summary)](https://www.postalmuseum.org/?p=75232)

**Telephones**
- The Post Office took over the National Telephone Company's system on 1 January 1912. It "now controls the entire Trunk and Local Telephone Service throughout the United Kingdom, except in Portsmouth… and in Hull". — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- The takeover brought 1,565 exchanges, 231 of them with more than 300 subscribers, and 561,738 subscribers. A further 450 new exchanges opened in the next three years. — [britishtelephones.com, History of the BPO](https://britishtelephones.com/bpo.htm)
- Trunk fees per 3-minute unit:

  | Distance | Fee |
  |---|---|
  | 25 miles or under | 3d |
  | 50 miles | 6d |
  | 75 miles | 9d |
  | 100 miles | 1s |
  | Each additional 40 miles | 6d |

  Six minutes cost double, and no one could hold a trunk line for more than six minutes. From 7 pm to 7 am, six minutes cost the day 3-minute fee. Callers originating or receiving trunk calls at a call office paid a 2d call office fee in the Provinces, and all fees were prepaid or deposited. Quarter-hour bookings were possible at slack times at three-quarters of the normal fee, e.g. London–Birmingham at "£6 8s. 9d. per month of 22 days" (OCR; the per-day figure beside it is garbled). — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913); confirmed by the [1913 Belfast directory preface](https://lennonwylie.co.uk/1913PhoneList1.htm)
- Provincial subscription rates were not printed: "The terms for Telephone Exchange circuits may be ascertained on application to the District Manager".
  - London 1913 rates, for comparison: message rate £5 a year plus 1d per call within the County of London, with a minimum of 30s a year in message fees; party lines £3; unlimited service £17 for the first line and £14 for additional lines.
  - Extension lines were 30s a year within the same premises (up to 110 yards) and £4 between premises (up to ¼ mile). "Special rates are quoted for private branch exchanges."
  - Private telephone and telegraph lines were rented from the Post Office. A private line to a telegraph office carried a desk and clerk charge, "usually £3 6s. a year in the Provinces" (OCR).

  — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- Telephone telegrams and express letters: subscribers could telephone telegrams to the Post Office and have express letters taken down by telephone, 30 words per express fee. The Guide's OCR reads "80"; the 1913 Belfast directory gives "30 words". Telegrams could be addressed to a telephone number, e.g. "Jones, Midland 245, Birmingham". — [Post Office Guide 1913](https://archive.org/details/BPOG-1913)
- Real Coventry listings in the *1914 Who's Who in Business*:
  - Rudge-Whitworth: "Telephone: No. 2 (G.P.O.) and 404 (National), Coventry. Telegraphic and Cable Address: 'Rudge, Coventry.'"
  - Alfred Herbert Ltd (machine tools, staff 2,000): "Telephone: No. 860 Coventry (6 lines). Telegraphic Address: 'Lathe, Coventry,' Codes: A B C, Lieber's, Private, Western Union."
  - Rotherham & Sons (cycle and motor accessories, watches): "Nos. 14 and 245 Coventry"
  - Thomas Stevens Ltd: "No. 488 Coventry… 'Graphic, Coventry.'"
  - Randle radiator works: "No. 675 Coventry… 'Coolers, Coventry.'"
  - Perkins & Sons: "No. 74 Coventry… 'Bouquet, Coventry.'"
  - Cadbury, for comparison: "Code: Lieber's"

  — [Grace's Guide, 1914 Who's Who in Business: R](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_R); [H](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_H); [S](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_S); [P](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_P); [C](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_C)
- Coventry exchanges: forum contributors cite a 1905 directory listing the National Telephone Company at "Theatre Yard, 17-18 Smithford Street", and a later GPO exchange in the Hertford Street post office building, entered from Greyfriars Lane. This is local forum testimony and is unverified. — [Historic Coventry Forum](https://historiccoventryforum.co.uk/main/forum-posts.php?id=29509)
- Coventry was not among the 13 towns that obtained municipal telephone licences under the Telegraph Act 1899, according to a search summary: Glasgow, Belfast, Grantham, Huddersfield, Tunbridge Wells, Brighton, Chard, Portsmouth, Hull, Oldham, Swansea, Scarborough and West Hartlepool. The fetched britishtelephones.com page lists only the six municipalities that actually opened systems: Glasgow 1901, Tunbridge Wells 1901, Swansea 1902, Portsmouth 1902, Brighton 1903 and Hull 1904. Either way, Coventry's 1913 service was Post Office only. — [britishtelephones.com, History of the BPO](https://britishtelephones.com/bpo.htm)
- Internal telephones: in Spencer's 1914 works routine "it is the duty of the works manager's clerk to telephone the necessary inquiry" to the storeroom about stock. — [Spencer (1914)](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- ABC code: *The ABC Universal Commercial Electric Telegraphic Code*, 5th edition of 1901 on archive.org, "specially adapted for the use of financiers, merchants, shipowners…". — [archive.org, ABC code (1901)](https://archive.org/details/abcuniversalcom00clau)

### Inferences
- Rudge-Whitworth's two numbers, "G.P.O." and "National", show that in 1913–14 Coventry still had two parallel exchange systems, the old Post Office exchange and the ex-National Telephone Company exchange. They were not yet unified, so a large works might keep a line on each. This is inferred from the listing.
- Sample trunk costs, estimated from the scale with distances measured roughly between exchanges: Coventry–Birmingham (about 18–20 miles) 3d per 3 minutes; Coventry–London (about 94 miles) 1s; Coventry–Liverpool (about 100–110 miles) 1s or 1s 6d.
- A message model for a 1913 works:
  - post four times a day, with the first delivery the heaviest (orders, invoices, railway advices)
  - telegrams brought by a uniformed boy on a bicycle within minutes to an hour, often in ABC or Lieber's code, costing 6d for 12 words
  - a telephone or private branch exchange (PBX) in the office with one or more exchange lines
  - internal telephones from the works office to the stores and despatch
  - trunk calls booked through the operator in 3-minute units

### Gaps
- No 1913 Coventry post office delivery or collection times were found; the Coventry head office entry in the Guide's office list is garbled in the OCR.
- Provincial telephone subscription rates and the size of the Coventry exchange or its subscriber numbers in 1913 were not found.
- The exact 1913 porterage rate per mile for telegrams needs checking against the page scan.

## 7. What a goods yard and a works despatch bay looked like (for a stylised 3D model)

### Takeaway
The Coventry goods yard of about 1913 was a walled, brick-built yard off Warwick Road. It contained long single-storey goods sheds with through sidings and internal platforms, a two-storey LNWR range along the street with stables below, a Midland goods office with a painted sign, a weighbridge hut at the gate, a hand crane, cattle pens and wagon turntables. Horse-drawn lorries and vans worked it under a chief cartage foreman. The wagons were lead-grey LNWR opens and vans with white diamonds and "L N W R" lettering, sheeted with tarpaulins. A works despatch bay of the period would have a packing shop that made its own cases, stencilled shipping marks and a despatch clerk's office with carbon-copy books.

### Cited Findings
- The elements of the Coventry yard are documented with dates of c. 1890, 1910 and 1920: boundary wall, weighbridge office at the entrance, cattle pens, joint LNWR/MR shed, No. 1 and No. 2 goods sheds, a two-storey LNWR building with stables, a 5-ton hand crane, wagon turntables and main offices with claims and telephone rooms. — [warwickshirerailways.com lnwrcov3728](https://www.warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrcov3728.htm); [lnwrcov554c](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrcov554c.htm); [plans & maps](https://www.warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry_plans_&_maps.htm)
- Generic goods sheds had a through track and an internal platform, with "a 30-hundredweight jib crane" for heavy "smalls". Capstans were hydraulically powered iron drums whose ropes pulled wagons. Goods offices had weighbridges. — [Wikipedia, Goods shed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goods_shed); [Historic England, Railway Goods Sheds and Warehouses IHA](https://live.historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/iha-railway-goods-sheds-warehouses/heag115-railway-goods-sheds-and-warehouses-iha)
- LNWR wagon insignia: white diamonds until 1908, "L N W R" letters after 1908, and diamonds ceased c. 1915. Bodies were medium lead grey. — [HMRS](https://hmrs.org.uk/lnwr-wcjs-loco-coach-and-wagon-insignia.html); [RMweb citing *LNWR Wagons* Vol. 1](https://www.rmweb.co.uk/forums/topic/114259-lnwr-van-livery/page/2/)
- Cartage staff at Coventry wore distinguishing hatbands, "three different hatbands" in one photo. There were "many horses stabled at Coventry station". — [warwickshirerailways.com, Coventry station](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry.htm)
- GWR single-horse vans had about 2 ft wooden sides and a canvas sheet roof on iron hoops. The 1920 GWR one-horse trolley had 3 ft 3 in wheels, 8 ft shafts and a canvas hood. — [warwickshirerailways.com GWR](https://warwickshirerailways.com/gwr/gwrhd705.htm)
- Works side: "packing and marking are supervised by the forwarding clerk, who is authorised to requisition from the woodshop any packing cases". A numerical register of returnable packing cases was kept. The Delivery Advice Note was octavo size, and the Goods Received Book measured 9½ × 18 in. — [Spencer (1914)](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)

### Inferences
- Suggested stylised palette, as an inference rather than a documented specification:
  - dark red-brown brick
  - slate roofs
  - wagons in lead grey with white lettering
  - black ironwork
  - dark green, brown or grey tarpaulins, unverified
  - railway carts possibly in dark company colours (unverified)
  - works vans with the maker's name sign-written
- Staff to show in the yard:
  - one or more gangs of a checker, caller-off and two to four porters in the shed
  - a crane man
  - a shunt horse with its driver, or a capstan man
  - a weighbridge clerk
  - several carmen with horses
  - the foreman
  - a number-taker
- The despatch bay at the works: a covered loading dock at cart-bed height (about 3–4 ft), a woodshop for crates, a stencil table, platform scales, and stacked cases marked like this example:

  ```
    ◇
   RW
  1/6
  BOMBAY
  ```

  It would also have a clerk's desk with carbon-copy delivery advice books, a consignment note pad and a telephone.
