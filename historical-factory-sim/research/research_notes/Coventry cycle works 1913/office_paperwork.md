# Office organisation, documents and paper flows of a British engineering/manufacturing works, c. 1913

Scope note for the report writer. Almost everything below comes from the full texts of British period textbooks, read on archive.org:

- **Elbourne 1914**: E. T. Elbourne, *Factory Administration and Accounts* (Longmans, London, March 1914). This is the backbone source. Its Section V gives 145 specimen "routine forms", each with a note on who makes it, how many copies and where they go. The specimen firm is "W. Blank & Co. Ltd., Efficiency Works", a London engineering works. Elbourne had been Works Accountant at Vickers, Sons & Maxim (Erith) and a departmental works manager at BSA (Birmingham Small Arms), which matters because BSA was a cycle maker. [Elbourne 1914](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Spencer 1907**: Henry Spencer, *The Commercial Organisation of Engineering Factories* (Spon; preface Glasgow, January 1907; "new impression" 1914). It describes a motor-car works ("car costs book", "new cars", "cars returned after repairs"), which is a close analogue for a Coventry cycle and motor firm. [Spencer 1907/1914](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Burton 1899**: F. G. Burton, *The Commercial Management of Engineering Works* (Manchester, 1899). [Burton 1899](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
- **Dicksee 1906**: L. R. Dicksee, *Office Organisation and Management* (Pitman, first edition 1906). [Dicksee 1906](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- **Hawkins 1905**: L. W. Hawkins, *Cost Accounts* (Gee & Co., 1905). [Hawkins 1905](https://archive.org/details/costaccountsexpl00hawk)
- **Best 1911**: J. W. Best, *Cost Accounts with special reference to those of an Engineer and Ironfounder* (2nd ed., Gee & Co., 1911; Sheffield). [Best 1911](https://archive.org/details/costaccountswith00bestrich)

Page numbers are printed page numbers in those books. The texts are OCR, and where the OCR was garbled (some form sizes) I say so. I did not obtain J. Slater Lewis (1896): only a 1980 reprint exists on archive.org and it has no full text. Nor did I obtain Pearn & Pearn (1904); it is not on archive.org under that title. I did not use Garcke & Fells, because only the 1922 edition was readable and it falls outside the date window.

## 1. How was the office organised (departments, job titles, staff numbers, hours, equipment)?

### Takeaway
By 1907–1914 British textbooks described a works office split into named functions. On the commercial side these were correspondence, orders, invoicing and sales ledger, buying, cashier and bought ledger. On the works side they were the works office (production, ratefixing and tool-design sections), the works accounts or cost office, the wages office, the general stores, the work depot, the warehouse and despatch, and the drawing office. In a mid-sized works several of these were one or two clerks each, sharing a general office.

The equipment was a mixture of old and new:
- New: typewriters, carbon copies, card indexes, vertical files, loose-leaf ledgers, card time recorders, rotary duplicators, house telephones and messenger-boy "works post".
- Persisting: press-copy letter books (still legally preferred for signed letters), bound books for the financial ledgers and some calculating machines.

### Cited Findings

**Departments and functions**
- Elbourne's "Diagram of Staff Functions" divides General Administration into Sales Administration, Works Administration and Financial Administration. Correspondence is a supplementary function of General Administration. Works Accounts sit under both Works and Financial Administration, with the primary line to Financial. — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 28–29](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- His "Representative Staff Arrangement Diagram" runs Board of Directors → General Manager → Sales Manager, Works Manager, Chief Designer and Financial Manager.
  - Positions shown below them: Travellers, Draughtsmen, Foremen, Plant Engineer, Works Accountant and Estimator, Buyer, Cashier, Storekeeper, Inspector, Tool Designer, Ratefixer, Wages Clerk, Gatekeeper and Warehouseman.
  - The note says "to simplify diagram assistants are not indicated."
  - The Secretary and Accountant are merged as "Financial Manager", though "quite usually separate officers act in the respective positions." — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 30–31](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- The Works Accountant is often doubled up as Estimator. "Sometimes the duties of a Chief Cost Clerk approximate to those advocated for a Works Accountant." — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 30–31](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Elbourne complains that the Buyer is "very often, and it may be said much too often" a man with "little or no technical qualifications", who is then "really a special clerk." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 32](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- The **Works Office** contains a "Production Section", a "Rate-fixing Section", a "Tool Designing Section" and so on. It sets due dates, raises purchase requisitions for production materials and controls reserve stock. — [Elbourne 1914, p. 175](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- The **Work Depot** is a shop-floor "collecting and distributing station for all work in progress", run by a Work Depot Chargehand under the head of the Production Section. — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 176–178](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Official orders are issued from one source. Depending on the firm this is the Correspondence Office or the Estimating Office, and "it is not uncommon, where orders are numerous, to have a separate Order Department." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 62](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer's chapter list for a motor works sets out its departments: Correspondence; Contracting (i.e. buying); Receiving; Storeroom; Estimating; Advertising; Orders Received; Drawing Office; Costs (which includes "time and wages office"); Forwarding; Accountant's; Cashier's; Selling Agents and Travellers; Secretary's. — [Spencer 1907, Contents](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Dicksee's general-office layout:
  - The cash staff are "partitioned off" with the cashier's own safe.
  - Other areas of the room are allotted to "the orders and supplies clerks", "the sales and invoice department", "the wages", and "the prime cost and estimating assistants".
  - The drawing office should be housed separately even if it is one man, and travellers get separate quarters.
  - Dicksee recommends "the ordinary sloping desk with a single line of drawers". — [Dicksee 1906, pp. 17–18](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- Comparator office, not a factory: the Maypole Dairy office at Southall (c. 1906) had about 30 office workers, mostly men. They sat at "long double-sided desks with overhead racks and the clerks on stools each side". — [Southall history (Maypole)](https://www.southall-history.co.uk/the-maypole-and-southall-green/11/)

**Job titles found in the period texts**
- Secretary; Accountant; Cashier; Petty Cashier; Chief Clerk; Correspondence Clerk or "Correspondent"; typists ("lady clerks"); Order Clerk; Invoice Clerk; Bought Ledger Clerk; Sales Ledger and Sales Cash Book clerks; Buyer or purchasing agent; Storekeeper; Receiving Clerk; Stores Server; Wages Clerk; Pay Clerks; Timekeeper; Job Clerk; Ratefixer; Progress Clerk; Inspector or viewers; Despatch or forwarding clerk; Warehouseman; Door Attendant; Gatekeeper; office boys and messenger boys. — Sources:
  - [Elbourne 1914, pp. 30–31, 44–48, 106–108, 149, 179](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
  - [Spencer 1907, pp. 1–14, 69–73, 99–112, 161–187](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
  - [Burton 1899, pp. 87–95](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
- Elbourne lists the progress clerk's synonyms: "a Progress Clerk, Tracer, Chaser, Worrier — whatever his designation — under the direction of the Works Office." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 179](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Women as typists:
  - Burton (1899): "Ladies undoubtedly make good, if not indeed the best, typists… a separate room should be provided for them."
  - He also reports girls employed as tracers in a large engineering firm in North Britain. — [Burton 1899, p. 93](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
- Mail-room juniors:
  - The postal register "office boy or junior clerk" keeps the postage book. — [Burton 1899, pp. 94–95](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
  - Spencer complains mailing is "too frequently left to… a mere boy". In large establishments "two being told off by the chief clerk each day" do it. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 6–7](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)

**Staff numbers**
- Burton: "Where the works are of small or moderate size, employing up to 500 or 600 hands, it is undoubtedly better to write all the letters in the manager's or general office." In other words, one central correspondence point. — [Burton 1899, p. 91](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
- Dicksee notes that in smaller concerns there may be "but one clerk dealing with each separate branch of the clerical work." — [Dicksee 1906, pp. 17–18](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- All British industry covered by the Census of Production:
  - 1907: 6,493,129 operatives against 490,947 "administrative, clerical and technical staff".
  - That is about 7.6 salaried staff per 100 operatives.
  - Source: Nature (1936) comparing the 1907 and 1930 censuses. I saw this only in a search-result excerpt and did not read the full text. — [Nature 138:653 (1936)](https://www.nature.com/articles/138653a0)

**Office hours**
- Comparator, not engineering: "9.00 am. to 6.00 pm., with one hour for lunch, 9.00 am. to 12 pm. noon Saturday". Staff were not paid overtime, and "no such thing as holidays". This is from a 1906 Maypole Dairy employee. — [Southall history (Maypole)](https://www.southall-history.co.uk/the-maypole-and-southall-green/11/)
- Elbourne on staff discipline:
  - Staff should record attendance "on arrival in the morning and on leaving at night".
  - For small staffs an Attendance Book in which each signs is sufficient. If a recorder is used, it should be "of the signature type".
  - Staff overtime is rewarded by a monthly award or "so much per night", and "tea may reasonably be provided by the Company". — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 37–38](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Shop hours are a different thing. Elbourne's specimen engagement form says "per week of 53 hours". — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-16, p. 407](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

**Equipment**
- Typewriters:
  - "The office which nowadays conducts its correspondence by means of the pen is antiquated indeed."
  - "upwards of twenty makes" are on the English market, "nearly all… of American origin". The Salter is one of the few English machines.
  - The Elliott-Fisher book typewriter "will make a carbon copy of a letter in the letter-book, or of an invoice in the day book". — [Dicksee 1906, pp. 25–27](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- Burton (1899): the typewriter has become "almost indispensable"; "several duplicate copies may be obtained… by means of carbonised paper". — [Burton 1899, p. 92](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
- Calculating machines (Dicksee):
  - Tate's "Arithmometer" (Layton, London), the "Brunsviga", and "Burrough's Adding Machine… perhaps too costly to be used as widely as it deserves".
  - Calculating machines are "not yet sufficiently realised in commercial circles in this country". — [Dicksee 1906, pp. 24–25](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- Elbourne says that summarising the Weekly Time Allocation Sheets into the Departmental Wages Allocation Summary is done "by mechanical means — any other method being prohibitive in time and cost." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-30, p. 421](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Comptometer: by 1918 Herbert E. Robbins Ltd were "sole concessionaire for the United Kingdom" for Felt & Tarrant Comptometers, at Finsbury Square, London. — [Historic England photo BL24245](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/photos/item/BL24245)
- Duplicators and copying:
  - The Roneo rotary stencil duplicator: "Sixty copies per minute is a good average", "up to five thousand copies… from one original".
  - Rotary press-copying onto a continuous tissue roll gives loose copies for filing. — [Dicksee 1906, pp. 27–28](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- Elbourne recommends gelatine or "process slab" copying for home-made forms in small quantities, and "Forms of simple character should be duplicated by means of the typewriter." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 40](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Addressing machines:
  - Customers' names and addresses on rubber or die plates in an endless chain, used for envelopes and ledger headings (the "Addressograph"). — [Dicksee 1906, p. 24](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
  - Elbourne proposes addressing machines to pre-head Wages Sheets and Weekly Time Allocation Sheets, the "stencil method being perhaps more convenient". — [Elbourne 1914, p. 105; Forms 5-28, 5-31](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Internal communication:
  - Bell pushes with codes: "one ring being for the shorthand clerk, two for the principal assistant, three for the office boy". With several chiefs, an indicator board with dropping discs shows who rang.
  - "Some offices still patronise the old-fashioned speaking-tubes; but these are most unsanitary". House telephones are recommended. — [Dicksee 1906, pp. 28–29](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- Elbourne (1914): "When there is a private telephone exchange this has frequently to be looked after by the Door Attendant". He suggests a telephone in the waiting room so callers can ring department heads. — [Elbourne 1914, p. 46](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Dicksee mentions pneumatic tubes only for factory and warehouse internal transport, together with lifts and trolley ways. — [Dicksee 1906, p. 67](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- Card indexes: the standard card sizes for card index systems are 3 × 5 in., 4 × 6 in. and 5 × 8 in. — [Elbourne 1914, p. 510](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Paper sizes: Elbourne tabulates writing papers (Foolscap, Post, Demy, Large Post, Medium, Royal, Imperial) and their folio and quarto subdivisions. Foolscap folio is about 13½ × 8½ in.; the OCR is slightly unclear. — [Elbourne 1914, p. 510](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Loose-leaf and card ledgers:
  - Spencer recommends a loose-leaf stores ledger on a "Premier" patent file. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 37–38](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
  - Elbourne's wages sheets, works expenditure book and stock ledgers are all on "removable sheets". — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-31, 5-115, 5-123](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Filing equipment: Burton names the Shannon, Amberg and Ceres files, noting "The Shannon and Amberg are of American make". — [Burton 1899, p. 95](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)

### Inferences
- **Suggested office roster for a 300–500-hand Coventry cycle works.** This is my construction, not a period source. It applies the roughly 7.6 per 100 Census ratio and Burton's single-general-office rule.
  - Totals: about 25–35 salaried staff overall, of whom perhaps 12–20 are clerical. The rest are managers, travellers, draughtsmen and ratefixers.
  - Commercial roster: Secretary/Accountant; Cashier (doubling as petty cashier); Chief Clerk; 1 correspondence clerk plus 1–2 typists; 1 order and invoice clerk; 1 sales ledger clerk; 1 bought ledger clerk; Buyer plus 1 clerk.
  - Works roster: Works Accountant/Estimator plus 2 cost clerks; 2 wages clerks plus a timekeeper; Storekeeper, receiving clerk and 1–2 stores servers; Works Office production clerk plus a progress chaser; 1 ratefixer; 2–3 draughtsmen; warehouse and despatch clerk; gatekeeper; 2–3 office or messenger boys.
- The "office" is therefore best modelled as one general office with partitioned zones following Dicksee's layout:
  - cash cage
  - orders and buying
  - sales, invoicing and ledgers
  - wages
  - cost and estimating
- Separate small offices sit beside it for the manager, secretary and drawing office.
- Separately, out in the works: the works office, the stores office, the gatehouse/timekeeper's office and the warehouse/despatch office.
- Office hours of roughly 9–6 Monday–Friday and 9–1 Saturday are a plausible default. The works runs a 53-hour week from about 6 a.m., so the office opens after the works. This is inferred from a non-engineering comparator plus Elbourne.

### Gaps
- There are no period figures specific to Coventry cycle works for clerical headcount or office hours. I found no surviving staff list for Rudge-Whitworth, Singer, Triumph or similar in online sources. The Coventry Archives and Modern Records Centre would be the places to look.
- When the Comptometer reached ordinary British works offices before 1914 is not established. I have a UK concessionaire only for 1918.
- I could not read Anderson's *Victorian Clerks* or Heller's *London Clerical Workers 1880–1914* online for hours and pay.

## 2. Sales-order flow (post → acknowledgement → order book → works order → despatch → invoice → day book → ledger → statement)

### Takeaway
In 1907–14 the customer's order was handled in this sequence:
1. Scrutinised for credit and for terms by the General Manager or Estimator.
2. Acknowledged the same day, typically by printed postcard.
3. Entered in an order book, or typed as a multi-copy "Office Order" (Elbourne: six carbon copies at one typing, to invoice clerk, works accountant/estimator, works manager, drawing office, works office and warehouse).
4. Filled from warehouse stock or put through the works.
5. Despatched with a packing slip and an advice of despatch whose General Office copy triggers the invoice. The quickest method is to write the invoice as a carbon copy of the advice.
6. Entered in a numbered Sales Day Book, posted to the Sales Ledger by invoice number and summarised monthly by statement.

### Cited Findings

**Receipt of orders and credit check**
- Orders "will advisably first find their way to the general manager". The most important question is "his financial standing". The safeguards are "cash with order", "on delivery", or "cash against bill of lading". — [Spencer 1907, p. 69](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- "Orders as received from customers will be subject to scrutiny, first as to the credit status of the firm, and secondly, as to any technical or commercial conditions." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 62](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Credit status is checked by subscribing to agencies, which is preferred to trade references. Replies are kept in the cashier's department, filed with a card index. — [Spencer 1907, p. 178](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Elbourne's Sales Promotion Index Card (5 × 8 in.) has fields for Credit Status, Traveller, Correspondence File No., Traveller's Calls, Literature sent, Form Letters sent, Enquiries received, Quotations sent and Orders received. Cards may be in different colours for different travellers' districts, or for exporters, factors and agents. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-8, p. 395](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

**Acknowledgement**
- "Acknowledgment should be made if goods are not despatched the same day." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 62](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Elbourne's Acknowledgment of Order (Form 5-13) reads: "Your Order, No… for… is to hand and has our strict attention. We enter at prices as per… and anticipate making delivery…". It "may be in postcard form with advertising matter thereon". — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-13, p. 401](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer: acknowledge "on the day of their receipt" with a postcard "printed in copyable ink with a vacant space for customer's reference". — [Spencer 1907, p. 72](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)

**Order book and the order sent to the works**
- Spencer's **Orders Received Book / "Office Order to Factory"** (Fig. 38, quarto):
  - It is "a manifold carbon copying book of quadruplicate pages, size 8 in. by 10½ in.", with an A–Z index.
  - Three copies are perforated and go to the works manager. He files one and passes the others to the costs and forwarding departments.
  - The fourth stays in the book as office reference and "alone is permitted to show the price… for the information of the invoice clerk".
  - Rule: "no work is to go forward in the factory except by the authority of a record in the 'orders received' book." — [Spencer 1907, pp. 70–72](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- The costs department then allots a manufacturing or factory order number. The order clerk records part and full deliveries from forwarding department particulars. Customers' original orders are returned to the correspondence department for filing. Delivery-date penalty clauses are watched "with the aid of his diary". — [Spencer 1907, pp. 72–73](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Spencer's Factory Order** (Fig. 61, 7 × 5 in.):
  - "No work must be done without instructions on this form."
  - Particulars are dictated to a typist, and copies are "pasted in the departmental order books".
  - Orders for saleable stock are distinguished from those for establishment extensions and maintenance "by printing in distinctive colours". — [Spencer 1907, pp. 116–117](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Elbourne's Office Order** (Form 5-12, 10 × 8 in.):
  - Typewritten; "with suitable paper and machine, six copies may be obtained at one typing".
  - Copies go to: 1. Invoice Clerk; 2. Estimator and Works Accountant; 3. Works Manager; 4. Drawing Office; 5. Works Office; 6. Warehouse.
  - Sales Repairs and Sundries orders may manage with three copies: Invoice Clerk, Works Office and Warehouse.
  - "The bottom right hand corner is to be cut off when the order is completely invoiced."
  - Fields: Order No.; Date issued; Copy for…; Customer; Customer's Reference; Date; Delivery (address of consignee); Packages Marked; Route; Carriage; Delivery required; Invoice Ref.; Checked; Acknowledgment sent; Order approved; Price and Terms of Payment; Our Tender No.; Shipping Instructions; Marine Insurance; Deliveries (Despatch Ref., Quantity, Date); Completed; Signed. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-12, p. 401](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Order number series:
  - "Series A — Sales Orders", "B — Sales Repairs and Sundries Orders", "C" for stock manufactures, "D" for developments and experiments.
  - A sales order used as a production order takes a P, e.g. "AP 1001, BP 2457".
  - Guarantee work is marked G, e.g. "B 690 G". — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 63–64](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Elbourne suggests the Estimator issues Sales Orders and fills in delivery due dates. A complete set of all Sales Orders goes "to the Warehouse — and also to the General Office for invoice reference". The Warehouse also receives a copy of every Production Order. — [Elbourne 1914, p. 215](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Warehouse Sub-Orders** are issued by the warehouseman, always under an official Sales Order, for packing cases, minor modifications and so on.
  - They carry a perforated coupon on which the Works Office fills in the expected delivery and returns it.
  - The Warehouse files the coupons "in sequence of delivery dates".
  - A carbon copy of each goes to the Works Accounts Office. — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 216–217](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Sales Sundries Order Specification** (Form 5-51):
  - Made out in the Drawing Office from the customer's instructions, for issue to the Warehouse. It "constitutes the official order" for spares.
  - The Warehouse issues Warehouse Memos to the Works Office for parts it needs. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-51, p. 437](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

**Release of finished product**
- **Works Product Note** (Form 5-108):
  - Made out "in triplicate by the Work Depot, one copy passing to the Warehouse or General Stores, and one to the Works Office (Production Section), and thence to the Works Accounts Office".
  - "No product not already in the Warehouse should be despatched without the authority of this note." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-108, p. 483](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Progressive No. Register** (Form 5-107, 13 × 8 in.): progressive or serial numbers of complete products, cross-referenced to production order, erecting sub-order, sales reference and Works Product Note. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-107, p. 483; p. 64](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Warehouse Stock Record** (Form 5-110, 8½ × 11 in.): one line per item received ("rotation Nos."), showing appropriation to customer orders with the delivery promised. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-110, p. 485](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

**Despatch documents**
- **Packing Slip** (Form 5-112, 6½ × 8 in.):
  - "in triplicate, one remaining in Warehouse for reference, one going with package and one passing to General Office for invoicing purposes."
  - Each day's papers are fastened together and lent to the Works Office, to clear the Production Programme, and to the Works Accounts Office, for the Delivered Orders Cost Abstract.
  - Printed caution: "The above goods should be examined before being signed for. If this is not possible, sign for as 'Unexamined.'" — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-112, p. 487](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Advice of Despatch** (Form 5-113, 6½ × 8 in.):
  - Fields: Date; A.D. No.; To; Per; Consignee's Ref.; Carr.; Packages; Value; Marks; Measurements; Gross Weight (cwts, qrs, lbs); "as per Packing Slip"; Carrier's Receipt.
  - One copy can serve as the carrier's consignment note. "One remaining in the Warehouse, being signed by Carrier as acknowledgment of receipt, and the other going to the General Office for completing the invoice."
  - The customer's copy "should go by post". "A separate advice should be used for each Sales Order." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-113, p. 487; pp. 219–220](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- The alternative is a **Despatch Book** passed between Warehouse and Financial Department on alternate days, "one book for Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, and another for Tuesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays". Elbourne says this "cripples the work in both departments". "The quickest way is to make the invoice out in blank as a carbon copy of the advice." — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 219–220](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer's **Delivery Advice** (Fig. 83, octavo):
  - Kept in "manifold carbon-copying books of duplicate pages, one of which is perforated".
  - It covers new cars, cars returned after repair, parts on loan, parts on approval, replacement parts, and patterns sent to steel founders.
  - "With the passing of [the advice], accompanied by the despatch clerk's copy of the office order, we have an implied authority for an entry in the sales daybook." — [Spencer 1907, pp. 161–162](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Every consignment also gets a carrier's instruction or delivery note, with a duplicate kept and the carrier's acknowledgement of receipt on its face. Elbourne advises the sender to use "a printed form of his own" for railway consignment notes. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 164–165](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich); [Elbourne 1914, p. 221](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Returnable packages:
  - Each is numbered, the number branded or painted on, and logged on an **Outwards Package Tracing Card** (4 × 6 in.).
  - Fortnightly lists of outstanding packages go to the General Office. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-114, p. 487; pp. 217–218](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Warehouse Daily Report of Despatches from Stock** (Form 5-111): carbon duplicate, sent to the Works Accounts Office. It also gives fortnightly sales-by-class totals for management. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-111, p. 485](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

**Invoice, day book, ledger, statement**
- Spencer:
  - "The work of invoicing or billing commences with the mailing of advice notes, the bound duplicates of which are handed to the invoice department."
  - The **Sales Daybook** (Fig. 88) is 12 × 19 in.
  - The clerk endorses his copies of the office order and despatch advice with the daybook folio.
  - "In small factories there is a growing custom to use duplicate carbon-copying books as a combined daybook and invoice." — [Spencer 1907, pp. 173–175](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Elbourne:
  - "Every invoice, whether a press copy invoice book is used, or carbon copies of invoices retained, must bear a suitable reference number." The number appears in the Sales Day Book and the Sales Ledger, and ledger postings "bear the invoice or credit note number instead of the… folio".
  - Loose-sheet day books are used where there are several classes of sales.
  - Each invoice and credit note is initialled by the Accountant's Department "indelible pencil being used, so that the Press copy… shews the initial". — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 528–532](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Elbourne's sales books: Sales Cash Book, Sales Day Book, Sales Returns Book, Sales Ledger, Bills Receivable Book and Sales Ledger Balances Book. Balances are extracted monthly, "debit balances… in black ink, the credit balances in red". — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 511, 528–535](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Statements: "It is a custom universally adopted to make monthly copies of the various accounts in the debtors' ledger, and to furnish each customer with a statement… per se, a silent demand for payment." — [Spencer 1907, p. 175](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Credit notes to customers: "a special form closely resembling the form of invoice, but distinguished therefrom by printing in red", copied from a "returns daybook". — [Spencer 1907, p. 176](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Cash received:
  - Elbourne: a **Cash Inwards Book** entered "during the process of dealing with the mail and before the letters are issued from the Board Room", checked by the Secretary and Correspondence Clerk. — [Elbourne 1914, p. 44](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
  - The Bank Paying-in Book is written up by the Accountant's Department and passed to the Sales Cash Book clerk. "Receipts under no circumstances should be used for payment of expenses." — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 528, 533](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
  - Spencer: printed numbered receipt books with counterfoils, cheques banked promptly. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 178–179](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Specimen terms on a tender: "Terms of account: Monthly, less 2½% cash discount". Packages are charged and credited in full if returned promptly, carriage paid. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-11, p. 399](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Burton treats "Collection of Accounts by Travellers" and travellers' "Daily Reports" under the Sales Department. I saw these as headings only and did not read the content. — [Burton 1899, Contents, ch. VIII](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)

### Inferences
- For a cycle works most finished machines would be sold from warehouse stock made under "C" stock orders, rather than built to individual customer order. In Elbourne's terms the sales flow is mostly Sales Order → Warehouse appropriation → Packing Slip / Advice of Despatch → invoice. Special builds and repairs would go through the Drawing Office and works as A–P and B orders.
- The Progressive No. Register maps naturally onto bicycle frame numbers.
- A clean simulation chain for one sale is: post bag → Cash Inwards Book (if a remittance is enclosed) → order letter → credit check card → Office Order typed in six carbon copies (each a separate object) → postcard acknowledgement → Warehouse copy appropriated → Packing Slip (3 copies) and Advice of Despatch (3 copies) → General Office copy plus office order → invoice typed with carbon → Sales Day Book line → Sales Ledger posting → monthly Statement → remittance → Paying-in Book → Sales Cash Book.

### Gaps
- None of the books gives a specimen invoice form layout with column headings for an engineering works. Spencer and Elbourne describe invoices but do not reproduce one; Elbourne's day book specimen is a ruled book.
- Cycle-trade specifics are missing: agents' discount structures, seasonal "booking" orders from agents, catalogue numbers on orders. The only items I found were the existence of large Coventry cycle dealers and factors ([Grace's Guide: Edward O'Brien](https://gracesguide.co.uk/Edward_O'Brien); [Grace's Guide: Hubbard and Sons](https://gracesguide.co.uk/Hubbard_and_Sons)).

## 3. Production control (works orders, job numbers, route sheets, job tickets, progress, drawings, batch sizes, stock vs customer orders)

### Takeaway
Elbourne's 1914 scheme works as follows:
1. An **Office Order**, a "C" Stock Manufacturing Order sanctioned by the General Manager, or a customer order is planned by the **Works Office (Production Section)** on a Production Programme sheet.
2. Drawing Office **Assembly Lists** carry superimposed **Quantity Slips**. These generate purchase requisitions and stock appropriations.
3. A shop-floor **Work Depot** splits the order into **sub-orders**, which are batches sized so that they are "not broken at any operation".
4. Each batch is issued on a **Work Tally** that travels with the work, with a **Stage Ticket** per operation.
5. The **Job Ticket** and the time limit, set by the ratefixer, are the wage and cost link.
6. Shortages are chased via weekly lists and a "V.U." (Very Urgent) stamp applied by a progress chaser.

Spencer's 1907 motor-works version is simpler: Factory Orders pasted in departmental order books, shop lists ticked by foremen, and job/cost cards.

### Cited Findings

**Order types and stock production**
- "It may be presumed that all stock production, however seemingly trivial, shall be sanctioned at least by the General Manager." The Estimator may issue Stock Manufacturing Orders under these sanctions. — [Elbourne 1914, p. 61](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Application for Stock Manufacturing Sanction** (Form 5-57):
  - Columns: Description; Average Monthly Sales; Unappropriated Stock; Last Sanction; Sanction now applied for; Approx. Total Cost; Stock Mfg Order.
  - Prepared in the Works Office, passed by the Works Manager, approved by the General Manager. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-57, p. 443](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Elbourne describes stock orders for "assembled units", drawn later "in various combinations… in accordance with Sales Orders". "Spare parts" are ordered on the basis of past repair consumption. — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 61–62](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

**Drawings and lists**
- **Assembly List** (Form 5-48): "intended for reproduction by photo-printing". The size is one-eighth Double Elephant, about 9½ × 13 in. Line numbers are printed in; the columns are Part No., Name, No. per Assembly Unit (Castings, Forgings, From Bulk, etc.) and Purchase Specification. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-48, p. 435](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Production Instruction** (Form 5-49): "typewritten in the Drawing Office for issue to Works Office, Inspector, etc.", for matters outside the Assembly List. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-49, p. 435](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Print Delivery Ticket and Print Recall Ticket** (Forms 5-45 and 5-46, 2½ × 3½ in., "printed four deep, with fast carbon copy"):
  - The Works Office initials the carbon for receipt.
  - "Identical tickets of distinctive colour" are used between the Works Office and the Works Drawing Stores.
  - Print Index Cards (4 × 6 in.) come in "distinctive colours" for issues to Works, to Suppliers and to Customers. — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-44 to 5-46, p. 433](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Drawing Loan Slip and Tool Loan Slip** (2½ × 3½ in. pads; the tool slip is in a distinctive colour):
  - "This drawing must be returned as soon as finished with, but in any case on Saturday by 12 noon."
  - Tools are exchanged against brass tool checks. — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-90, 5-91, p. 473](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer: shop lists are copied by a typist and bound in "Stolzenberg" files for the works manager, costs, stores and contracting departments. "Each foreman will mark his copy of shop lists with a 'tick'… as he gives them out to his men, and indicate delivery to 'finished store' room by a 'cross tick'." — [Spencer 1907, p. 116](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)

**Planning and progress documents (Elbourne)**
- **Production Programme** (Form 5-52):
  - One sheet per office order, with weekly columns. Stages run Drawings → Materials → Patterns → Castings → Forgings → Jigs/Tools → Machining → Assembling → Erecting → Completion → Despatch. Each stage is cross-referenced to the form that reports it.
  - "Deliveries entered in red."
  - It "constitutes a record of the original planning of the due dates for each stage." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-52, p. 439](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Quantity Slip** (Form 5-53):
  - Made by the Works Office (Production Section) and "superimposed on the Assembly Lists".
  - It allows a margin of reserve material and records stock appropriation, process products, bulk material, purchase requisition number and date available (filled in by the General Stores).
  - Copies go with the Assembly Lists to the manufacturing departments, the General Stores and the Work Depot. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-53, p. 441](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Tools Provided Schedule** (Form 5-58): a photo-print listing process, operation and jigs/tools. Issued to the Works Office, Tool Designer, Machine Shop Foreman and Tool Store Chargehand, and "affixed on the same mount as the detail drawing". — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-58, p. 445](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Tool Sub-Order** (Form 5-59): "made out in triplicate (Tool Room, Works Accounts Office, Issuing Office)". — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-59, p. 445](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Rate Fixing Estimate** (Form 5-60, 5 × 8 in.): one sheet per operation. It separates a "Preparation Allowance" per batch from "Operating Times" per piece. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-60, p. 447](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

**Sub-orders and batch size**
- "Sub-orders are the means by which office orders are split up into proportions suitable for the proper arrangement of work." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 171](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- "The size of a batch of components should be such that it will be feasible to lay down a hard and fast rule that the batch shall not be broken at any operation." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 172](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Batches are set jointly. "The Departmental Foremen will have something to say as to the maximum size of batch they are prepared to pilot through without splitting… the Work Depot must be able to give and take a little." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 176](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Replacements for rejects run under the original sub-order number "prefixed with X". — [Elbourne 1914, p. 174](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Work Tally** (Form 5-100, about 2½ × 5 in.; the OCR is unclear):
  - It "constitute[s] the sub-orders for the manufacture of components". It is made out in the Work Depot.
  - The tally, minus its coupon, goes to the shop foreman as notice that material is ready. The coupon stays in the Work Depot, "the corner being cut off when material has been drawn".
  - The tally accompanies the batch through all operations. Viewers record rejects on the back.
  - Completed coupons go to the Works Office for the Production Programme. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-100, p. 479; pp. 176–178](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- "Work Tallies are sometimes distinctively coloured to indicate urgent or 'rush' work." Elbourne prefers a progress clerk stamping "a large letter rubber stamp 'V.U.'", and "this sign must be respected". "Due dates should be week ending dates." — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 178–179](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Assembly Sub-Order** (Form 5-101, 5 × 8 in.):
  - "Made out in triplicate by the Work Depot, one copy remaining for reference, the second copy accompanying the sets of components to the foreman concerned, and the third… to the Works Office (Production Section)… and thence to the Works Accounts Office."
  - The **Erecting Sub-Order** (Form 5-102) works the same way. — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-101, 5-102, p. 479](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Daily List of Sub-Orders** (Form 5-103, 10 × 8 in.): "made up in carbon duplicate as the Work Tallies… are made out — thus serving as a register of numbers". One copy goes daily to the Works Office and then to the Works Accounts Office to open Cost Allocation Cards. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-103, p. 481](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Weekly Shortage List** (Form 5-105, 10 × 8 in.): "carbon triplicate — one copy passing to the dept. having work in hand, and the second copy to the Works Office (Production Section) for a personal investigation of every item and marking tallies V.U.… The third copy remains in the Work Depot." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-105, p. 481](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Stage Ticket** (Form 5-97):
  - "Briefly a delivery and viewing ticket. It serves as a 'move' instruction to the shop labourer."
  - After viewing it is "sent through the works post to the Wages Office", and a new ticket is made for the next operation. It is finally attached to the Job Ticket. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-97, p. 477](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Viewing Report** (Form 5-98, 4 × 6 in.):
  - "Made out by the View Room in triplicate": one is kept for reference; one goes to the Works Office for replacement, then to the Ratefixer to estimate the cost of the fault, then to the Works Accounts Office; the third goes to the Work Depot with the rejected material.
  - It covers rejects, out-of-limits work passed, drawing errors, transfers between orders, and inspection of purchased goods. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-98, p. 477](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Inspection Certificate** (Form 5-99, 4 × 6 in.): for assembly units and erected product. It goes to the Work Depot, then the Works Office, then the Works Accounts Office. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-99, p. 477](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Departmental Memorandum** (Form 5-106, 4 × 6 in.): "arranged in triplicate, with the second copy in every case passing, by means of the works post, to the Works Office (Production Section)". It is used as a warehouse replenishment requisition, a request on the Tool Stores, a report of delays or plant stoppage, and so on. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-106, p. 483](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Committees.**
  - "Progress Committee — under Works Manager. Chief Designer, Head of Production Office, Estimator and Principal Foreman attend. Position of orders gone into. Overtime requirements settled. Production programme agreed."
  - There are also Sales, Purchases and Tool Committees. — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 38–39](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

### Inferences
- In a Coventry cycle works, frames, hubs, chainwheels and the like would be made in sub-order batches under C-series stock orders. Assembly and erecting sub-orders would draw finished components from the Work Depot.
- The Work Tally plus Stage Ticket is the ideal "physical document that rides on a box of parts" for a 3D simulation. Each operation spawns a new Stage Ticket. The completed one walks (via the works post) to the Wages Office.
- Elbourne describes an "achieved organisation" of an advanced kind; he says the forms "represent an achieved organisation rather than the stages by which that organisation can be obtained" ([Elbourne 1914, p. 387](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). A typical 1913 Coventry firm probably ran something closer to Spencer's factory order, shop list and job slip system. A simulation could treat the Elbourne system as a "progressive" firm's option.

### Gaps
- No textbook here gives an explicit "route sheet" or "process sheet" in the American sense. The nearest equivalents are the Tools Provided Schedule (operations sequence) and the Rate Fixing Estimate.
- I have no direct evidence of batch sizes used in Coventry cycle works.

## 4. Stores and purchasing (requisitions, bin cards, stores ledger, minimum stock, purchase orders, GRN, inspection, invoice matching, bought ledger, returns)

### Takeaway
Purchasing ran on this sequence:
1. A purchase requisition. Elbourne: carbon triplicate from the Works Office, approved by the Works Manager, top copy to the Buyer. Spencer: a foolscap stores requisition book from the storekeeper.
2. An enquiry or tender, if needed.
3. A numbered purchase order in triplicate: supplier; Buyer; Receiving Clerk's copy without prices. Conditions are printed on the back.
4. Follow-up by reminder postcards and an outstanding-orders chart.
5. A Goods Received Note (or Goods Received Book) made by the Receiving Clerk and certified for inspection.
6. Matching of GRN with invoice in the works accounts / accounts office, never in stores, then numbering and entry in the Bought Book.
7. Monthly statements reconciled to the Bought Ledger, a payments list sanctioned by directors, and cheques with receipt vouchers.

Stock records were kept twice: a quantity record at the stores (stock control card, or loose-leaf stores ledger with bin number and min/max) and a value ledger in the accounts office.

### Cited Findings

**Requisition and order**
- **Purchase Requisition** (Elbourne Form 5-54, 6½ × 8 in.):
  - "Made out in carbon triplicate by the Works Office (Production Section), and, after approval by the Works Manager, the top copy is sent to the Buyer and the second copy to the General Stores."
  - The Stores may have to confirm the goods are not in stock.
  - Fields: Date, Requisition No., Purpose, Quantity, Description, Approx. Value, Part No., Office Order No., Purchase Specification, Delivery Requirements, Dept. to whom goods are to be issued, Purchase Order No., Date, Supplier, Certified (Works Office), Approved (Works Manager). — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-54, p. 441](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer's **Stores Requisition Book** (Fig. 15, foolscap) runs from storekeeper to purchasing department. The works manager must "O.K. and initial each item", and purchasing enters the order number against each item. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 20–21](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Enquiry form** (Form 5-14, 10 × 8 in.):
  - "We beg to invite your quotation, stating your best trade and cash discounts… state your shortest time for delivery."
  - Unaccepted quotations are acknowledged by postcard. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-14, p. 403](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Purchase Order** (Form 5-15, 6½ × 8 in. or letter size):
  - "Purchase Orders require to be in triplicate, the second copy remaining with the Buyer and the third copy going to the Receiving Clerk, without prices… all three copies loose… typewritten."
  - "Sometimes… the top or executive copy is copied, after being signed, in a press letter book."
  - Signed "frequently by the General Manager". The Buyer sees every order acknowledged, and the General Stores take over following up delivery.
  - Printed text: "It is important to us that this Order No. be marked on the packages and quoted on Advice of Despatch and Invoice… We rely on your sending us by post an Advice of Despatch the same day as goods are sent… Usual Terms: Monthly, less 2½%." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-15, p. 405](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Petty purchase orders may be issued under lesser authority with a limit of "say £1". "In some instances, all orders above £50 in value have to be signed by the Managing Director." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 146](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer's **Purchasing Order Form** (Fig. 16):
  - Either "bound in book form with counterfoil or carbon duplicates, or… loose sheets printed in copyable ink, when used in conjunction with the copying press".
  - Conditions are printed on the back.
  - Terms note: "Our pay day is third Wednesday in the month, and remittances are made for all accounts passed in respect to deliveries for the preceding month." — [Spencer 1907, pp. 21–22](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Purchasing Orders Register** (Fig. 17, 17 × 12 in., A–Z index by supplier). If an order goes unacknowledged, "defaulters are to be communicated with two days after" by postcard.
- A rubber stamp on the copy order records acknowledgement, advice and invoice (Fig. 18).
- An **Outstanding Orders Chart** (Fig. 19) is a large pasteboard-mounted sheet with order numbers written under due dates, three months ahead. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 23–25](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Elbourne's follow-up:
  - **Delivery Reminder Card** (Form 5-80), a postcard with a product advertisement on the back.
  - **Purchase Order Endorsement** (Form 5-81), a rubber stamp on the Stores copy with boxes for Delivery Reminder, Telephone Enquiries, Letters Sent, Telegrams, Quantity Received, Date, G.R. Ref., Returns.
  - Weekly statements of overdue deliveries. "Lurid colours of paper" on urging forms are not taken seriously. — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-80, 5-81, p. 465; pp. 147–148](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

**Receipt and inspection**
- **Goods Received Note** (Form 5-82, 6½ × 8 in., "printed two deep, in book style, with the carbon duplicate fast"):
  - "Made out by the Receiving Clerk in carbon duplicate, the duplicate being retained in the General Stores." The loose note is signed by the inspector.
  - It is then "passed to the Works Office (Production Section) for noting on the Production Programme… and then passed to the Works Accounts Office, where it will be used to check the invoice, and be completed as to prices."
  - Fields: Supplier; Purchase Requisition No.; Purchase Order No.; Date Goods Received; Ref. G.R.; Entered for Stock Control; Description; Per (carrier); Quan. rec'd; Rejections; Notes; Price; Carr.; Certified by; Goods inspected by; Inv. No.; Ret'ble Packages; Invoice Cost; Cost Allocation Ref.; Credit Claims. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-82, p. 465](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- GRNs are "numbered serially using a prefix such as 'G.R.'".
  - The Receiving Clerk marks off receipts "on the stores copy of the purchase order… held under supplier's names".
  - Suppliers must send advice notes separately from invoices.
  - "Suppliers' invoices shall not be sent beyond the Works Accounts Office, however trustworthy the Stores Staff may be." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 149](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Matching: the completed GRN goes to the Works Accounts Office "never later than the day following receipt". It is marked off against their copy of the purchase order and "sorted alphabetically in a suitable sorting device. As the invoices come to hand the Goods Received Reports will be matched up with them." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 150](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer's **Goods Received Book** (Fig. 21, 9½ × 18 in.):
  - Records all goods received: purchases, samples, and parts sent by clients for repair.
  - Has a column for the date of the supplier's invoice, to catch duplicates.
  - The incoming invoice is date-stamped. The contract dept initials "prices checked", the receiving clerk checks quantities, and the accounts dept checks extensions ("a junior clerk").
  - Invoices are then filed in an A–Z expanding file, transferred monthly to a Stolzenberg file and "numbered seriatim". — [Spencer 1907, pp. 26–30, 167–168](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Packages:
  - Spencer: number each incoming package in rotation with "coloured adhesive labels" and keep a Packages Register (octavo). — [Spencer 1907, pp. 30–31](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
  - Elbourne: mark them with the GRN number. The Works Accounts Office issues a Returnable Package Card for each package invoiced, and "no package is to be returned except on the authority of these cards". — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 150–151; Form 5-84, p. 467](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Acknowledgment of Goods Received** (Form 5-83, 6½ × 8 in.): for non-purchase receipts such as customer returns and repairs. "In triplicate (two loose and one fast in book)". Two copies go to the General Office, one of which is posted to the sender. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-83, p. 467; p. 151](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Rejections and returns:**
  - Elbourne: **Credit Claim Notes** ("more often called Debit Notes") are made by the Works Accounts Office "in triplicate", sent to the supplier and posted via the Bought Returns Book. — [Elbourne 1914, p. 152; p. 526](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
  - Spencer: **Stores Returns Note** (Fig. 26), "an octavo carbon-copying duplicate book, preferably lithographed in red ink for the purpose of distinction from the delivery note". — [Spencer 1907, pp. 45–46](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)

**Stock records and issue**
- Spencer's **Stores Ledger** (Fig. 23, 7 × 8½ in., loose-leaf quarto sheets):
  - Gives the reference number and bin number, and minimum and maximum in the top right corner.
  - "A coloured disk… suspended from any bin the contents of which will shortly have to be replenished… and… a differently coloured disk to indicate that the stock has fallen below the desired minimum."
  - Perpetual stock audit by two persons on stock sheets in copyable ink, press-copied, which "supersedes the old-time annual stocktaking". — [Spencer 1907, pp. 37–44](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Elbourne prefers "an ordering level… rather than to fix the maximum and minimum quantities", plus "the normal quantity to be ordered". — [Elbourne 1914, p. 154](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Stock Control Card** (Form 5-89, 8½ × 11 in.):
  - Kept in quantity only at the General Stores.
  - Columns: Location; Unit; Ordering Level; Normal Quantity to be Ordered; Advance Appropriation; Purchase Requisition No.; Purchase Orders; Receipts (G.R. No.); Issues (Allocation Ref.); Transfers to sub-stores; Balance; Stock Scrutiny (Actual Stock, Taken by, Surplus, Deficit).
  - Wholesale-stock cards are stamped "Wholesale" or are "of distinctive colour". — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-89, p. 471](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- The value **Stock Ledger** (General Stock Ledger, Form 5-123; Component Stock Ledger, Form 5-126) is kept in the Works Accounts Office on removable sheets.
  - Receipts come from GRNs; issues are summarised fortnightly on **Stock Issue Abstracts** (Form 5-125); a **General Stock Rate Card** (Form 5-124, 9 × 4 in.) records prices.
  - Stock scrutiny is done by the Works Accounts Office. — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-123 to 5-126, pp. 495–497](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Goods Issue Voucher** (Form 5-86, carbon duplicate; size roughly 3 × 5½ in., OCR unclear):
  - The stores' advice to the Work Depot that goods for a specific Office Order are ready. "The goods are duly issued when the voucher is sent back signed by the department receiving the goods."
  - Vouchers pass "from the Stores to the Works Accounts Office". — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-86, p. 469](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Stores Tally** (Form 5-85, about 2½ × 5 in.): attached to each special consignment. It is marked off as issues are made and sent to the Works Accounts Office when exhausted. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-85, p. 469](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Shop Credit Slip** (Form 5-87, carbon duplicate):
  - For goods loaned or overdrawn and returned, and for scrap and swarf.
  - When goods come back, "a corner is cut off the carbon copy to show the item is cleared". — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-87, p. 469](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Stock Appropriation Ticket** (Form 5-55, about 2½ × 3½ in.): numbered, carbon duplicate. Issued by the Works Office to reserve rough or finished components. "When goods are issued, cancel by crossing with date in crayon." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-55, p. 443](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer: "All withdrawals of material from the storeroom must be effected by a written order from the departmental foremen." A "waster-slip" accompanies spoiled parts to the storekeeper, who records them in a "wasters register". — [Spencer 1907, pp. 46–48](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Workman's Tool Book** (Form 5-92) for a worker's permanent kit; Tool Clearance Ticket (Form 5-21) before discharge; the Summary of Tools Broken and Lost is fortnightly. — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-21, 5-92, 5-93, pp. 413, 473](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

**Bought ledger and payment**
- Elbourne's purchase books:
  - Bought Cash Book; Bought Book (Works Expenditure) and Bought Book (General Expenditure); Bought Returns Books (both); Bought Ledger; Bills Payable Book; Bought Ledger Balances Book.
  - Works-expenditure invoices are entered only "after they have been properly checked and passed by the Works Accounts Office. The Works Accounts Office will number each invoice as passed." — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 516–518](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- "A list of payments proposed to be made should be submitted by the Bought Ledger Clerk… gross amount… discount taken and the net amount payable… checked by the Accountant with… the Creditors Statements." Adjusting entries need a written "Accountant's Instruction" (Form 6-13). — [Elbourne 1914, p. 526](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- The **Combined Cheque and Receipt** form (Form 6-4) is "now used by a large number of trading firms". It carries a 1d. stamp box ("1d. stamp if £2"). — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 516–517](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer: creditors send monthly statements, which the ledger clerk certifies against the creditors' ledger.
  - The cashier lists statement, discount and cheque amounts "for the sanction of the directors' meeting".
  - Cheques are mailed with "a numbered voucher" (three per page with counterfoils) and "an addressed envelope printed and of distinctive colour". — [Spencer 1907, pp. 169, 182–184](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)

### Inferences
- Stores staff never see prices or invoices (Elbourne). This makes a nice simulation rule: the Receiving Clerk's copy of the PO is unpriced, and the invoice route is post → General/Correspondence Office → Works Accounts Office only.
- A cycle works' bought-in items (tubes, stampings, tyres, saddles, chains, balls, fittings) would make the GRN and Stock Control Card the busiest documents in the stores. This is inferred from the nature of cycle assembly.

### Gaps
- The term "bin card" as such does not appear in Elbourne or Spencer. Spencer's bin-numbered loose-leaf stores ledger and coloured disks, and Elbourne's stock control card, fill that role. I found no British specimen titled "Bin Card" before 1914 in these sources.

## 5. Cost accounts (prime cost, oncost, allocation, cost ledger, job cost sheets, reconciliation)

### Takeaway
British practice c. 1905–14 defined cost as follows:
- **Prime cost** is direct wages plus direct materials plus "chargeable expenses".
- **Oncost** (Elbourne calls it "shop charges"; Best calls it "establishment charges") covers everything indirect.
- Works oncost was most commonly charged as a percentage on direct wages (Hawkins: "the method most generally adopted"). Office oncost was charged as a percentage on works cost.
- Advanced writers such as Elbourne argued for hourly shop-charge rates per department or per machine ("producing unit"), and kept the percentage-on-wages method only for estimating.
- Costs were gathered from time tickets and stores vouchers onto cost cards or sheets and into a cost ledger, posted fortnightly in Elbourne's scheme. They were reconciled to the financial books through suspense and adjustment accounts.

### Cited Findings
- "I. — Direct Charges or Prime Cost… (c) Chargeable Expenses. II. — Indirect Charges or Oncost. The total of Prime Cost and Oncost is Gross Cost." — [Hawkins 1905, ch. II](https://archive.org/details/costaccountsexpl00hawk)
- "The simplest, and at the same time sufficiently accurate plan, is to charge Oncost on the amount of Direct Wages, and this is the method most generally adopted." Office oncost is better charged "upon Prime Cost, plus Works Oncost (i.e., on Works Cost)". Worked example: "Works Oncost — 50 per cent. on Wages; Office Oncost — 10 per cent. on Works Cost". — [Hawkins 1905, ch. IX "How to charge Oncost"](https://archive.org/details/costaccountsexpl00hawk)
- Hawkins warns that some manufacturers "use the same rate of Oncost year after year". His example: a works believing it needs 75% on wages actually needs 110% once indirect wages are separated out. — [Hawkins 1905, ch. IX](https://archive.org/details/costaccountsexpl00hawk)
- Best (Sheffield): to prime cost "must be added percentages — variable, perhaps, according to the class of work — to cover general charges and establishment charges". He warns that "a fixed percentage on wages or materials" may be "quite inequitable". — [Best 1911, pp. 28–29](https://archive.org/details/costaccountswith00bestrich)
- Elbourne's index: "Oncost — see Shop charges." His method:
  1. Apportion works expenses to departments fortnightly by formula.
  2. Apportion them to "individual producing units", where "it is convenient to think of the machine as the producing unit".
  3. "Divide the total expenses by the number of hours worked… to arrive at a shop charge rate per hour", either a departmental rate or an individual unit rate. — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 304–307](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- On percentage-on-wages: "such a practice is usually only condoned as being particularly easy… For estimating purposes, a percentage on wages… is more excusable." — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 307–308](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Estimate Detail Sheet** (Form 5-9, 8 × 13 in.): columns for Material Cost, Process Product, Direct Wages (Machine and Hand), Shop Charges and Notes. Shop charges are "applied for estimating purposes as a percentage of Direct Wages… although applied on basis of time worked in the cost accounts." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-9, p. 397](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Estimate Reference Sheet** (Form 5-10, 10 × 8 in.) compares estimate with actual cost under these heads: Drawings, Patterns, Jigs and Special Tools; Material (special purchase / general stock); Component stock; Process products; Direct wages (machine/hand/secondary); Shop charges; Direct overtime allowance; Errors and defects; Disbursements; Final inspection, packing and despatch. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-10, p. 397](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Cost Allocation Sheets Stage I and II** (materials, 10 × 8 in.) and the **Cost Allocation Card Stage III** (wages and shop charges per sub-order, 5 × 8 in.) are posted "fortnightly" to the **Cost Ledger** (Form 5-132, 8 × 13 in.).
  - Wages come from the Weekly Time Allocation Sheets and extra pay from the carbon Extra Pay Slips.
  - Materials come from GRNs and Goods Issue Vouchers; disbursements from Cash Reports to Works.
  - Transfers go through a **Cost Transfer Journal** (Form 5-133). — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-129 to 5-133, pp. 499–501](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- The **Works Expenditure Book** (Forms 5-115 to 5-119) is in five parts on removable sheets, fortnightly: Purchases and Disbursements; Wages; Purchase Credits; Process Product; Stock Product. — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 489–491](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- The **Delivered Orders Cost Abstract** (Form 5-138, 5 × 8 in.) gives, fortnightly, the works cost against invoice price for each order delivered, "to present to the General Manager a concise survey of profitable and unprofitable business". It is "mounted in a guard book for privacy". — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-138, p. 505](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Interlocking with the financial books:**
  - Bought Book works-expenditure totals are posted to "Materials Suspense" and "Works Disbursements Suspense" accounts, and works wages to a "Works Wages Suspense Account", all in a Manufacturing Ledger.
  - These are cleared "by means of Journal entries based on reports received fortnightly from the Works Accounts Office". — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 512–513](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer argues for interlocking through a "stores purchases account", with the costs department's records relied on for distribution. He uses an "E. E. and M. transfer journal" (establishment extensions and maintenance) to move works-made plant from cost records to plant accounts, including a share of establishment expenses. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 166–173](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Standing order numbers carry overheads. Elbourne's "Standing Orders" (works expenses, repairs, additions) appear as such in Spencer's costs chapter, "Standing order numbers". — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 244–258](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich); [Spencer 1907, Contents ch. IX](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Secondary: Boyns, Anderson and Edwards edited an anthology of contemporary British cost-accounting essays, 1887–1952, including oncost apportionment. — [Routledge listing](https://www.routledge.com/British-Cost-Accounting-1887-1952-RLE-Accounting-Contemporary-Essays-from-the-Accounting-Literature/Boyns-Anderson-Edwards/p/book/9781138987944)

### Inferences
- For a 1913 Coventry cycle works the most realistic default is:
  - job or batch cost cards accumulating direct wages from job tickets and materials from stores requisitions;
  - works oncost as a single percentage on direct wages, checked against the trading account periodically;
  - office oncost as a percentage on works cost.
- Elbourne's machine-hour shop charges would mark a "modern" firm.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain Garcke & Fells in a 1905–1914 edition, nor Pearn & Pearn (1904), to cross-check British use of the machine-hour rate in that window.
- I found no Coventry cycle-firm cost records.

## 6. Wages office procedure (paper side)

### Takeaway
The weekly wages cycle runs as follows:
1. **Clock cards** stamped in a card time recorder (Elbourne) or key recorder plus time sheet (Spencer) give attendance hours.
2. **Job slips or tickets** booked at each change of job give job time for costing and premium.
3. The wages week closes midweek (Wednesday per Elbourne's time card; Thursday per Spencer).
4. Extra (premium or piece) pay is computed on Extra Pay Slips with carbon copies.
5. Loose **Wages Sheets** are copied forward from last week and summarised on a **Wages Abstract**.
6. A **coin analysis** goes to the cashier, who draws the cash from the bank by cheque.
7. Two pay clerks make up numbered **pay tins** (or envelopes), each with a **pay slip** on top.
8. Pay is on **Friday** at pay stations against the returned time card, which serves as the "pay card".
9. Unclaimed pay is listed and returned to the General Office.

### Cited Findings
- Time recorders versus brass checks:
  - "The long established method of checking men in and out of the Works by means of a system of metal checks, has given way largely to the more convenient method of mechanical time recorders."
  - The card type is preferred because "the man sees for himself the time he has recorded". Cards are held "in racks beside the time recorders, one set… holding the cards ready for stamping and the other receiving the cards after stamping."
  - "Abnormal times such as late arrival and overtime can be indicated by a different colour stamping." — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 91–93](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Time Card** (Form 5-22, about 7 × 3½ in., "to suit time recorder"):
  - Headed Week No., Check No., "Ending Wednesday"; "Only Clock stamping will be recognised."
  - It has In/Out columns before breakfast, after breakfast, dinner and finish, plus night-shift stampings.
  - "This card is issued again on Fridays as a Pay Card." Deductions are stamped on the back; Extra Pay Slips are stapled on. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-22, p. 415](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer's alternative:
  - A "Bundy Time Recorder" in which "each man records… by means of a small key which bears his shop number", with key-boards either side.
  - A **Time Sheet** (Fig. 51, 20½ × 14 in.) holds 50 names, "written up after each meal-time".
  - A "Rochester Card Recorder" is used for compact departments of up to 100 men. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 99–100, 126–127](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Overtime Ticket** (Form 5-23, 2½ × 3½ in.): authorises overtime and is handed to the Gatekeeper as a gate pass.
- **Workman's Gate-Pass** (Form 5-24): its "colour should be distinguishable readily from Overtime Ticket". — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-23, 5-24, p. 415](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Job Advice Slip** (Form 5-25, 2½ × 3½ in.):
  - Made out "by the men at each change of job, and deposited in suitable boxes for collection at short intervals by the Chargehand… and sends to Ratefixer."
  - "Distinctive colours should be used for Machine Work and Hand Work." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-25, p. 417](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Job Ticket** (Form 5-26, same size as the Time Card):
  - Made out by the Ratefixer.
  - Fields: Description, Dwg No., Sub-Order No., Office Order No., Part No., Pattern Mark, Quantity, Operation, Preparation Time per Batch, Operating Time per Piece, Total Time Limit; Stage Ticket attached; Check No., Wage Rate, Mach. No., Week No., Hrs Worked, Less Adm'ts, Counted; Extra Pay Due; Average Time per Piece. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-26, p. 417](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Daily Time Slip** (Form 5-27, 2½ × 3½ in., pads) is for short jobs. "Colour of form should differ from Job Advice Slip." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-27, p. 419](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer's **Time Slip** (Fig. 71, 5 × 3½ in.):
  - Pads of 50 rubber-stamped with name and shop number "by a boy". Each slip is foreman-signed and dropped in a box at meal times, "cleared by a boy from the costs department".
  - "At stopping time on Thursday — which is usually recognised as week-end in the wages department."
  - Wooden date-calendar racks are hung in the shops. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 127–128](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Weekly Time Allocation Sheet** (Form 5-28, 7 × 10½ in.): one per man, compiled in the Wages Office from Job Advice Slips and Time Cards. "Distinctive colours… for Machine Work and Hand Work. Secondary labour… on white sheets." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-28, p. 419](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Extra Pay Slip** (Form 5-29, 2½ × 3 in.): "arranged in perforated strip form and for a carbon copy to be taken". The carbon (the "Extra Pay Book") feeds the Wages Sheets and then the Works Accounts Office. "Excess Time Slips" are used where the limit is exceeded. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-29, p. 421; pp. 104–105](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Extra pay is computed the week after the job is completed. "The staff being able to concentrate on the extra pay work for the first half of the week and on the ordinary wages the latter part of the week." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 104](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Wages Sheet** (Form 5-31, 14½ × 10½ in., "used on one side only, and held in binder"):
  - Columns include Check No., Name, Rate, Hours, Time Wages, Extra Pay, Gross Wages, Insurance (worker: Health, Unemp.), Net Wages, Insurance (employer), Stamps.
  - The number of lines on each sheet equals the number of Pay Tin Slips per sheet.
  - Names and rates are copied forward early in the week, and new men are entered at the tail for the first week.
  - Leavers are paid on a separate sheet. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-31, p. 423; p. 105](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- At the end of each pay section a rubber-stamp endorsement is applied: "Wages Sheets Made up / Checked / Insurance Cards to date / Wages Paid out by / Stamped / Verified". — [Elbourne 1914, p. 106](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- The **Wages Abstract** (Form 5-32) goes to the Works Manager and General Manager. "Deductions (in red)". It must agree with the summary of Wages Sheets. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-32, p. 423](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Cash and coin.**
  - Spencer: "the wages clerk will prepare a list of sovereigns, half-sovereigns, silver and copper he will require, and pass the figures to the cashier, who will prepare his cheque on the bank accordingly." — [Spencer 1907, p. 109](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
  - Elbourne: "The coin analysis may be carried to the extent of sovereigns, half-sovereigns, half-crowns, threepenny pieces, copper and sundry silver." Silver is drawn from the bank in guaranteed bags so that a "silver sorter" can be used; the bag value is illegible in the OCR, and unguaranteed "£5 bags" are the alternative. Copper comes in "five shilling bags".
  - Elbourne: wages are computed to the halfpenny because of National Insurance. — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 106–108](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Pay Tin Slip** (Form 5-33): "arranged in sheets… The net wages… are totalled and compared with Wages Sheets. When in order, the slips are separated and the pay tins made up from them and the slips put in the tins." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-33, p. 423](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Making up the pay.**
  - "Pay Clerk No. 1 will select the coin and slide the amount to Pay Clerk No. 2, who will check and put in a pay tin with the pay slip on top… and puts them in trays for locking up until pay time, when the pay boxes will be carried to the pay stations."
  - At the pay station, empty tins go into "a suitable basket — one with a canvas funnel top".
  - Pay clerks may get "sixpence for each pay correctly made up". — [Elbourne 1914, p. 107](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
  - "If a pay envelope is used instead of a tin, then the envelope will constitute the pay ticket." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 106](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **At the pay station:** "two clerks in attendance at each pay station — one the pay clerk handing out the money and the other receiving the pay cards and cancelling them by a crayon mark across the corner."
  - "Pay cards can be placed with the current time cards in the recorder racks during the meal-time immediately preceding pay time."
  - "It is better generally to pay on Friday night rather than Saturday morning." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 108](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer: "Wages Slips" (Fig. 58, 2 × 2 in.) are printed in copyable ink "in perforated sheets of fifty slips each, copied in a press-copying book". They are then "placed in small numbered envelopes or cash tins along with the several wages, arranged in numerical order on numbered wooden trays… by two men". — [Spencer 1907, pp. 112–113](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- Physical pay tin: a museum example from a Pontarddulais works is a tin box 52 mm in diameter and 50 mm high, with a hinged lid, a triangular suspension attachment and a number stamped on the lid. — [Museum Wales, pay box 2002.183](https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/5947a7e4-baa4-3ff8-b570-195186066be7?page=25)
- **Unclaimed Pay Report** (Form 5-34, 5 × 8 in., "with carbon duplicate (unprinted)"): "To be filled in by Pay Clerk at end of pay and handed to Cashier with money in pay tins." Afterwards, a "Pay Ticket [is] required from Wages Office to claim pay". — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-34, p. 423; p. 108](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Spencer keeps an "Unclaimed Wages" book signed by the recipient. "Payments by proxy… only… on a written request." — [Spencer 1907, p. 113](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Special Pay Ticket** (Form 5-35, 5 × 8 in., carbon duplicate): "serves for the Wages Office to control works payments without handling any cash". It covers leavers' extra pay, away time, advances and unclaimed wages. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-35, p. 425](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Discharge Note** (Form 5-20): the foreman fills in the upper portion. The Wages Office detaches the upper portion, and "only the lower portion is seen by the workman when being paid". A Tool Clearance Ticket must be attached. The Cashier pays against the "payment due" voucher plus the time card. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-20, p. 413](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Also in the wages-office set: Engagement Form (Form 5-16), Character Report (Form 5-17), Wages Advice Slip (Form 5-18), Workman's Rate Sheet (Form 5-19), Away Expenses and Away Time sheets (Forms 5-36, 5-37) and Accident Report (Form 5-38). — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 407–427](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- In the financial books, wages flow through a **Wages and Petty Cash Book** and a **Wages and Petty Ledger** kept by the petty cashier. The Accountant verifies the cash and vouchers "at least twice a week". — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 514–515](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)

### Inferences
- The weekly rhythm suggested for the simulation:
  - Thursday–Wednesday wages week.
  - Monday–Wednesday: extra-pay calculations.
  - Thursday: wages sheets.
  - Friday morning: cheque and coin from the bank; making up tins.
  - Friday after dinner: pay cards in racks.
  - Friday evening: pay at pay stations.
  - Unclaimed tins back to the cashier.
- In the simulation the bank run can be an office boy or the cashier with a messenger. That last point is my inference; the sources only say the cashier draws a cheque.

### Gaps
- National Insurance stamping details are deliberately not pursued (another researcher covers this).
- No Coventry-specific evidence of brass pay checks versus card recorders in 1913.

## 7. Correspondence, filing, telegrams, memos and physical movement of paper

### Takeaway
Morning post handling:
- The post was collected (in Spencer's case in a locked bag), opened by the Secretary or Manager with the correspondence clerk present, and remittances were entered at once.
- Letters were date-stamped and numbered with a combined stamp, registered, and distributed in baskets to departments. Elbourne prefers typed copies to go out, with originals kept in the Correspondence Office.

Replies:
- Typed with carbon copies, filed with the incoming letter in numbered or alphabetical folders in vertical cabinets indexed by cards.
- The signed original was usually also press-copied in a letter book, because a press copy of the signed letter was the legal evidence.
- Telegrams were confirmed by post.

Internal paper moved by printed memorandum forms in triplicate and by a messenger-boy "works post" doing regular rounds, envelope for envelope. Trays and baskets held work in progress.

### Cited Findings
- **Post collection.** Spencer's firm arranges "for the delivery of all letters to a special messenger from the firm… a leather bag, fitted with a lock having duplicate keys, one of which is retained by the postmaster." — [Spencer 1907, p. 1](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Opening.**
  - Burton: all letters "should be opened by one officer, and one officer only", ideally the Secretary, "at an early hour". "In a well-ordered office all letters received before noon are answered, or at least acknowledged, on the date of receipt." — [Burton 1899, pp. 88–90](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
  - Elbourne: the morning mail is dealt with in "the Board Room, or wherever". Remittances go into a Cash Inwards Book at once. "Every letter received ought to be replied to the same day as received, or, failing a reply, a formal acknowledgment should be sent." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 44](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Inwards Correspondence Endorsement Stamp** (Form 5-4): "dates and numbers consecutively at one operation". The stamp boxes are Rec'd / Time / Dept. to Reply / Copies to / No. / Reply Dated / File No. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-4, p. 393](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Inwards Correspondence Register** (Form 5-5, sheets about 8½ × 11 in., typed):
  - Columns: Ref. No.; Time rec'd; Sender; Subject Key Words; Copies sent to; Dept. to reply; Summary of contents if original leaves Corr. Off.; Ack. (p/a preliminary, f/a final); Reply letter sent; File No.
  - "Telegrams: entered in red or underlined in red."
  - "The corner is to be cut off when all the letters registered thereon have been dealt with." — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-5, p. 393](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Elbourne's preferred routine is "to retain the original letter in the Correspondence Office and type a copy for issue to a Department… extra carbon copies [to] other interested parties". The departmental copy of the reply goes with it. "The official copy should be on distinctive colour paper from that of the departmental copy." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 45](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Press copies:**
  - "Press copies of outward letters showing the signatures must, of course, be taken, and the modern copying machines allow for loose sheet copies to be made very rapidly."
  - "Not infrequently the letter book system is retained in conjunction with the filing of carbon copies of the replies with the original letters."
  - The objection to the letter book is "the serious delay in getting out any volume of correspondence". — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 45–46](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Dicksee: "every letter which is typed has a carbon copy made simultaneously… annexed to the letter to which it is a reply and filed with it… this does not obviate the necessity of press-copying the original letter in the office letter book as, in the event of production for legal purposes, it would be the actual press copy of the signed letter which should be put in evidence." — [Dicksee 1906, p. 51](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- Dicksee's morning routine: "In going through the morning's mail, the Manager should dictate replies to as many letters as possible right away." Then "the Correspondence Clerk… putting those referred to each department in a special basket which he sends without delay to the sub-chief". — [Dicksee 1906, pp. 50–51](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- Burton, for works of 500–600 hands, has the correspondent "visit the various departments with his note book, and take down the dictated answers". Where letters are written departmentally, "two copies may be taken of each letter — one in the letter book, the other to be sent to the department affected, there to be pasted in a skeleton letter guard book." — [Burton 1899, p. 91](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
- **Spencer's routine:**
  - A **Postal Register** (Fig. 1, foolscap) is written up by the chief correspondence clerk while the secretary peruses the mail; a self-dating stamp is applied.
  - A **Correspondence Index** card (Fig. 3, 5 × 3 in.) is numbered and filed vertically, alphabetically.
  - Each firm has a numbered manila letter file, "filed on end… in a cabinet of drawers with an inside capacity of about 30 inches deep", with decimal sub-files (e.g. 1·1 for accounts).
  - "A carbon copy of the reply is attached to the letter received… superiority over the old system of press-copy letter books… is too patent for words." — [Spencer 1907, pp. 2–6](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Spencer's despatch routine:**
  - A gummed **Enclosure Slip** (Fig. 5).
  - Sorting, then a **Postage Book** (Fig. 6, foolscap) balanced daily.
  - Two clerks on mailing duty: "the elder clerk… checks the enclosures and calls out the necessary particulars for the other to write up the 'postage book'."
  - A chart of postal arrangements hangs in the office.
  - "Wicker-work trays" hold papers awaiting filing ("within twenty-four hours at the latest").
  - A **Requisition for File** slip (Fig. 8) is left as a receipt when a file is borrowed. — [Spencer 1907, pp. 6–12](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Telephone and callers:**
  - Spencer: a Telephone Messages Received slip (Fig. 9), a Telephone Messages book (Fig. 10, foolscap), confirmation by post, and a "Caller's Slip" (Fig. 11) that "eventually finds its way to the general manager". — [Spencer 1907, pp. 12–14](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
  - Elbourne prefers callers' cards to callers' slips, routed by the Door Attendant via the Correspondence Clerk. — [Elbourne 1914, p. 46](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Telegrams:**
  - "All telegrams sent away should be confirmed by the following post. The triplicate telegram books, which can be obtained from any commercial stationer, are useful." The confirmation memo is "copied in the letter book". — [Burton 1899, p. 92](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
  - Dicksee enters telegrams "in red ink" in the Letter Record Book. — [Dicksee 1906, p. 50](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
- **Filing and indexing:**
  - Elbourne recommends number symbols plus an alphabetical card index (Form 5-6, 4 × 6 in., addressable by addressing machine). He quotes a scheme with "240 subdivisions of the alphabet" and gives a table of English surname initials per 1,000 (B 105, S 96, H 83, C 82, W 78, M 74…). — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 46–48; Form 5-6, p. 393](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
  - Dicksee: vertical cabinets with "follower" blocks and guide cards. Transfers are made "keeping all the correspondence for, say, a year in one set of transfer cases" or piecemeal. — [Dicksee 1906, pp. 57–58](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)
  - Burton (1899) names the Shannon, Amberg and Ceres files and describes the L&NWR (Euston) Lapper register system for "1,000 letters a day". — [Burton 1899, pp. 89, 95](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)
- **Inter-departmental memo and works post:**
  - "Internal Correspondence within the Works should be for the most part carried out by specified forms appropriate to each routine." The recipient of a memo may "initial for it on the fast copy in the memorandum book, brought by the messenger."
  - "The transit of routine forms and memorandums between departments… should be dealt with by a works post operating from a suitable centre… the Correspondence Office as to office departments, with a separate service for the shops run from… the Works Office."
  - "Messenger boys make regular journeys round the departments leaving one post envelope and collecting the waiting one." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 48](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- Elbourne's "Departmental Memorandum" (Form 5-106, triplicate; second copy to the Works Office via the works post) is quoted in section 3. — [Elbourne 1914, Form 5-106, p. 483](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Form control:**
  - All forms should be designed "through one channel", with "a plain reference No. and title to each form in the top left-hand corner".
  - Edition letters are added after the number.
  - Routine instructions for each form are kept in a "guard book… handed in on resignation", "supplemented by a routine diagram, showing the path of the different forms as to the executive copy and the memorandum copies." — [Elbourne 1914, pp. 42–43](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- **Colour conventions:**
  - "Where carbon duplicates are made, the carbon copies should be distinguished from each other and the original… by distinctive colours of papers or inks… the executive (preferably the top) copy is always on white paper… The carbon copies are of a memorandum character." — [Elbourne 1914, p. 42](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
  - Red ink was used for exceptions throughout: telegrams in registers; late arrivals in the staff attendance book; deductions on the wages abstract; credit balances on sales ledger balances; credit notes and stores-returns notes printed in red. — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-2, 5-5, 5-32; p. 533](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich); [Spencer 1907, pp. 46, 176](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)
- **Cut corners as status markers:** the Office Order corner is cut when fully invoiced; the Register sheet corner when all letters are dealt with; the Work Tally coupon corner when material is drawn; the Shop Credit Slip carbon corner when goods are returned. — [Elbourne 1914, Forms 5-5, 5-12, 5-87, 5-100](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)
- US flag. JoAnne Yates' *Control through Communication* (1989) traces the shift in American firms "from pigeon hole desks to filing systems; from handwritten copies to typewriters to duplication technologies; and from letters to memos". It is American evidence only. — [Wikipedia: JoAnne Yates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JoAnne_Yates)

### Inferences
- A good physical model has three parts:
  - A central Correspondence/General Office "post room": an in-tray, a sorting rack with departmental pigeon holes, a stamp press, a letter-copying press or rotary copier, and filing cabinets.
  - Two messenger loops. One is an office loop from the Correspondence Office. The other is a works loop from the Works Office through the shops, stores, warehouse, gatehouse and wages office, run on a fixed timetable.
  - Pick-up boxes for job slips in the shops, emptied by boys.
- Wicker trays and baskets are the period-correct "queues".

### Gaps
- No source here gives an exact number or frequency of works-post rounds per day.
- No source confirms pneumatic tubes in British factory offices c. 1913.
- Retention periods for correspondence and accounting documents are not stated in any of these texts. Dicksee mentions annual transfer cases; Spencer says order acknowledgements "may be destroyed after an order has been filled".

## 8. Specimen forms: consolidated document catalogue (titles, fields, copies, colours, sizes, routing)

### Takeaway
Elbourne's 145 forms (1914) are the richest British specimen set of the period. Together with Spencer's 92 figures (1907) they allow nearly every document to be specified as an object.

Recurring physical conventions:
- Standard card sizes of 3 × 5, 4 × 6 and 5 × 8 in.
- Small shop slips at 2½ × 3½ in.
- Office forms at 6½ × 8 or 10 × 8 in.
- Carbon duplicates or triplicates, with a white "executive" top copy and coloured memorandum copies.
- Red printing for credit and return documents.
- Corners cut off to show completion.

### Inferences
- For simulation data modelling, every document object needs at least these fields:
  - `form_no`
  - `title`
  - `originator_role`
  - `size_in`
  - `copies[]`, each with `colour`, `destination`, `retained_in_book: bool` and `priced: bool`
  - `fields[]`
  - `book_entered_in`
  - `status_markers`, such as corner cut, crayon cross, "V.U." stamp or red entry
  - `next_documents[]`
- Elbourne's own notes supply most of these values directly.
- Elbourne recommends colours but rarely names them, except: the top copy white; red for exceptions; red printing on the "OUT" side of pattern cards; tints for guide cards. So specific copy colours (e.g. "pink second copy, yellow third") would be simulation design choices, not documented fact.

### Gaps
- Specific paper colours per copy are almost never named in the British texts. They say "distinctive colours" without saying which.
- Retention periods per document are not given in any of these sources.
- I had no specimen form images. The OCR loses ruling and layout, so the column lists above are reconstructed from OCR'd headings and may be incomplete.
