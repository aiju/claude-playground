# Rebuilding a 1913 Coventry Cycle Works

A mid-sized Coventry cycle works in 1913 was a firm of about **400 hands**, one of only **49 cycle makers** left in the city. It worked from a three- or four-storey red-brick block with offices, showroom and stores on the street front and a yard of boiler house, smithy and despatch dock behind. It made roughly **10,000–12,000 bicycles a year in batches of about 100**. It built and brazed its own frames, turned its own hubs, cups and cones, and did all its own polishing, nickel plating and stove enamelling. It bought tube, stampings, tyres, chains, saddles and three-speed hubs from a dense Midlands network of specialists. It sold a catalogue of three or four grades, from about **£5 10s to 14 guineas**, to local agents at trade discounts after the November show. Crated machines went by horse dray to the LNWR goods yard at Warwick Road for overnight delivery. Behind the machinery the firm ran on paper:
- an office order typed in six carbon copies;
- a purchase order whose receiving copy carried no prices;
- a work tally riding on every box of parts;
- a time card that came back on Friday as a pay card;
- a numbered tin of coin for every worker.

Labour worked a **53–54-hour week** at **6d an hour (labourers) to 10¼d (patternmakers)**. Its main legal frame was the Factory and Workshop Act 1901, the Workmen's Compensation Act 1906, and National Insurance, whose stamps began in July 1912. The company was most plausibly a **private limited company** under the Companies Act 1908. It banked with Lloyds or the London City & Midland, paid income tax at **1s 2d in the pound**, and paid ordinary dividends of 5–10% in a fair year.

The evidence is richest on processes, office forms and law. It comes mainly from four sources: W. F. Grew's 1921 tour of a cycle factory, G. R. Carter's 1912 chapter on the trade, built on interviews with Coventry managers, E. T. Elbourne's 1914 set of 145 works forms (Elbourne had managed at BSA), and the statutes themselves. Evidence is thinnest for 1913-dated floor plans, building colours, hooters, daily timetables and trade terms. For those, this report gives labelled estimates rather than invented facts.

## Mid-sized meant about 400 hands among Coventry's 49 makers

Coventry's cycle trade in 1913 was a small core of large, permanent firms surrounded by short-lived small ones. The city had **75 cycle makers in 1897 and 49 in 1913** ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). The Victoria County History counts about 50 producers in each of 1896, 1905 and 1912, but only about 30% of the 1896 firms still existed in 1912. **About 5,800 Coventry workers were in the cycle trade in 1911**, and the city made **310,000 cycles in 1906** ([VCH Warwickshire vol. 8](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)). Rudge-Whitworth sat at the top. It made about 75,000 machines in 1906 with 1,800 workpeople (VCH), or 2,700 (Lloyd-Jones & Lewis); the two sources conflict. Its frame numbers ran from 663,066 in 1912 to 697,524 in 1913 and 726,731 in 1914 ([Online Bicycle Museum](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/frame-number-bicycle-dating-guide/)), which implies 30,000–35,000 machines a year by 1913. In 1891 the VCH found "a hundred cycle workshops … employing any number of workers from a dozen to 500" ([VCH](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189)).

The ratios separate two kinds of firm. Integrated makers that produced most of their own parts turned out **about 30–45 machines per worker a year**. Assembling branches that bought in components reached 60–90. Grew draws the same line. The "small maker" buys frames, wheels, handlebars and pillars and does little but polish, enamel, plate and assemble. The "medium-sized factory owner … makes most of the parts himself but purchases a number of finished or partly finished components" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)).

Grew also notes that "1,000 machines per week can be made with the same staff, or approximately the same staff, as is required for 500". That penalises a mid-sized firm, which should therefore sit at the low end of the integrated range. This report's reference firm follows from that (my estimate): **about 400 works hands plus 25–30 salaried staff, making 10,000–12,000 machines a year**. That is about 250–300 a week at the spring peak and half that in the autumn slack. An assembler buying most components could reach the same output with 150–250 hands.

The staff ratio follows Coventry Chain Co. in 1914, which had 1,400 works hands and 100 staff ([1914 Who's Who in Business: C](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_C)). Two real firms are good templates. Calcott Bros sold 5–11 guinea roadsters, carriers and juveniles. Timerick of 7 Little Park Street built "for the trade, supplying machines with the agent's transfer" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).

## A red-brick block stacked heavy work low and brazing high

### The 1897 Humber plan remains the best floor-by-floor template

Coventry cycle works came in three building types:
- **Converted textile premises.** Examples are the ribbon "cottage factories" and the four-storey cotton mill that Daimler turned into its Motor Mills.
- **Purpose-built multi-storey red-brick works.** Rudge-Whitworth's 1906 factory at Spon Street and Crow Lane was a **six-storey steel-framed block 400 ft long, 60 ft wide and 89 ft high**. It cost over £40,000, of which £26,230 was the builder's contract and £9,000 the steelwork. The architects were Harrison and Hattrell of Coventry ([Manchester Victorian Architects](https://manchestervictorianarchitects.org.uk/buildings/cycle-factory-spon-street-and-crow-lane-coventry)).
- **Single-storey north-light sheds.** These stood behind office blocks, as at Challenge on Foleshill Road (1906–7), "an impressive symmetrical red-brick office building with sheds behind" ([Wikipedia: Challenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Challenge_(cycle_and_car))). The same type was built on a vast scale at Humber's Folly Lane works (1907–8).

No floor-by-floor description of a Coventry cycle works dated 1905–1914 survives online. The best template is therefore the Institution of Mechanical Engineers' 1897 visit to Humber's rebuilt Lower Ford Street works. It is sixteen years early, but it describes the kind of building still in use in 1913. That works had **four storeys and 80,000 sq ft** for "1,000 machines a week" and 1,300 men, with "5,000 feet of benching and 800 fixed vices". Its offices and stores fronted Lower Ford Street and Hood Street "with a spacious entrance at the corner", fitted with "telephonic communication, electric lighting, and steam radiators" ([IMechE 1897](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1897_Institution_of_Mechanical_Engineers:_Visits_to_Works)). The departments were laid out by floor:
- **Ground floor:** the machine shop of about 20,000 sq ft with **250 machine tools**, including a 100-ft bay "almost filled with capstan lathes". A 3,000 sq ft polishing shop held "100 double spindles, at which 200 men are engaged". A 1,500 sq ft plating shop held "nine large plating vats".
- **First floor:** the enamelling shop of 3,000 sq ft. It held **25 glazed-brick stoves, "in each of which 100 cycle frames can be put at one time"**, a partitioned room for "special colouring and lining", and a Blackman ventilating fan. Beyond it came wheel-making, erecting, fork-building, handlebar and brake fitting, and finishing. The chief engineer's office and drawing office sat "in the middle of this floor".
- **Second floor:** frame-building, where "400 men are employed on frame-building alone", with "large brazing shops, which are separate from the main shop". Frame-trueing and sand-blasting were over the enamelling shop.
- **Third floor:** carpenters, pattern-makers, and a large stock room.
- **Separate building:** a one-storey smithy of 2,000 sq ft in a courtyard. It had a glass roof, four chimneys, ten fires blown by a Root's blower, and a steam hammer.

Two other works of 1897 show how the street frontage worked. Premier had frontages of 300 ft on Read Street and 200 ft on South Street. Its main entrance was "flanked by office suites" and led to "a lofty stock room, 80 feet square, capable of storing upwards of 6,000 cycles", then showrooms, with the tyre department in the basement. Premier's building shop measured 180 × 80 ft under roof lights. Triumph's Priory Street buildings covered "more than 6,000 square yards". Its front offices and managing director's rooms were electrically lit, and it had fire hydrants throughout and a fire brigade drawn from its own workmen ([IMechE 1897](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1897_Institution_of_Mechanical_Engineers:_Visits_to_Works)). Singer's Canterbury Street factory of 1891 had a 90 m frontage on a 5-acre site ([Coventry Society](https://www.coventrysociety.org.uk/2025/05/30/george-singer/)).

The fabric was consistently **red brick**. A listed former ribbon factory in New Buildings has an office in Flemish bond, an engineering-brick plinth and sills, "large five-light segmental-headed casements, mostly with glazing bars", and a hipped slate roof ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101265695-former-ribbon-factory-and-office-st-michaels-ward)). Humber's Folly Lane sheds had "Northern light and lantern roofs; flat roofs covered with vulcanite and two inches of gravel". Their works roads were 30 ft and 20 ft wide with dust-proof finishes, and their floors were cemented concrete, with wood and tiles in specialised areas ([Manchester Victorian Architects](https://manchestervictorianarchitects.org.uk/buildings/humber-motor-and-cycle-works-folly-lane-coventry)). Rudge's 89 ft over six storeys gives **about 14–15 ft floor to floor**. Cash's 12-metre, three-storey cottage factory gives about 13 ft ([DASH](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/dash/article/view/5094)).

For the model, I recommend the following, derived from those dimensions:
- **Main block:** about 150–250 × 40–60 ft, three or four storeys, 13–15 ft floor to floor, with a taller ground floor. Long, narrow blocks like Rudge's and Daimler's 172 × 35 ft shop could be lit from both sides.
- **Street range:** a two- or three-storey office, showroom and stores range with a corner entrance.
- **Floors:** the Humber allocation above, with the brazing shop partitioned off on the frame-building floor under fume hoods.
- **Behind the block:** one or two single-storey north-light sheds.
- **Yard:** a detached one-storey smithy, a boiler house and engine house against the main block, one tall chimney, coke and coal heaps, and a raised despatch dock.

### Steam, gas and corporation current drove belted line shafts

By 1897 Coventry works already mixed steam, gas and electricity:
- **Humber:** ran its main shafting from "a high-pressure condensing engine of 50 horse-power, provided with steam by two Galloway boilers". The polishing shop had its own 25 hp engine and the extension "a 35 horse-power Crossley gas engine". Plating current came from "two dynamos, each having an output of 300 amperes", and the sand-blast blower was "driven from the main shafting".
- **Alfred Herbert's machine-tool works:** drove "each line of shafting … by an electric motor". It lit its shop with "incandescent gas burners arranged in groups, and hung on spiral coils of tube" to protect the mantles from vibration, and warmed it with exhaust steam ([IMechE 1897](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1897_Institution_of_Mechanical_Engineers:_Visits_to_Works)).
- **Rudge-Whitworth by 1907:** had found that "electrical driving from 23 motors yielded a bigger and better output over that yielded by the gas engines formerly used", with cooler, cleaner shops. Other cycle firms soon copied it ([Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)).

Carter still described the usual motive power in 1912 as "usually gas or oil", tended by "a staff of mechanics and enginemen" ([Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)). Coventry Corporation's Sandy Lane station had supplied electricity since November 1895, with 350 kW of capacity by 1898 ([Wikipedia: Coventry power stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coventry_power_stations)). Even so, Daimler built its own powerhouse (plans 1907, extended 1911) because supply outages "interrupted production and could damage the machining of components" ([Coventry Society](https://coventrysociety.org.uk/2019/05/21/the-daimler-powerhouse-one-of-coventrys-hidden-industrial-gems)). Belts outlasted the war: Rover's machine tools were still driven by "over six miles of belting" in 1925 ([Motor Sport](https://www.motorsportmagazine.com/archive/article/december-1972/48/factory-methods-of-the-vintage-era-2/)).

The defensible 1913 power plant is one or two Galloway- or Lancashire-type boilers and a 50–150 hp engine driving overhead line shafts and flat belts. A Crossley gas engine would serve an outlying shop, and group-drive electric motors could be shown as the firm's modernisation. Gas also fed the hot processes. Brazing hearths used "an open fire of 'breeze' (small coke) … kept at a high temperature by allowing a pressure coal gas flame to impinge on the glowing coke", under hoods "to carry off the fumes". Enamelled parts hung "in gas heated stoves" ([Grew 1921, ch. 5](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Cycle_Industry/Chapter_5)).

### The Factory Act left visible marks on the building

The Factory and Workshop Act 1901 required:
- unpainted interior walls and ceilings to be **limewashed at least every fourteen months**;
- at least **250 cubic feet of space per person**, or 400 during overtime;
- separate sanitary conveniences for each sex;
- **secure fencing of every flywheel, hoist and part of the mill gearing**;
- a safety valve, steam gauge and water gauge on every boiler, with a thorough examination at least every fourteen months;
- for factories built after 1 January 1892 employing over forty people, a district council **certificate of means of escape in case of fire**;
- in works built after 1896, doors opening outwards from any room holding more than ten people ([FWA 1901](https://archive.org/details/b22416365)).

A 1903 order set **one convenience per 25 males and the same for females** ([St Marylebone MOH 1903](https://wellcomelibrary.org/moh/report/b18254172/36)). In a model these rules become visible as follows: whitewashed shop walls, railed belts and flywheels, external iron fire-escape stairs on the upper floors, outward-opening doors, separate men's and women's lavatory blocks, and a board of statutory notices at the entrance (section 6).

### Terraces, trams and the canal corridor framed the site

Cycle works clustered in three areas:
- **The old city edge:** Spon Street and Crow Lane (Rudge), Priory Street (Triumph), Cheylesmore (Swift), and Far Gosford Street and Lower Ford Street.
- **The ribbon-weaving suburb of Hillfields:** Singer and Premier.
- **Increasingly, the canal and railway corridor to the north:** Daimler on Sandy Lane and Challenge on Foleshill Road.

Hillfields terraces of 1888–1905 were "small, densely arranged, long terraces situated on straight roads" ([Coventry HLC Hillfields](https://www.coventry.gov.uk/downloads/file/17442/hlc-area-31-hillfields-character-area)). Spon End's ran "up to 24 houses long", built to the street corners behind "low brick walls", with "Pavements … narrow with no grass verges or trees" ([Coventry HLC Spon End](https://www.coventry.gov.uk/downloads/file/17443/hlc-area-30-spon-end-character-area)).

A works that needs a private siding belongs on the northern corridor. There, Daimler's cotton-mill branch "was in use and worked by the LNWR locomotives until the First World War" ([Warwickshire Railways](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/daimlerhalt.htm)). The street needs a tram. Coventry Corporation bought the 3 ft 6 in gauge company tramway on 1 January 1912 for £220,638 9s 0d, including 41 cars, and its cars ran in **chocolate and cream with cream rocker panels**, lettered "Coventry Corporation Tramways" in gilt ([Wikipedia: Coventry Corporation Tramways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coventry_Corporation_Tramways)). The fleet was open-top double-deckers, and the first top-covered cars arrived in 1913 ([Local Transport History](https://localtransporthistory.co.uk/fleetlists/coventry2/)).

No source documents a Coventry works hooter, works clock, painted wall sign or door and window colour. Those are free design choices. One exception: the firm's painted name is not a guess. The Companies Act 1908 required every limited company to keep its name painted "on the outside of every office or place in which its business is carried on, in a conspicuous position" ([CA 1908 s.63](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/63/enacted)).

## Batches of 100 moved from rough stores to the railway dray in about two weeks

### Thirteen stations from tube to crate

Grew's tour of "a large cycle factory", "commencing at the stores and finishing at the loading bay of the packing department", is the backbone source for the process sequence. Carter's 1912 Coventry list of shops gives the same order. Work started with a requisition to "put through, we will say, 100 machines of a certain model". The rough stores then issued "100 sets of frame lugs, 100 sets of tubes cut to length and mitred, enough tube to make up 100 seat pillars, fork-sides, and crowns for 100 forks, 200 rims, the necessary spokes". Finished frames, wheels and forks returned "to the finished stores; here they are viewed, and re-issued to the finishing department with the necessary tyres, saddles, handles, brakework". Each return to stores credited the operatives with their piecework ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)). "Messengers, usually youths, transport material from one shop to another" ([Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)).

Some stations need more detail than the table gives.

**Lugs.** Lugs came in three quality grades: "malleable castings for cheap, low quality machines, forged stampings for those of a somewhat superior make", and pressed sheet for the best ([Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog)). Raleigh and Rudge-Whitworth used pressed-steel lugs ([Grew](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)).

**Plating line.** The 1897 sequence ran: hot potash bath, rinse, cyanide or hydrochloric dip, pumice scour, rinse, then the nickel vat. Parts left it "dull plated" and went back to the calico mops ([Wallis-Tayler](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog); [Grew](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)).

**Enamelling.** A first coat stoved "for one hour at 380° F., leaving the ventilator open for the first five minutes". Later coats took "one and a quarter to one and a half hours". Each coat was rubbed down with pumice and water, and "a cycle frame should be hung head downwards with the first coat" ([Cassell 1916](https://tekniskkulturarv.dk/book/dec6aa56-2a37-4558-8ab8-f70b760598dd?page=86)). A stove of about 1900 measured 6 × 6 × 3½ ft. It reached 400 °F, had iron hanging rods 3–4 in below the roof and a thermometer read through a mica window. "A single cycle may be completed with as little as one gallon of enamel" ([Hasluck](https://archive.org/details/cyclebuildingan00unkngoog)). Carter's Coventry enamellers finished frames, forks and mudguards in "black, green, red, etc., as required" ([Carter](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)).

### Stoves and hearths set the factory's pace

The batch of 100 is the right simulation unit. It matched the stores' issue unit, and it matched exactly Humber's stove capacity of 100 frames per stove. No source gives a measured throughput time, but the documented process times allow an estimate (mine): **about one to two weeks from issue of tubes and lugs to packed machines**. That breaks down as follows:
- building, pegging and brazing: 1–2 days;
- pickling and filing: 1–2 days;
- iron polishing: about a day;
- viewing in stores: under a day;
- two or three enamel coats with rubbing down: 1–2 days;
- lining and transfers: a day;
- wheels and plating, in parallel: 2–4 days;
- finishing, viewing and wrapping: 1–2 days.

Turned parts came out of stock made weeks or months earlier. At the 1896 boom peak, makers quoted six weeks' delivery ([Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)).

Hearth and truer counts can be calibrated from the sources. Rudge's 50 open hearths of 1911 served a firm whose cycle sales Lloyd-Jones and Lewis tabulate at 41,949 that year, a figure that is probably units but may be pounds ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). Scaled to 11,000 machines, that suggests about 13 hearths. American dip-brazing claims of "one man and a boy … 100 complete frames in ten hours" imply far fewer ([Cycle Age 1899](https://archive.org/details/cycleagetra2118981899nati)). **Eight to ten hearths** is a defensible middle for the reference firm.

Carter quotes Coventry wheel truers averaging 37s a week, and a piece rate of "10½d. per pair of wheels finished" ([Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)). If that rate is the truers', it means about 42 pairs per truer per week, so 250 machines a week need about six truers. Filing was the hidden labour sink. An American 1899 cost sheet put filing at $28.00 per 100 frames, against $10.06 for all dipping and grinding ([Cycle Age 1899](https://archive.org/details/cycleagetra2118981899nati)).

### Coventry made frames and finish, and bought tyres, chains and saddles

Carter is explicit that in 1912 Coventry "Cycle Manufacturers" did "not make their own supplies of tyres, wheel rims, chains, accessories — bells, lamps, etc.". Instead they "purchase their supplies of steel tubes, bars, wire, accessories, etc., and thence produce the finished cycle" ([Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)). Grew's large factory bought only "saddles, handles, tyres, and toolbags". Even it bought small screws and nuts, mudguards "rolled by specialists", and spokes "supplied headed, bent, and screwed" ([Grew](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)). On spokes the two sources conflict: Carter has "semi-skilled machine minders" making them in the wheel shop.

The make-or-buy split for the reference firm follows Carter, as the better fit for a Coventry firm.

**Made in-house:**
- frames and forks;
- lug machining;
- hubs, bottom brackets, cups, cones, axles, cranks and chainwheels;
- seat pillars and brakework;
- all polishing, plating, enamelling and lining;
- wheel building and assembly.

**Bought in:**
- tube, lug blanks and stampings;
- rims, spokes, nipples and balls;
- chains, free-wheels and three-speed hubs;
- saddles, tyres, bells, lamps and toolbags;
- enamels and nickel salts.

Smith's Stamping Works moved to Red Lane, Coventry, in 1896 "just so as to be near the Coventry cycle manufactories" ([Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)).

The machine shop's character can be documented. Triumph's shops in 1905 were "filled by automatic machine tools, the majority of which were of American origin" ([Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)). Buyers of Alfred Herbert's No. 4 capstan lathe included Rudge-Whitworth, Triumph, the Coventry Machinists' Co. and Centaur ([Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14097/)). Premier built its own automatic rim drillers, hub drillers and hub-turning lathes ([Wallis-Tayler 1897](https://archive.org/details/moderncyclesapr00wallgoog)).

### Viewers, frame numbers and the packing bench

Viewers gauged parts as they came back into stores. "With lightning like quickness the gauge detects inaccurate workmanship", and "back go the faulty parts to the producer to be rectified". The final viewer, "a practical cyclist as well as a practical mechanic", weighed each machine, entered "its number in a book", tested every bearing, "bangs the machine up and down and about, tries the brakes, pinches the tyres", and passed it to the women and girls who greased and wrapped it ([Grew](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)). Frame numbers ran in one continuous sequence. Rudge stamped under the bottom bracket until 1927 ([Retrobike](https://www.retrobike.co.uk/threads/rudge-whitworth-frame-numbers.326293/latest)), and Raleigh under the seat lug ([Menotomy](https://menotomymaps.com/bikepix/ra_sn.php)). No source says at which step the number was struck. Bare steel that stays legible under enamel points to stamping after filing and before enamelling.

Machines going by passenger train went wrapped only. Goods-train machines went in narrow crates, "either singly or with two or three others", and overseas machines were dismantled into metal-lined cases ([Grew](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewuoft)). Stock was kept lean. "Makers and dealers do not care to produce or carry heavy stocks", but all leading makers kept depots "in half-a-dozen of the principal towns", stocked from December to March ([Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)).

### About twenty trades staffed the floor

Carter's 1912 list names the jobs on the floor. In the turnery and press shops:
- turners and machine minders, who were "youths or women" in some works, "though most of the leading firms employ only male 'turners'";
- skilled "hardeners".

In the frame shop:
- frame builders with youth learners;
- the trained "brazier".

In the finishing shops:
- "filers, or sand blasters", polishers, platers, and enamellers ("men, assisted by youths");
- "liners", who were "highly skilled … very well paid and much in demand".

In the wheel shop:
- spoke-machine minders;
- youths lacing;
- "expert wheel builders" who trued.

On assembly, testing and packing:
- finishers or assemblers with learners;
- "cycle testers";
- wrappers and packers.

Around the works:
- toolmakers and toolsetters, pattern makers and smiths;
- storemen and warehousemen;
- carpenters "to make crates";
- mechanics and enginemen;
- "'Charge-men'" over sets of machines, foremen over each shop, and youth messengers.

Liners, wheel truers and testers needed "a considerable period as learners". Wrapping, lacing dress guards, scrubbing parts for plating and rough polishing were "'picked up' by youths and girls in a few days". Coventry was unusually male: in 1907 "only 602 females were employed in the cycle and motor manufacture" there, and firms were "confining their employees more and more to skilled male workers, with youths as learners" ([Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)). The 1911 census, which counts by occupation rather than by industry, gives a higher **1,476 women in Coventry cyclemaking** ([Carr 1978](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)).

### The season, not the week, set output

Carter gives the cycle trade's year:

| Period | What happened |
|---|---|
| October | "Cycle makers decide upon the models for the coming season"; pattern-makers and toolmakers go to work |
| Late November | The new models are shown at the "Show" |
| End of December | "Most branches of the factories are set in motion" |
| December to March | The works stock their depots and fill large stock orders |
| February to the end of May | "The busiest period at the works", "when much overtime is worked" |
| June and July | Work follows the volume of orders |
| End of August to early November | "The slackest period", with "short time" |

Turners, pressers and automatic-machine hands could work for stock all year, because turned parts changed little between models. The hand trades felt the swings "earliest and most of all": plating, enamelling, wheel building, finishing, wrapping, packing, filing, truing and polishing ([Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)). Raleigh in 1901 employed 550 in the slack season and 850 in the busy one ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).

## Three grades from £5 10s to 14 guineas reached riders through agents

### The catalogue spanned single-speed "Specials" to gold-lined three-speed tourers

Coventry prices for the 1911 season were set at the November 1910 Olympia show:
- **Premier:** a 14-gn "Royal" with patent oil-bath and three-speed; a 12-gn "Helical"; a 10-gn "Tourist"; a "Standard Green" at £8 15s with a metal gear case and three-speed; a "Popular" at about £7 15s (£8 drop frame); and a "Special" and "Speedy" at £5 10s (£5 15s drop frame).
- **Swift:** "from the 'Imperial Swift,' at £6 17s. 6d., to the 'Swift No. 1,' at thirteen guineas", the latter with an oil-bath case, three-speed and "22 carat gold leaf" lining.
- **Rover:** 10-gn "Royals" and "Three-Speed Specials", £14 "Imperials", and a 10-gn all-black tradesman's carrier.
- **Humber:** the "Beeston-Humber" with three-speed at fifteen guineas.
- **Calcott Bros:** a range of "five to eleven guineas", with "most of the roadster pattern cycles … provided with three-speed gears" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).

Singer in 1908 sold bicycles at 6, 7, 8, 10 and 14 gns, juveniles at "5 guineas for boys and £5 10s. for girls", and tricycles at 18 and 20 gns. "Only one guinea extra is charged for either a two or three-speed gear" ([Online Bicycle Museum: Singer](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/34539-2/)). Raleigh's pre-war three-speed extra was 21s ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). "In 1914 excellent English bicycles could be bought for about £8 8s." Saddles, pedals and toolbags were "always included in the price … yet bells and lamps are seldom, if ever, thrown in" ([Grew 1921](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)). At the top end in 1913, a Beeston Humber sold at £15 12s 6d and a Raleigh Superbe at 15 gns ([Online Bicycle Museum](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/1913-all-black-3-speed-royal-sunbeam-for-gentlemen/)).

The market was shifting from leisure to utility riding. Cyclists' Touring Club membership fell from 56,147 in 1900 to 15,474 in 1913, and the average British machine price fell by over £2 between 1905 and 1914 ([Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)). That shift is why carriers, all-weather models and easy payments crowd the 1910 show report.

Remember that a guinea (gn) is 21s, so 10 gns is £10 10s and 14 gns is £14 14s. A plausible catalogue for the fictional firm, modelled on Premier, Swift and Calcott (my synthesis of the cited prices), is:

Surviving 1913 Coventry machines have 24–26 in frames and 28 in wheels: a Triumph Gloria with a Sturmey-Archer "FX" (frame no. 59088) ([Online Bicycle Museum](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/1913-triumph-gloria-three-speed-roadster/)), and a Beeston Humber sold through Humber's Paris depot at 23 Avenue des Champs-Élysées ([Online Bicycle Museum](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/1913-beeston-humber-paris-depot/)). Rudge offered "five different sizes … from four feet four inches to six feet and over" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)). Black with gold lining was the norm.

### Agents bought at trade price after the November show

The sales chain ran maker → sometimes a factor → local agent (cycle dealer, ironmonger or garage). Alongside it ran contracts with stores, co-operatives, mail-order houses, government buyers and export houses. Some factories built "bicycles for the trade … sold under the trader's own name" ([Grew](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)).

No 1913 trade-terms sheet survives. Raleigh's 1901 catalogue priced every model at a list price and a "Net cash price" of exactly two-thirds of list. In 1897 a dealer price was quoted "less 2.5 per cent discount for cash". In 1898–99 "discounts of 10 per cent had been given for guaranteed orders of 12 machines" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). Elbourne's specimen engineering tender reads "Monthly, less 2½% cash discount" ([Elbourne 1914](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). The defensible default terms are therefore **list less 25–33⅓% to agents, 2½% for settlement on monthly account, and a 5–10% bonus on a guaranteed season order**. Sole agencies for a town were real: even Timerick could "arrange for a few sole agencies" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).

Travellers carried the catalogue to agents. Harold Bowden served as "Raleigh's traveller for Eastern England" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). Elbourne's 5 × 8 in Sales Promotion Index Card logged each traveller's calls, the literature sent, enquiries and orders, on cards that could be coloured by traveller's district ([Elbourne 1914](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Easy payments existed but are documented mainly for Raleigh. Its "gradual payment system, administered via the company's sales depots" began around 1906 ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)).

The trade body was the Cycle and Motor Cycle Manufacturers' and Traders' Union, based in Coventry. It ran the annual show and framed rules for dealing with agents. Its 1912–13 president was Arthur Brampton ([Grace's Guide: CMTU](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/British_Cycle_and_Motor_Cycle_Manufacturers_and_Traders_Union)). Formal retail price maintenance came only with the Cycle Trade Union of 1914 ([MRC](https://mrc-catalogue.warwick.ac.uk/records/MCA/X/1)). The 34th and final Stanley Show was held in November 1910 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Cycle_Show)). That same month the CMTU staged "the first international show of cycles, motor-cycles, and their accessories" at Olympia ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition)). Swift held its own exhibition in show week "at their Holborn Viaduct depot" ([1910 show report](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1910_Cycle_and_Motorcycle_Exhibition:_Cycles)).

### Exports went through confirming houses at about £4 a machine

Britain led world cycle exports from 1909 to 1913 ([Petty, ICHC](https://www.ichc.biz/proceedings/ICHC-011-24.pdf)). In **1913 it exported 147,633 complete cycles worth £609,482**, about £4 2s 7d each ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). That average sits well below home retail prices, which suggests cheaper grades valued at trade price.

Raleigh used "confirming houses to cover sales in the rest of the world". Levetus & Co. handled the Punjab, Burma, Nepal and India, and Agar Cross covered Argentina and the Far East. In 1898, "95 per cent of their foreign business, with the exception of Paris, was conducted on a cash basis" ([Lloyd-Jones & Lewis](https://dokumen.pub/raleigh-and-the-british-bicycle-industry-an-economic-and-business-history-1870-1960.html)). "Russia, Spain, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, etc., were all large buyers … up to August, 1914". France's tariff of "about 1s. per pound weight" squeezed out the British Paris depots ([Grew](https://archive.org/details/cycleindustryits00grewrich)). Premier opened a branch factory at Kobe in 1910 ([Petty, ICHC](https://www.ichc.biz/proceedings/ICHC-011-24.pdf)).

## Six carbon copies and a messenger boy ran the office

### One general office, partitioned by function

The principal source here is Elbourne's *Factory Administration and Accounts*, published in March 1914, with 145 specimen forms. Elbourne had been Works Accountant at Vickers and a departmental works manager at BSA, a cycle maker ([Elbourne 1914](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Elbourne himself warns that his forms "represent an achieved organisation". A typical Coventry firm of 1913 probably ran something closer to Spencer's simpler 1907 motor-works routine ([Spencer 1907](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)). How "Elbourne" the firm is can be a tuning parameter for the simulation.

Elbourne's staff diagram runs: Board → General Manager → Sales Manager, Works Manager, Chief Designer and Financial Manager. Below them come travellers, draughtsmen, foremen, a plant engineer, a works accountant and estimator, buyer, cashier, storekeeper, inspector, tool designer, ratefixer, wages clerk, gatekeeper and warehouseman ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Burton advised that works "employing up to 500 or 600 hands" should write all letters in one general office ([Burton 1899](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)). Dicksee's office layout:
- the cash staff "partitioned off" with the cashier's own safe;
- separate zones for orders and supplies, sales and invoicing, wages, and prime cost and estimating;
- the drawing office housed apart even if it is one man, and separate quarters for travellers;
- "the ordinary sloping desk with a single line of drawers" ([Dicksee 1906](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)).

A roster for 400 hands (my construction from those texts) has two halves.

**Commercial side:**
- a Secretary-Accountant;
- a Cashier who doubles as petty cashier;
- a Chief Clerk;
- a correspondence clerk with one or two typists;
- an order and invoice clerk;
- one sales ledger clerk and one bought ledger clerk;
- a Buyer with a clerk.

**Works side:**
- a Works Accountant-Estimator with two cost clerks;
- two wages clerks and a timekeeper;
- a Storekeeper, a receiving clerk and stores servers;
- a production clerk and a progress chaser;
- a ratefixer;
- two or three draughtsmen;
- a despatch clerk;
- a gatekeeper;
- two or three messenger boys.

The office mixed old and new equipment:
- **Typewriters.** "Upwards of twenty makes" were on the English market, "nearly all … of American origin", the Salter being one English exception.
- **Copying.** Carbon paper; a Roneo rotary duplicator at "sixty copies per minute".
- **Calculating machines.** The Arithmometer, the Brunsviga and the "perhaps too costly" Burroughs adder.
- **Bell pushes.** Coded rings: "one ring being for the shorthand clerk, two for the principal assistant, three for the office boy".
- **Speaking tubes.** "Most unsanitary" ([Dicksee 1906](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)).
- **Telephone exchange.** A private telephone exchange was "frequently … looked after by the Door Attendant" ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)).
- **Cards and paper.** Card indexes came in 3 × 5, 4 × 6 and 5 × 8 in sizes, and Elbourne tabulates foolscap, post, demy and other writing-paper sizes ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)).

Staff signed an attendance book, with late arrivals and overtime entered in red ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). No Coventry office hours survive. The GWR's Swindon office staff worked from nine to five-thirty ([Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)). Opening at about 9 a.m., three hours after the works, is the defensible default.

### Paper flows drove every transaction

**Sales.** A customer's order is first scrutinised "as to the credit status of the firm, and secondly, as to any technical or commercial conditions". Agency reports are filed with the cashier. It is then acknowledged the same day, by a postcard "printed in copyable ink" that may carry advertising ([Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich); [Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Elbourne's **Office Order** was typed so that "six copies may be obtained at one typing". The copies went to:
1. the Invoice Clerk;
2. the Estimator and Works Accountant;
3. the Works Manager;
4. the Drawing Office;
5. the Works Office;
6. the Warehouse.

Its corner was "cut off when the order is completely invoiced". Orders were numbered by series: A for sales, B for repairs and sundries, C for stock manufacture, and D for experiments, with a G suffix for guarantee work ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Spencer's simpler version was a quadruplicate Orders Received Book in which only the office copy showed the price, under the rule that "no work is to go forward in the factory except by the authority of a record in the 'orders received' book" ([Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)).

Despatch produced a **Packing Slip** in triplicate and an **Advice of Despatch** whose General Office copy triggered the invoice. "The quickest way is to make the invoice out in blank as a carbon copy of the advice". Invoices then went into the Sales Day Book and Sales Ledger by invoice number ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Every customer received a monthly statement, "a silent demand for payment", and credit notes were printed in red ([Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)).

**Production.** Stock production needed General Manager sanction on an "Application for Stock Manufacturing Sanction". The Works Office planned each order on a **Production Programme** sheet with weekly columns and "deliveries entered in red". A shop-floor **Work Depot** split orders into sub-orders, under the rule "that the batch shall not be broken at any operation". It issued each batch on a **Work Tally** that "accompanies the batch through all operations", with a **Stage Ticket** for each move that went "through the works post to the Wages Office". Viewers' rejects produced a triplicate **Viewing Report**, and replacements ran under the same sub-order number "prefixed with X". A progress clerk ("Tracer, Chaser, Worrier") stamped urgent tallies "V.U.", and "this sign must be respected". A Progress Committee under the Works Manager settled overtime and agreed the programme ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Spencer's foremen simply ticked their copy of the shop list when they issued work and marked it with a "cross tick" on delivery to the finished store ([Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)).

**Purchasing.** A triplicate **Purchase Requisition** from the Works Office went, once the Works Manager approved it, to the Buyer. The Buyer typed a triplicate **Purchase Order**, "the third copy going to the Receiving Clerk, without prices". It was "signed frequently by the General Manager", and its printed terms read "Usual Terms: Monthly, less 2½%". Petty orders could go under lesser authority up to "say £1", and in some firms orders over £50 needed the Managing Director's signature. The Receiving Clerk made a **Goods Received Note** in carbon duplicate. It was signed by the inspector, noted by the Works Office, and matched with the supplier's invoice in the Works Accounts Office. "Suppliers' invoices shall not be sent beyond the Works Accounts Office, however trustworthy the Stores Staff may be" ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)).

Stock was recorded twice: by quantity on a **Stock Control Card** at the stores, and by value in a Stock Ledger in the accounts office. Spencer hung "a coloured disk" on any bin due for replenishment and a different colour when stock fell below minimum ([Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)). Spencer's firm paid suppliers on the "third Wednesday in the month" for the previous month's deliveries ([Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)).

**Costing.** Prime cost was direct wages plus direct materials plus chargeable expenses. Oncost was "most generally" charged as a percentage on direct wages. Hawkins's worked example uses 50% works oncost on wages and 10% office oncost on works cost ([Hawkins 1905](https://archive.org/details/costaccountsexpl00hawk)). Elbourne preferred hourly shop-charge rates per department or per machine. His fortnightly Delivered Orders Cost Abstract set works cost against invoice price for the General Manager, "mounted in a guard book for privacy" ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)).

**Correspondence.** Spencer's post came in a locked leather bag, with one of the two keys held by the postmaster ([Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)). The Secretary opened it "at an early hour", and "all letters received before noon are answered, or at least acknowledged, on the date of receipt" ([Burton](https://archive.org/details/cu31924004021154)). Remittances went straight into a Cash Inwards Book. Letters were stamped and numbered in one operation, and logged in a register where telegrams were "entered in red" ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Carbon copies were filed with the incoming letter, but the signed original was still press-copied into a letter book, because "in the event of production for legal purposes, it would be the actual press copy of the signed letter which should be put in evidence" ([Dicksee 1906](https://archive.org/details/officeorganisat00dickgoog)). Internal paper moved by a works post: "Messenger boys make regular journeys round the departments leaving one post envelope and collecting the waiting one" ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)).

Three status conventions run through all of this. The top "executive" copy was always white and the carbons were "distinctive colours". Red marked exceptions: telegrams, late arrivals, deductions, credit balances and credit notes. A cut-off corner marked completion ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). The exact colour of each carbon is never named, so pink or yellow copies are a design choice.

### The wages week closed midweek and paid out on Friday

Elbourne's time card was headed "Ending Wednesday" and was "issued again on Fridays as a Pay Card". Spencer's wages week ended "at stopping time on Thursday" ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich); [Spencer](https://archive.org/details/commercialorgani00spenrich)). Elbourne's staff did extra-pay calculations "for the first half of the week and … the ordinary wages the latter part". Wages were computed to the halfpenny because of National Insurance. The coin analysis listed "sovereigns, half-sovereigns, half-crowns, threepenny pieces, copper and sundry silver", and copper came from the bank in five-shilling bags. "Pay Clerk No. 1 will select the coin and slide the amount to Pay Clerk No. 2, who will check and put in a pay tin with the pay slip on top". The pay clerks might earn "sixpence for each pay correctly made up". At each pay station one clerk paid out while the other cancelled pay cards "by a crayon mark across the corner", and empty tins went into "a basket … with a canvas funnel top". "It is better generally to pay on Friday night rather than Saturday morning" ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). A surviving works pay tin measures 52 mm across and 50 mm high, with a hinged, numbered lid ([Museum Wales](https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/5947a7e4-baa4-3ff8-b570-195186066be7?page=25)). Dicksee's audit rule was that "a cheque should always be drawn for the exact amount of wages required", and wages paid "in the presence of a works manager" ([Dicksee, Auditing 1912](https://archive.org/details/practica00dickauditingrich)).

## A 53-hour week at 6d to 10¼d an hour, under five statutes

### Hours, timetable and the gate

In August 1910 the Coventry and District Engineering Employers' Association and the five main craft unions signed the "Terms of Working Conditions to be Maintained in Workshops". It set **54 hours for day workers, "work ending at noon on Saturday", and 45 for night workers**. Overtime was capped at "15 hours in any one week", with an "effort" to keep within 32 hours a month, so the permitted maximum was 69 hours. The Coventry Trades Council's 1913 report gives engineering workers "a 53 hour basic week" ([Carr 1978](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)). The ASE's recognised Coventry hours in 1909 were 53 ([Board of Trade 1909](https://archive.org/details/wagesstandardtim00grearich)), and Elbourne's specimen engagement form says "per week of 53 hours" ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). The May 1912 labourers' settlement was "27/- for a 54 hour week" ([Carr](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)). A simulation can therefore run craftsmen on 53 hours and labourers on 54.

Women and under-18s had legal limits. Their weekday period was 6–6, 7–7 or 8–8, and Saturday 6–2 or equivalent. They needed at least 1½ hours for meals on weekdays, one hour of it before 3 p.m., and no spell longer than five hours without a half-hour break ([Bowstead 1901](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)). Both timetables below are reconstructions that satisfy the agreement and the Act; no documented Coventry timetable for 1913 survives.

| Version | Monday to Friday | Saturday | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 54-hour | 6:00–8:15, breakfast to 9:00, 9:00–1:00, dinner to 2:00, 2:00–5:30 (9¾ h) | 6:00–8:15, 9:00–12:00 (5¼ h) | 54 h |
| 53-hour | 6:00–8:00, breakfast to 8:30, 8:30–12:30, dinner to 1:30, 1:30–5:00 (9½ h) | 6:00–8:00, 8:30–12:00 (5½ h) | 53 h |

Holidays were the bank holidays (Easter Monday, Whit Monday, the first Monday in August and Boxing Day) plus Good Friday and Christmas Day ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_Holidays_Act_1871)). At Swindon they were unpaid: after the July "Trip", "the toilers march past the pay-table and receive nothing" ([Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)).

At the gate, the long-established system was a numbered brass check dropped in a box at each start. Elbourne wrote in 1914 that checks had "given way largely" to mechanical time recorders, and he preferred the card type ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Gledhill-Brook began making card recorders in Huddersfield in 1912 ([Grace's Guide](https://gracesguide.co.uk/Gledhill-Brook_Time_Recorders)). The Swindon routine, flagged as a railway works, gives the texture. "After the five minutes' grace", lateness cost a quarter-hour of pay, then half an hour. After that the doors closed "with a loud bang", and the latecomer started after breakfast, "losing a quarter". A lost check cost half an hour's wages ([Williams 1915](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40975)). Fines were lawful under the Truck Act 1896 only if they appeared in a posted notice or signed contract, were "fair and reasonable", were notified in writing each time, and were entered in a register open to the inspector ([Bowstead 1901](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)).

### Hiring, wages and payment by results

Foremen hired and fired. Men asking for the district rate at the Coventry Ordnance Works in 1907 were told that "as soon as their jobs were finished out they had to go". The labour force was young and migrant: Coventry grew from 69,978 people in 1901 to 106,349 in 1911, "the fastest growth for any County Borough in the Kingdom", and over 60% of inhabitants were under 30 ([Carr 1978](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)). Labour Exchanges opened from 1910 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Exchanges_Act_1909)).

Carter's cycle-trade averages are for a 54-hour week. Carter also quotes piece prices of "10½d. per pair of wheels finished, 6d. for polishing a handle-bar, 3d. each for forks, 7d. for gear sets, 6d. for brake sets" ([Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)). In Coventry "the move [to piecework] … was particularly rapid. Often it was gang piece-work", with prices fixed by "mutuality". Setters and toolmakers stayed on time rates ([Carr](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)).

Daimler's tinsmiths struck for seven weeks in 1907 against a premium bonus and lost ([Carr](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)). Under the Rowan premium bonus, the bonus is (time saved ÷ time allowed) × time worked: a 10-hour job done in 7 hours pays 9.1 hours. Under the Halsey system the worker typically gets a third of the time saved: the same job pays 8 hours ([Weekes 1970](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/55860/1/WRAP_thesis_Weekes_1970.pdf)). Women in the Birmingham cycle trade around 1905 averaged 10s 6d. In one works, women displaced 60 of 80 men at a top wage of 18s against the men's 30–40s ([Cadbury et al. 1906](https://archive.org/details/womensworkwagesp00cadbrich)).

### Five statutes governed the paperwork attached to every worker

**Factory and Workshop Act 1901.** The Act required these documents "affixed at the entrance" ([Bowstead 1901](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)):
- the prescribed abstract of the Act;
- the name and address of the district inspector;
- the name and address of the certifying surgeon;
- a notice of the clock that regulated hours.

The firm also posted a notice of its hours and meal times. It kept a general register of young persons, limewashing, accidents and exceptions, open to the surgeon and inspector. No child under 12 could be employed, and children of 12–14 could work only half-time. Anyone under 16 needed a surgeon's certificate of fitness within seven working days. The surgeon's fee was 2s 6d per visit plus 6d per person after the first five ([Bowstead](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)).

**Notice of Accidents Act 1906.** Written notice went "forthwith" to the inspector for any fatal accident, any machinery, molten-metal, hot-liquid, gas or electrical accident that kept a worker off for a whole day, and any accident disabling a worker for more than seven days ([Notice of Accidents Act 1906](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/6/53/enacted)).

**Workmen's Compensation Act 1906.** The employer paid without proof of fault. Incapacity was compensated at up to **half average weekly earnings, capped at £1 a week**, with nothing for the first week if incapacity lasted under two. A worker under 21 earning under 20s received full earnings up to 10s. Death with dependants brought three years' earnings or £150, whichever was larger, capped at £300 ([Willis 1913](https://archive.org/details/workmenscompensa00willrich)).

**National Insurance Act 1911.** Health contributions were **7d a week for men (3d employer, 4d worker) and 6d for women (3d each)**, from age 16. Collection began in July 1912 and benefits from 15 January 1913 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Insurance_Act_1911)). Sickness benefit was 10s a week for men and 7s 6d for women, and maternity benefit 30s. Unemployment insurance cost **2½d each from worker and employer** (1d each under 18) in insured trades, which included "mechanical engineering" and "construction of vehicles". It paid 7s a week for up to 15 weeks ([NI Act 1911](https://sochealth.co.uk/national-health-service/health-law/national-insurance-act-1911/)). A man in an insured trade therefore lost 6½d a week to stamps. No ruling has been found on whether cycle-making counted as an insured trade.

**Truck Acts 1831–1896.** Fines and deductions needed a posted notice or signed contract, written particulars each time, and a register open to the inspector, as described above ([Bowstead](https://archive.org/details/cu31924101087926)).

## Crates left by horse dray for Warwick Road; orders arrived by penny post

### The LNWR goods yard was the works' real despatch bay

The LNWR had "sole control of all railway services to and from Coventry". The Midland ran goods in over the Nuneaton line on running powers ([VCH Communications](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)). Cycle traffic went through the Warwick Road goods yard beside the station. Its features came from different dates:
- **c. 1890:** a weighbridge office at the entrance, "three sidings with trap points", and the Railway Inn opposite ([Warwickshire Railways](https://www.warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrcov3728.htm)).
- **1890s:** a two-storey LNWR range along Warwick Road "with stables beneath".
- **1910 plan:** goods sheds, wagon turntables, cattle docks and signal cabins 1–4 ([Warwickshire Railways](https://www.warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry_plans_&_maps.htm)).
- **1920 photo captions:** a joint LNWR/MR goods shed, a No. 2 goods shed, a 5-ton hand crane, and offices "housing claims and telephone exchange" ([Warwickshire Railways](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/lnwrcov554c.htm)).

An undated photo, probably later, shows "a rake of open wagons being loaded with crates of Triumph cycles inside Coventry No 2 goods shed" ([Warwickshire Railways](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry.htm)). Cartage ran under Chief Cartage Foreman John James King, whose men wore distinguishing hatbands. A staff photograph is dated 31 August 1912 ([Warwickshire Railways](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventry.htm)). The goods-only Coventry Loop Line opened on 16 August 1914, too late for 1913 ([Warwickshire Railways](https://warwickshirerailways.com/lms/coventryloopline.htm)). LNWR wagons were "medium lead grey" ([RMweb](https://www.rmweb.co.uk/forums/topic/114259-lnwr-van-livery/page/2/)), marked with white diamonds until 1908 and "LNWR" lettering after ([HMRS](https://hmrs.org.uk/lnwr-wcjs-loco-coach-and-wagon-insignia.html)).

A railway goods-station manager's 1913 handbook describes the daily rhythm. Outward goods came "tumbling in upon him between 5 and 7 o'clock in the evening, for trains that must depart within the next two or three hours in order to reach their destinations early the next morning". Railway invoices were written "at racehorse speed, so that the documents may go by the last train out to meet the goods". The ideal unloading gang was "a checker, a caller-off, a turner-out, and four truckers". The loading rules:
- sheet loads "rick-wise";
- "Leave the load over rather than use a bad sheet";
- label "fragile goods" ([West 1913](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)).

The works' despatch clerk wrote a consignment note, marked "Paid" or "To pay", and chose between the railway's full-liability rate and a cheaper "owner's risk" rate. That choice rested on the "fair alternative" doctrine of *Peek v North Staffordshire Railway* (1863) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_and_Canal_Traffic_Act_1854)). Bicycles were **Class 5, the top merchandise class**, in the statutory classification of 1891–92 ([Darlington 1893](https://archive.org/details/railwayratescarr00darlrich)), which made them expensive to send by weight.

### Horses still did the carting

In 1900, 330 weekly carriers' cart services still ran from Coventry inns ([VCH](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)). A GWR cart horse was expected "to haul up to 36 cwt at a speed of 6 m.p.h. and cover 20 miles" a day, and a standard two-horse van of 1912 carried 4 tons 10 cwt ([Warwickshire Railways](https://warwickshirerailways.com/gwr/gwrhd705.htm)). Motor vehicles were limited to 20 mph under the 1903 Act ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_Car_Act_1903)). Heavy motor cars of 2–5 tons unladen were held to 5–12 mph, depending on tyres and axle weight ([Phillimore](https://tekniskkulturarv.dk/book/11ab5513-b799-4fa9-a59d-b031fa2b12cf?page=147)). Railway managers knew "a well-known trader's own carman" ([West](https://archive.org/details/railwaygoodssta00westgoog)). Two to four horses with a covered van and a flat dray is therefore a reasonable works stable, though no Coventry cycle works' stable is documented. Plater of Birmingham patented wicker cycle crates in 1899. One surviving example measures 96 × 26 × 50 in and has castors "for pushing along railway platforms" ([Online Bicycle Museum](https://onlinebicyclemuseum.co.uk/1899-john-j-plater-cycle-crate/)).

The Coventry Canal carried mainly coal, in horse- or donkey-drawn boats of up to 71 ft 9 in × 7 ft ([VCH](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp34-39)). Fellows Morton & Clayton kept a Coventry depot ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellows_Morton_%26_Clayton)). The canal suits boiler coal, not finished bicycles.

### Export cases went to a port agent with stencilled marks

An inland exporter railed its cases to a shipping agent at the port. The agent, "for a small commission of so much per package", loaded them, cleared Customs and took out the bills of lading. Meanwhile the exporter posted an Advice Note giving the steamer, measurements, weights and value. The rest of the paperwork ran as follows ([Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)):
- **Bills of lading.** Made out in sets of two or three, each needing "a sixpenny stamp".
- **Freight.** Charged per ton weight or per ton measurement, "generally 40 cubic feet", plus 10% primage.
- **Customs.** The Customs "Specification" was due within six days of the ship's clearance.
- **US consular invoice.** Needed for US invoices over £20. Chile, Brazil and Portugal also required consular invoices.
- **Payment.** Usually a documentary draft "at 60 or 90" days.

Cases were lined with "zinc or oilcloth, or waterproof paper" and "secured with iron bands", and limited to about 10 cwt. They were stencilled with marks "15 to 18 inches each way", with letters "3 to 4 inches in height within triangles, double triangles, diamonds, hearts, or circles", a running number and the port. They carried no consignee address ([Hooper & Graham](https://archive.org/details/importexporttrad00hoopuoft)). A single home-trade crate of 48½ cu ft would pay as 1.2 freight tons. That arithmetic is why export machines went knocked down, several to a case. Elswick-Hopper in 1949 packed six to a case ([icenicam](https://icenicam.org.uk/articlez/archive/arc0545.html)); that is a later analogy.

## A private limited company banking with Lloyds and paying 1s 2d in the pound

### Company law fixed the statutory books and the annual calendar

Under the Companies (Consolidation) Act 1908, a private company was one whose articles restricted share transfers, limited members to fifty (excluding employees), and barred public issues ([CA 1908 s.121](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/121/enacted)). Such a company **did not have to file a balance sheet** with its annual return ([CA 1908 s.26](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/26/enacted)). That privacy made the form attractive. Singer "became a private company" in 1909 ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Singer_and_Co)).

Every company still had to:
- keep a registered office ([s.62](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/62/enacted));
- display its name on its premises, seal, cheques, invoices and letters ([s.63](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/63/enacted));
- keep a register of members with numbered shares ([s.25](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/25/enacted));
- hold a general meeting every calendar year, no more than fifteen months after the last ([s.64](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/64/enacted));
- keep minute books ([s.71](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/71/enacted)) and a register of directors ([s.75](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/75/enacted));
- register any mortgage or debenture charge within 21 days ([s.93](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/93/enacted));
- appoint auditors at each meeting who report on whether the balance sheet gives "a true and correct view" ([s.113](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/section/113/enacted)).

Under the default articles (Table A), the seal was used only "in the presence of at least two directors and of the secretary", and "no dividend shall be paid otherwise than out of profits" ([Table A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/69/schedule/FIRST/enacted)). The secretary's room therefore holds the seal press, the Register of Members (often combined with the Share Ledger), the Register of Transfers, the Register of Mortgages and two minute books ([Dicksee, Bookkeeping for Company Secretaries](https://archive.org/details/bookkeepingforco00dickrich)).

### The books, the bank and the stamps

The ledger was split into Sold, Bought and General ledgers. The General Ledger was split again into a Nominal Ledger and a Private Ledger, "which alone shows the result of the trading" ([Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)). Annual accounts were a Trading Account, a Profit and Loss Account and a Balance Sheet. Machinery was depreciated at **7½% on the reducing balance**, with 10% as "an outside limit". Patterns were written off at 25–33⅓% ([Dicksee, Depreciation 1912](https://archive.org/details/depreciationrese00dickrich)), and loose tools at 25–35% ([Garcke & Fells 1893](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620)). Dicksee's price benchmark: "a really first-class Screw-Cutting Lathe could not be purchased for much under £100" and would last 50 years ([Dicksee 1912](https://archive.org/details/depreciationrese00dickrich)). Cycle companies' financial years usually ended "August or September" ([Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)), so stock-taking and closing fell in the slack season.

Coventry's own banks had been absorbed by two national banks:
- **Lloyds** took over the Coventry & Warwickshire Banking Co. in 1879 ([Archives Hub](https://archiveshub.jisc.ac.uk/search/archives/a6ce81cb-b8a2-30a3-a458-0120ae67c596)). Rudge-Whitworth banked with Lloyds ([1914 Who's Who: R](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_R)).
- **The London City & Midland** absorbed the Coventry Union Banking Co. in 1889 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/midland-bank-plc)).

Barclays reached the Midlands only in 1916 ([Barclays](https://www.home.barclays/who-we-are/our-strategy/backing-the-uk/west-midlands/history/)). Coventry had no clearing house, so cheques on banks elsewhere went "to London to be collected through the Bankers' Clearing House". Country bankers usually paid interest on current accounts, and loans were charged with "half-yearly rests" ([Dictionary of Banking 1911](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofbank00thomrich)). Hopper of Barton-on-Humber, another cycle maker, issued £12,000 of first debentures and £5,000 of second debenture stock in 1907 "to secure bank overdraft of £15,000" ([Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)).

Stamp duties in 1913 ([Stamp Act 1891](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/54-55/39/schedule/FIRST/enacted)):
- a cheque needed **a 1d stamp**; the 2d rate came only in 1918 ([Finance Act 1918 s.36](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/1918/en/act/pub/0015/sec0036.html));
- a receipt for £2 or more needed 1d;
- time bills paid an ad valorem scale rising to 1s per £100.

Elbourne's combined cheque-and-receipt form carried a box for the 1d receipt stamp ([Elbourne](https://archive.org/details/administr00elfactoryborich)). Petty cash ran on the imprest system. Dicksee's exercise opens with "received from cashier for Petty Cash, £10" ([Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)).

### Tax and insurance

Income tax for 1913–14 was **1s 2d in the pound** ([Finance Act 1913 s.2](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/1913/en/act/pub/0030/sec0002.html)). Trading profit was assessed under Schedule D "upon a fair and just average of three years", with an allowance for "wear and tear" of machinery. Tax was deducted from preference dividends and debenture interest ([Garcke & Fells](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620); [Dicksee 1909](https://archive.org/details/6thbookkeepingfo00dickuoft)). Super-tax of 6d in the pound fell on wealthy individuals, not on companies ([Finance (1909–10) Act 1910](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/1910/act/8/enacted/en/print)).

Fire insurance came from "tariff offices" under the Fire Offices' Committee ([AIM25](https://atom.aim25.com/index.php/fire-offices-committee)). Boiler explosions were excluded from fire policies and insured with specialist companies whose engineers also inspected the boilers ([Garcke & Fells](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031273620)). Workmen's compensation insurance was voluntary and priced per £100 of wages. Farm rates in 1906 ran 7s 6d–10s per £100 of wages ([Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1906/mar/26/workmens-compensation-bill-1)), and light engineering would have cost more.

### A calibrated profit and balance sheet

No 1910–14 accounts of a Coventry cycle company were retrieved, so the model below is an estimate built on cited anchors:
- gross output per head of £229 in the UK motor and cycle trades in 1907, and £198 at Hopper in 1912 ([Harrison 1977](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/));
- the 1906 earnings above;
- Rudge-Whitworth's net profits of about £17,600–£21,200 a year at a 10% ordinary dividend in 1898–1900 ([Harrison](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/));
- the contemporary view that "10 per cent was … a big dividend" ([Harrison](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/14097/)).

| Item (year to 31 August 1913) | Model value (estimate) |
|---|---|
| Works hands / salaried staff | 400 / 25–30 |
| Weekly works wages | £570–£620 (about £29,000–£31,000 a year) |
| Salaries (managers, clerks, travellers) | £4,000–£5,000 |
| Turnover | £70,000–£95,000 (10,000–12,000 machines at £5–£6 trade, plus spares; or £198–£229 per head) |
| Cost shares | Materials and bought-in parts 45–50%; wages 32–35%; works overhead 6–8%; selling and administration 6–8% |
| Net profit | 5–10% of sales, about £4,000–£9,000 |
| Capital | £40,000–£50,000 in £1 ordinary shares plus £20,000 in 5–6% cumulative preference shares; possibly £10,000–£15,000 of 5% debentures |
| Fixed assets | Land and buildings £25,000–£35,000; plant £20,000–£30,000 net; tools, jigs and patterns £2,000–£4,000 |
| Working capital | Stock and work in progress £15,000–£25,000 (spring peak); debtors £10,000–£20,000; overdraft £5,000–£15,000 from February to April |
| Appropriation | Preference dividend in full; ordinary 5–10%; £1,000–£3,000 to reserve |
| Income tax | About £470 on an £8,000 three-year average profit at 1s 2d |

The capital and balance-sheet figures follow the Hopper and Swift precedents; Swift issued an extra £20,000 of shares in 1913 ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Swift_Cycle_Co)).

## Conclusion

The sources point to one design principle: **a 1913 cycle works was a set of rhythms tied together by paper, more than a set of machines**. The hundred-machine batch links four of them, because it was at once:
- the stores' issue unit;
- one load of Humber's frame stoves;
- the size of the sub-order a work tally followed through the shops;
- the unit by which pieceworkers were credited as parts returned to stores.

Around that batch run the week (time card ending Wednesday, coin from the bank, pay tins on Friday night), the working day (6 a.m. start, breakfast and dinner spells, the 5–7 p.m. rush to the goods yard) and the year (October designs, the November show, February–May overtime, August stock-taking, autumn short time). A simulation that models these clocks and the documents that cross between them will feel more authentic than one that models machine detail. The machinery itself is documented well enough to stylise: hearths, stoves, vats, belted lathes and pillar benches.

1913 is also a hinge year, and that makes "modernity" a natural tuning dial rather than a fixed fact. Many institutions were brand new: National Insurance stamps (July 1912), the Post Office telephone monopoly (January 1912), corporation trams (January 1912) and Gledhill-Brook card recorders (1912). Brass checks, press-copy letter books, gas engines and horse drays still worked beside them, and the Loop Line and the 2d cheque stamp were still to come. The firm's strategic position was also precarious. Coventry's share of the national cycle industry fell from 49% in 1891 to 26% in 1911 as the motor trade took hold ([Carr 1978](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/34740/1/WRAP_THESIS_Carr_1978.pdf)). British machine prices were falling. A realistic fictional firm has thin margins, a spring overdraft and a board watching neighbours move into motorcycles: Rudge-Whitworth's 1914 directory entry lists cycles, motorcycles and wheels ([1914 Who's Who: R](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1914_Who%27s_Who_in_Business:_Company_R)), and Carter reported in 1912 that Triumph's motorcycle shops "have been working day and night" ([Carter 1912](https://archive.org/details/seasonaltrades00webbuoft)). The open gaps are building colours, hooters, exact timetables, 1913 trade discounts and stove load sizes. In those areas the designer is free to choose, provided the choice stays within the documented frame set out above.
